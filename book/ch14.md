# CHAPTER 14|Multicultural, Language, Disability and Fairness Considerations in Assessment

## Introduction

Every assessment is cultural. The constructs it measures, the instruments it uses, the norms against which it compares, the language in which it is conducted and the assumptions it makes about what constitutes healthy functioning are all products of particular cultural and historical settings. When assessor and client share those settings, the assumptions are invisible; when they do not, they become the substance of the assessment.

This chapter treats cultural and linguistic competence not as a supplementary courtesy but as a condition of valid assessment. It addresses the specific technical problems — translation, equivalence, invariance, language proficiency — and the broader interpretive problems that arise when a client's presentation is understood through a framework that was not designed for them. It also addresses disability, which raises structurally similar questions about whether an instrument measures the construct or the barrier, and it considers how multiple social positions interact.

## 14.1|Culture and the Meaning of Symptoms

Culture shapes what distress feels like, how it is expressed, what it is attributed to, who is consulted and what help is acceptable. An assessment that ignores these influences will misinterpret both the presence and the severity of difficulty.

### Idioms of distress

Different cultural settings provide different vocabularies for distress. Somatic complaints, spiritual attributions, complaints about family relationships, and descriptions of "thinking too much" are all recognised idioms through which distress is expressed. The DSM-5-TR cultural concepts of distress — cultural syndromes, cultural idioms of distress and cultural explanations — provide a vocabulary for describing these patterns without treating them as exotic. The assessor's task is to establish what the client's experience means within their own framework, and then to determine whether it corresponds to a recognised clinical construct.

### Expression and threshold

Cultures differ in the expression of emotion, the acceptability of disclosing distress, and the threshold at which a difficulty becomes a problem. A client from a setting in which emotional restraint is valued may under-report symptoms; a client from a setting in which expressive disclosure is normative may appear more distressed than their functional status warrants. Neither pattern is a distortion; both are culturally patterned response styles that the assessor must interpret.

### Attribution and help-seeking

Beliefs about causation — spiritual, interpersonal, biomedical, moral — shape what the client expects from assessment and treatment. A client who believes their difficulty is a spiritual affliction may not accept a psychological formulation, and a client who believes it is a moral failing may feel judged by any diagnosis. The assessor should elicit the client's explanation and work with it, rather than treating it as a barrier to be overcome.

## 14.2|Language Proficiency and Interpreter Use

Language is the medium of most psychological assessment, and limited proficiency in the language of assessment is a direct threat to validity.

### Assessing proficiency

The assessor should establish the client's proficiency in the language of assessment and in their first language, using self-report, observation and, where possible, a structured measure. Self-report is unreliable: clients frequently over-estimate their proficiency, particularly in high-stakes settings where admitting limited proficiency feels risky. The assessor should observe comprehension directly — for example, by asking the client to explain a task in their own words — and should consider whether the client's presentation differs across languages.

### Working with interpreters

Where an interpreter is required, the assessment should use a trained interpreter rather than a family member or a bilingual colleague. The interpreter should be briefed on the purpose of the assessment, the instruments to be used, the style of interpretation required (first-person, verbatim, without editing), and the confidentiality obligations. The assessor should speak to the client rather than to the interpreter, should allow extra time, and should record that the assessment was interpreter-mediated.

### The effects of interpretation

Interpretation changes the assessment in ways that should be documented. Nuance is lost, idioms may be translated into clinical vocabulary or left opaque, and the interpreter's presence alters disclosure, particularly for sensitive topics and where the interpreter is a member of the client's community. The assessor should record the language of administration, the interpreter's role, and the resulting limits on interpretation. Where standardised instruments are administered through an interpreter, the norms no longer apply, and the scores should be reported descriptively rather than normatively.

## 14.3|Test Translation and Adaptation

Translated instruments are not equivalent to their originals by virtue of translation. Equivalence is an empirical claim requiring evidence.

### Standards for adaptation

The International Test Commission's guidelines for translating and adapting tests specify the required process: documented forward and backward translation by independent translators, reconciliation by a committee, pilot testing with the target population, cognitive interviews to check item comprehension, and empirical evidence of equivalence. Adaptation also requires attention to item content that may be culturally inappropriate — references to unfamiliar foods, activities, holidays or social situations — and to response formats that may be unfamiliar.

### Evidence of equivalence

Evidence of equivalence includes confirmatory factor analysis demonstrating the same structure in each group, tests of measurement invariance at configural, metric and scalar levels, and analyses of differential item functioning. Where scalar invariance is not demonstrated, mean comparisons between groups are not interpretable, and the assessor should not compare a client's score with norms derived from a different linguistic or cultural group.

### Practical guidance

Where a translated instrument has documented equivalence, it can be used with the same interpretive rules as the original. Where it has documented translation but no equivalence evidence, the scores should be interpreted with explicit caution and preferably descriptively. Where the instrument has not been adapted at all, and administration proceeds through an interpreter, the score is not a score in any norm-referenced sense, and the report should say so.

## 14.4|Bias and Fairness

Bias in assessment refers to systematic error in the estimation of a construct for members of a particular group. It is distinct from group differences in the construct itself, and distinguishing the two is the central technical problem of fair assessment.

### Construct bias

Construct bias occurs where the construct itself is defined differently across groups, or where the instrument samples the construct differently. A measure of "social competence" developed in one cultural setting may sample behaviours that are valued there and irrelevant or inappropriate elsewhere. The practical implication is that construct definition must be examined before instruments are chosen, and that the assessor should ask whether the construct, as operationalised, means the same thing for this client.

### Method bias

Method bias arises from the procedure rather than the construct: differential familiarity with testing, differential response styles, differential item familiarity, and differential examiner effects. Examples include the use of timed tests with clients from educational settings where speed is not emphasised, and the use of Likert scales with clients unfamiliar with the format. Method bias can be reduced by adaptation, by practice items, by alternative formats, and by interpreting scores in the context of the client's testing history.

### Fairness in use

Fairness in use concerns the consequences of score interpretation: whether a decision rule produces systematically different outcomes for groups of equal standing on the construct. The assessor should know whether the instrument's cut scores have been evaluated for differential impact, and should be cautious about applying cut scores derived from one population to another. The professional standards treat fairness as integral to validity, not as a separate consideration.

## 14.5|Normative Samples and Representativeness

Norms are comparisons, and a comparison is only meaningful if the reference group is relevant.

### The relevance question

The assessor should ask whether the normative sample resembles the client on the characteristics that affect the construct: age, education, language, culture, disability status, and the clinical or non-clinical nature of the sample. A clinical sample norm is not appropriate for a community client, and a norm collected in one country may not represent a client in another even when both speak the same language.

### Local norms

Where local norms exist, they may be more appropriate than the publisher's norms, provided they are of adequate quality. Local norms derived from a small or unrepresentative sample can be worse than no local norms, because they convey a false precision. The assessor should evaluate the sample size, the recruitment method and the stratification before adopting local norms.

### Interpretation without appropriate norms

Where no adequate norms exist, the assessor has three options: interpret the score descriptively rather than normatively; use within-person comparisons (for example, comparing the client's performance across domains rather than across people); and rely on functional and behavioural evidence. The report should state which approach was used and why.

## 14.6|Disability and Reasonable Accommodations

Disability raises a structural question analogous to culture: does the instrument measure the construct, or does it measure the barrier?

### The social and medical models

The medical model locates disability in the individual's impairment; the social model locates it in the interaction between impairment and an environment that fails to accommodate it. Contemporary frameworks, including the World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health, integrate both: disability is the outcome of the interaction between a health condition, personal factors and environmental barriers. For assessment, this means that a low score may reflect the construct, the barrier, or both, and that the assessor must determine which.

### Accommodations and their evidence

Accommodations remove barriers without changing the construct. The evidence that a given accommodation preserves score meaning varies by instrument and by accommodation; the assessor should know this evidence rather than assuming that all accommodations are equivalent. Where an accommodation has been applied without validation, the score should be interpreted descriptively.

### Disability and diagnostic overshadowing

A recurring problem is diagnostic overshadowing: attributing all difficulties to a known disability and failing to assess for additional conditions. A person with a learning disability may have depression that is untreated because their low mood is attributed to the disability; a person with a sensory impairment may have anxiety that is missed because their presentation is attributed to the impairment. The assessor should assess for comorbidity actively and should use instruments validated for the population where available.

## 14.7|Migration, Acculturation and Context

Migration produces a set of contextual factors that shape both the presentation and its interpretation.

### The migration history

The assessor should establish the reason for migration, the circumstances of the journey, the time since arrival, the client's legal status, the presence or absence of family, the loss of status and social role, and the experience of discrimination. Each of these is a potential contributor to distress and a potential resource for recovery.

### Acculturation

Acculturation describes the process of change in values, practices and identifications that follows contact between cultural groups. It is not a single dimension of "integration" but a set of strategies that vary across domains: a person may adopt the language of the host society while retaining the family's religious practice and gender norms. The assessor should not assume that a longer period of residence implies greater acculturation, nor that acculturation is desirable in itself.

### Intergenerational and family dimensions

Migration frequently produces differences in acculturation between generations, which can be a source of family conflict and of individual distress. A young person may be more acculturated than their parents and experience the difference as a conflict of loyalty. The assessor should consider these dynamics, particularly in family and child assessments.

## 14.8|Socioeconomic Conditions and Opportunity

Socioeconomic conditions shape both the presence of difficulty and the resources available to address it. They are also frequently confounded with the constructs that assessment measures.

### Poverty and assessment

Poverty affects cognitive development, school attainment, mental health and the practical capacity to attend appointments and adhere to treatment. A child's low achievement score may reflect the quality of their schooling, the availability of books, food insecurity or chronic stress rather than a learning disorder. The assessor should gather information about material circumstances and should consider them as explanatory factors rather than as background.

### Access to services

Access to assessment and treatment is unevenly distributed. Clients from disadvantaged backgrounds are more likely to be assessed in crisis, less likely to receive specialist assessment, and more likely to receive a diagnosis that carries stigma. The assessor should be aware of these patterns, not as a matter of individual guilt but as a reason to be particularly careful about the evidence base for conclusions about clients who have had limited access.

### The limits of individual formulation

Some difficulties are primarily structural: unemployment, discrimination, housing insecurity and violence are not individual pathologies. Formulating them as individual difficulties is both inaccurate and unjust. The assessor should distinguish between what the person can change and what requires collective or political response, and should say so in the formulation.

## 14.9|Community Values and Cultural Context

The assessor's own cultural framework is part of the instrument, and recognising it is a prerequisite for fair assessment.

### The assessor's position

Every assessor brings assumptions about family structure, autonomy, emotional expression, gender roles and the appropriate relationship between individual and community. These assumptions shape what is noticed, what is judged problematic and what is recommended. The assessor should identify their own position, seek supervision or consultation where it is likely to distort the assessment, and be explicit with the client about the limits of their cultural knowledge.

### Consulting cultural knowledge

Where the assessor lacks knowledge of the client's cultural context, several resources are available: consultation with a clinician from that background, the client's own account, family and community informants (with consent), and the published literature on cultural concepts of distress. None of these is definitive, and the assessor should treat cultural knowledge as a set of hypotheses to be tested with the client rather than as a set of facts about them.

### Avoiding both culturalism and colour-blindness

Two errors are common. Culturalism attributes all difficulty to culture and thereby fails to assess for disorder. Colour-blindness ignores cultural difference and applies a universal framework that may not fit. The appropriate position treats culture as one explanatory framework among several, to be tested against the evidence in the same way as any other.

## 14.10|Gender and Sexuality in Assessment

Gender and sexuality shape both the prevalence and the presentation of psychological difficulties, and they shape the assessment process itself.

### Gendered patterns and their interpretation

Many conditions show marked differences in prevalence between men and women, and these differences are partly artefactual: they reflect differences in help-seeking, in symptom reporting, in diagnostic practice and in the application of criteria. The assessor should be cautious about using prevalence differences as evidence in an individual case, and should consider whether the presentation has been shaped by gendered expectations — for example, the under-recognition of depression in men and of autism in women.

### Sexuality and gender identity

Lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans and gender-diverse clients experience elevated rates of mental distress, largely through minority stress: discrimination, stigma, internalised negativity and the anticipation of rejection. The assessor should not pathologise identity, should distinguish distress related to identity from distress related to minority stress, and should be aware of the history of psychiatric classification of homosexuality and of gender diversity as disorders. Assessment should be conducted in language that respects the client's own terms.

### Assessment content

Where relevant, the assessment should include relationship history, experiences of discrimination and violence, and the client's own understanding of the relationship between their identity and their difficulties. These topics should be raised without assumption, and the client's decision not to discuss them should be respected.

## 14.11|Intersection of Multiple Social Positions

Clients occupy multiple social positions simultaneously, and the effects of those positions are not additive. A Black woman with a disability, a gay man from a rural area, a migrant woman with limited language proficiency — each faces a configuration of advantage and disadvantage that cannot be understood by considering each characteristic separately.

### Intersectionality in assessment

Intersectionality, as developed in Black feminist scholarship and applied in psychology, directs attention to the ways in which systems of disadvantage interact. For assessment, the practical implications are: to gather information about the specific configuration of the client's circumstances; to avoid assuming that one characteristic explains the presentation; to consider how the client's own account reflects their experience of multiple positions; and to be alert to the possibility that the assessor's own assumptions are shaped by stereotypes about particular combinations.

### The limits of categories

Categories such as "ethnic minority" or "disabled" are administratively useful and analytically crude. Their use in assessment should be accompanied by attention to the specific circumstances of the individual, and the assessor should avoid treating a category as an explanation. The category is a starting point for enquiry, not a finding.

## 14.12|Culturally Responsible Formulation

The output of culturally responsible assessment is a formulation that integrates cultural, linguistic and structural factors with clinical reasoning, and that is explicit about its own limits.

### Structure

A culturally informed formulation addresses: the client's own explanation of the difficulty; the cultural and religious frameworks that shape it; the migration, acculturation and family context; the structural factors — discrimination, poverty, legal status — that contribute; the ways in which the assessor's framework may differ from the client's; and the points at which the formulation is uncertain.

### The cultural formulation interview

The DSM-5-TR cultural formulation interview provides a structured method for eliciting the client's explanatory model, the cultural identity of the client, the stressors and supports, the role of cultural factors in the current difficulty, and the relationship between the client and the clinician. Its use is appropriate whenever the assessor's cultural knowledge is limited, and it has the additional benefit of signalling to the client that their framework is taken seriously.

### Feedback across cultural difference

Feedback should be given in language the client can use, with attention to the client's explanatory model, to the role of family in decision-making, and to the practical constraints the client faces. Where the assessment's conclusions conflict with the client's or family's understanding, the assessor should present the findings as a perspective rather than as a verdict, invite discussion, and record the disagreement.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 45-year-old woman, who migrated eight years ago from West Africa and works as a care assistant, is referred by her general practitioner with "medically unexplained symptoms". She reports episodes of palpitations, heat in the chest, and a sensation of something moving upwards to her throat, which she attributes to a spiritual attack. She has been seen by a cardiologist and a gastroenterologist, with normal results.

**The assessment.** She speaks English as a second language and, although fluent in conversation, struggles with written questionnaires; she completed several with help from her daughter, which she did not initially disclose. The episodes occur in the evenings, often after arguments with her husband, and she describes feeling trapped and unsupported. She reports low mood, poor sleep and loss of interest. She does not drink alcohol and takes no medication. She describes her church community as her main support. On direct enquiry she discloses that her husband has threatened to send her back to her country of origin if she leaves him, and that her immigration status depends on the marriage.

**The reasoning.** The presentation includes a culturally recognised idiom of distress that overlaps with panic attacks, and the questionnaire scores cannot be interpreted normatively because of the language and assistance issues. Medical causes have been excluded by specialists. The pattern is temporally related to marital conflict, the client is in a coercive relationship with an immigration-related power imbalance, and she has limited social support outside her church. Depression is present and clinically significant.

**The decision.** The report describes the presentation as panic attacks occurring in the context of relational distress and coercive control, and documents the questionnaire limitations explicitly. It records the safeguarding concerns relating to coercion and immigration status, refers to a specialist service with the client's consent, and recommends treatment for depression delivered in her first language where possible. It recommends that the church community be engaged as a support, with the client's agreement, and that no joint couple work be undertaken.

**The teaching point.** The "unexplained symptoms" were explained by a combination of a culturally shaped presentation, a relational context of coercion, and a methodological problem with the questionnaires. None of the three would have been identified without asking.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Culture shapes the expression, interpretation and reporting of distress, and assessment that ignores it misinterprets both presence and severity. Language proficiency must be assessed rather than assumed, and interpreter-mediated assessment requires briefing, documentation and interpretive caution. Translated instruments require empirical evidence of equivalence before their scores can be interpreted normatively. Bias operates at the level of construct, method and use, and fairness is integral to validity. Norms are comparisons whose relevance must be established, and where no adequate norms exist the assessor should interpret descriptively and functionally. Disability requires attention to whether the instrument measures the construct or the barrier, and accommodations require evidence that they preserve score meaning. Migration, acculturation, socioeconomic conditions and intersecting social positions shape both the presentation and its interpretation. Culturally responsible formulation integrates these factors and is explicit about its limits.
