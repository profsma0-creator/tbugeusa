# CHAPTER 10|Assessment of Personality Disorders and Enduring Relational Patterns

## Introduction

Personality disorder diagnosis occupies a difficult position in mental health practice. It identifies a group of people with substantial and enduring difficulties who are often poorly served by services; it also carries a history of pejorative use that has caused measurable harm. The diagnosis can open access to specialist treatment or close it; it can support a formulation or replace one; it can be communicated respectfully or used as a description of a person's worth.

This chapter approaches the topic through two commitments. The first is that personality disorder is a disorder of functioning, not a defect of character: what is assessed is the severity of impairment in self and interpersonal functioning, together with the traits that characterise it. The second is that the assessment must be longitudinal, developmental and contextual. A personality disorder cannot be diagnosed from a single interview in an acute crisis, and much of the harm associated with the diagnosis has come from doing exactly that.

## 10.1|Understanding Enduring Personality Patterns

Personality refers to the relatively stable organisation of the psychological characteristics that make a person recognisable across time and situation. A personality pattern becomes a disorder when it is inflexible, pervasive, stable over time, and associated with clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational or other important areas of functioning.

### The criteria for disorder

Four criteria do most of the work. The pattern must be enduring and pervasive rather than situation-specific; it must deviate markedly from cultural expectations in more than one domain; it must be stable and of long duration, with onset traceable to adolescence or early adulthood; and it must cause distress or impairment. The criterion of cultural deviation requires care: what is a disorder in one cultural setting may be conformity in another, and the assessor must know which expectations apply.

### Stability and its limits

Longitudinal research has shown that personality disorder criteria are less stable than the diagnosis implies: many people who meet criteria in early adulthood no longer do so a decade later, and the remission is often substantial. Traits are more stable than symptoms, and impairment is more stable than either, which is one reason contemporary models emphasise functioning rather than criterion counts. The practical implication is that a diagnosis made in early adulthood should not be treated as a life sentence, and that reassessment over time is appropriate.

### Enduring relational patterns

Much of what is clinically important about personality disorder is expressed in relationships: the expectation of abandonment, the alternation between idealisation and devaluation, the avoidance of intimacy, the pursuit of approval, the need for control. These patterns are more informative to assess than individual criteria, because they describe mechanism, and they translate directly into formulation and treatment.

## 10.2|Trait-Based and Categorical Approaches

Two broad approaches to classification coexist: categorical, in which disorders are defined by polythetic criteria; and dimensional, in which personality is described along continuous dimensions of functioning and traits.

### The categorical approach

The categorical approach, exemplified by the DSM-5-TR criteria for the specific personality disorders, has the advantages of familiarity, clinical utility in communication and administrative recognition. It has well-documented disadvantages: extensive comorbidity and overlap among categories, arbitrary thresholds, heterogeneity within categories (two people with the same diagnosis may share no criteria), and poor coverage of presentations that do not fit a named category.

### The dimensional approach

The dimensional approach describes severity of impairment in personality functioning and the particular maladaptive traits that characterise the person. Both DSM-5-TR, through its alternative model, and ICD-11, through its severity-based classification, adopt this structure. Its advantages are that it captures severity, reduces comorbidity artefacts, accommodates subthreshold presentations and aligns with the evidence on the continuous distribution of personality pathology. Its disadvantages are complexity, unfamiliarity and imperfect correspondence with administrative systems.

### Combining them

For practical purposes, the assessor can combine the approaches: establish the severity of impairment dimensionally, describe the trait profile, and translate into a categorical diagnosis where one is required for communication or access. This produces reports that are both recognisable and accurate, and it avoids the choice between a system that is familiar and a system that is better.

## 10.3|Identity and Self-Functioning

The self domain of personality functioning comprises identity and self-direction. Both are assessed through interview and observation, and both are central to the severity judgement.

### Identity

Identity refers to the experience of a coherent and differentiated sense of self, with stable self-esteem and an accurate appraisal of one's capacities and limitations. Impairment is evident in a self-concept that shifts markedly with circumstances or relationships, in self-esteem that fluctuates between extremes, in an inability to describe oneself beyond a few characteristics, and in a fragile sense of boundaries between self and others. The assessor can explore identity by asking the client to describe themselves, what they value, what they would be like without their current difficulties, and how they have changed over time; the coherence and stability of the answer is the data.

### Self-direction

Self-direction refers to the pursuit of meaningful short- and longer-term goals, and the capacity to use self-reflection productively. Impairment is evident in goals that change unpredictably, in an absence of goals, in goals that are not connected to the person's capacities, and in an inability to learn from experience. Assessment requires enquiry into the person's plans, their history of pursuing them, and their explanation for what has not worked.

### Assessment methods

Identity and self-direction are assessed primarily by interview, supported by self-report instruments designed for the dimensional model, by informants who know the person across contexts, and by observation of the person's capacity to reflect within the assessment session itself. A client who can describe their difficulties with nuance and connect them to their history demonstrates a level of self-functioning that a checklist cannot capture.

## 10.4|Interpersonal Functioning

The interpersonal domain comprises empathy and intimacy. These functions determine much of the person's relational life and much of the difficulty that brings them to services.

### Empathy

Empathy comprises the capacity to understand and appreciate others' experiences and motives, and the capacity to consider alternative perspectives. Impairment ranges from a relative insensitivity to others' feelings, through an ability to understand intellectually but not to be moved, to a marked inability to consider others as separate psychological beings. Assessment is by interview, by exploration of specific relationships, and by observation of the client's behaviour in the session — including the client's response to the assessor's attempts to express a different perspective.

### Intimacy

Intimacy refers to the capacity for enduring, close and mutually satisfying relationships. Impairment is evident in relationships that are shallow, unstable, exploitative or marked by fear; in an inability to tolerate closeness or to maintain connections across conflict; and in patterns of pursuing then withdrawing. Enquiry should cover the number and duration of close relationships, the pattern of their endings, the person's account of why they ended, and the account of an informant where available.

### Relational patterns in the assessment relationship

The client's way of relating is enacted in the assessment. Idealisation of the assessor, devaluation, withdrawal, compliance, testing behaviour and seductiveness are all observable, and all are data. The assessor should record them descriptively, without interpretation in the moment, and use them in the formulation: what happens between assessor and client is often a sample of what happens elsewhere.

## 10.5|Longitudinal and Developmental Evidence

Personality disorder is by definition enduring, and the evidence for endurance must be longitudinal. A single cross-sectional assessment cannot establish that a pattern has been present since adolescence or that it is stable across contexts.

### Historical sources

Sources include the person's own account of their adolescence and early adulthood, school reports, employment records, accounts of family and friends, and previous clinical records. The assessor should look for a pattern that predates the presenting problem and that appears across contexts: the difficulties at school, the repeated job losses, the friendships that ended for the same reason, the family's longstanding description of the person as "always like this".

### The developmental trajectory

The trajectory matters: a pattern that appeared abruptly at 30 after a trauma is not a personality disorder, even if the current presentation resembles one. The assessor should establish the age at which the pattern was first evident, its consistency across development, and any periods of relative remission. Where the history is ambiguous, the diagnosis should be provisional and the ambiguity recorded.

### The problem of retrospective reconstruction

Clients reconstruct their history in the light of their present understanding, and a person who has recently received a personality disorder diagnosis may describe their adolescence through that lens. The assessor should prefer contemporaneous evidence — records, informant accounts — and should treat retrospective accounts as informative about the person's current self-understanding as well as about their history.

## 10.6|Differentiating State from Trait

The most consequential differential in this area is between an enduring pattern and a current state. Depression, anxiety, psychosis, substance use, acute stress and physical illness can all produce behaviour that resembles personality pathology.

### State effects on personality measurement

Acute depression inflates scores on scales measuring dependency, self-criticism, neuroticism and interpersonal sensitivity. Acute anxiety inflates scores on scales measuring avoidance and suspiciousness. Substance use inflates scores on scales measuring impulsivity and hostility. Psychosis distorts self-report entirely. Assessing personality during an acute episode therefore risks a diagnosis that describes the episode rather than the person.

### Practical guidance

The practical guidance is to defer personality assessment until the acute state has resolved, or to interpret scores obtained during an acute state with explicit caution. Where deferral is impossible — because the assessment is required for an urgent decision — the assessor should use informant and historical data, record the state effects, and state the limits of the conclusion. This is one of the clearest cases in which "the assessment cannot answer this question now" is the correct professional output.

### The differential with other conditions

The differential includes bipolar disorder (where mood instability may resemble affective instability), autism spectrum disorder (where social difficulties may resemble schizoid or avoidant patterns), complex post-traumatic stress disorder (where affect dysregulation and identity disturbance may resemble borderline patterns), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (where impulsivity may resemble antisocial or borderline features), and substance use disorders. Each requires its own assessment rather than being subsumed under a personality diagnosis.

## 10.7|Personality and Trauma

The relationship between trauma and personality disorder is clinically important and conceptually contested. Many people with severe personality disorder have histories of significant adversity; many people with significant adversity do not develop personality disorder.

### Complex trauma

The ICD-11 diagnosis of complex post-traumatic stress disorder describes a presentation arising after prolonged or repeated trauma, characterised by the core PTSD symptoms plus disturbances in affect regulation, self-concept and relational functioning. Its overlap with borderline personality disorder is substantial, and the two are often used interchangeably in practice despite being distinct constructs with different evidence bases. The assessor should determine whether the core PTSD symptoms are present; if they are, complex PTSD is the more specific diagnosis.

### Trauma as an explanatory framework

Where trauma is present, it provides an explanatory framework for the pattern: hypervigilance, mistrust, emotional dysregulation, avoidance of intimacy and a diminished sense of self can all be understood as adaptations to an unsafe environment. This framing is more useful clinically than a trait description, because it identifies targets for intervention and conveys that the pattern made sense. It does not, however, remove the need for an accurate description of current functioning.

### Avoiding false attribution

The converse error is attributing all difficulty to trauma and failing to consider other explanations, including conditions that require different treatment. A client with an autism spectrum condition and a trauma history may receive only a trauma formulation and miss the support that would address the developmental condition. The assessor should generate both explanations and test them.

## 10.8|Personality and Neurodevelopmental Conditions

Autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder are frequently misdiagnosed as personality disorders, and vice versa, with serious consequences for treatment.

### Autism

Autistic adults may present with social withdrawal, literalism, intense special interests, sensory sensitivities and difficulty understanding social expectations. These features may be read as schizoid, avoidant or obsessive-compulsive patterns. The distinguishing evidence is developmental: a lifelong pattern of social communication difference, sensory features, and a history of social difficulty that predates the onset of other symptoms. Assessment requires a developmental history, ideally with an informant, and consideration of structured instruments for autistic traits where indicated.

### Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder

Adults with ADHD may present with impulsivity, emotional dysregulation and unstable relationships, features that overlap with borderline and antisocial patterns. The distinguishing evidence is again developmental — onset in childhood, pervasive inattention or hyperactivity, and a consistent pattern across contexts — together with response to treatment. Where both conditions are present, both should be diagnosed.

### The implication

The implication is that a personality disorder diagnosis should not be made without a developmental history. Where the developmental history is unavailable, the diagnosis should be provisional, and the possibility of a neurodevelopmental condition should be recorded.

## 10.9|Personality and Substance Use

Substance use complicates personality assessment in three ways: it can produce behaviour that resembles personality pathology; it can be a consequence of personality difficulties; and it can be both.

### Substance-induced behaviour

Intoxication and withdrawal produce impulsivity, irritability, affective instability, suspiciousness and interpersonal conflict. A personality assessment conducted during a period of active substance use is unreliable, and the assessor should establish the pattern of use, the periods of abstinence and the behaviour during abstinence.

### Differential considerations

Antisocial and borderline features are common in people with substance use disorders, and the question is whether the personality features preceded the substance use and persist in its absence, or whether they are consequences of it. The answer requires a history that separates the two, and it usually requires a period of abstinence before a firm conclusion is possible.

### Combined presentation

Where both are present, both should be diagnosed and treated. The historical practice of withholding treatment for personality difficulties until substance use has resolved has been challenged on both ethical and empirical grounds, and services increasingly offer integrated treatment. The assessor's report should support integrated planning rather than a sequential demand that the client may be unable to meet.

## 10.10|Structured Personality Interviews

Structured and semi-structured interviews for personality disorder improve the reliability of diagnosis and reduce the influence of the assessor's impressions. They are time-consuming, require training, and are under-used in routine practice.

### Available instruments

The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Personality Disorders (SCID-5-PD; First, Williams, Benjamin, & Spitzer, 2016) assesses the categorical diagnoses. The Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (SCID-5-AMPD) assesses the level of personality functioning and the trait domains. The International Personality Disorder Examination and the Schedules for Clinical Assessment in Neuropsychiatry provide alternative approaches. Each has strengths and a training requirement; none replaces the need for developmental and collateral information.

### Using them well

A structured personality interview should be used with a client who is not in an acute state, with sufficient time to cover the domains, and with the assessor's attention to the interpersonal process as well as the content. The interview's structure supports reliability, but the assessor still has to decide whether the reported pattern is enduring, pervasive and impairing, which requires judgement beyond the instrument.

### Their limits

Structured interviews assess reported experience at a point in time. They do not establish the developmental history, they do not capture the interpersonal process of the assessment, and they cannot distinguish state from trait without additional information. Their output is a set of criteria met, which is the beginning rather than the end of the assessment.

## 10.11|Feedback without Stigma

Feedback on personality assessment is where the diagnosis either helps or harms. There is good evidence that the way a diagnosis is communicated affects how it is received, how the client engages with treatment and how other professionals treat the client.

### Principles

Feedback should be explanatory rather than evaluative, describe patterns and their origins rather than traits as defects, be specific and behavioural, invite correction, and connect to treatment. It should acknowledge the difficulty of hearing the information and the client's own expertise about themselves. It should avoid words with a history of pejorative use where a descriptive alternative exists.

### Discussing the diagnosis

Where a personality disorder diagnosis is given, the assessor should explain what it means and what it does not mean: that it describes a pattern of difficulty that developed in response to circumstances, that it is not a judgement of the person's worth, that it can change, and that effective treatments exist. The assessor should also explain why the diagnosis is being recorded, since clients often experience the diagnosis as a label applied without consent.

### The record as a document the client may read

Clinical records are increasingly accessible to clients, and the language used in them has consequences. Descriptions should be written as though the client will read them, which improves both accuracy and respect. Pejorative terms, speculation presented as fact, and irrelevant personal detail should be excluded.

## 10.12|Formulating Enduring Relational Patterns

The formulation is the point at which assessment becomes useful. It should explain how the pattern developed, what it achieves for the person, what it costs, and what would need to change.

### Structure

A useful structure describes the core pattern (for example, anticipating rejection and withdrawing pre-emptively), its developmental origins (a history in which expressing needs led to humiliation), its maintaining processes (withdrawal produces relief and prevents disconfirmation), its costs (isolation, depression, lost opportunities) and its adaptive value (protection from further humiliation). It then identifies the point of intervention: the moment between the anticipation and the withdrawal.

### Testability

The formulation should be testable: if it is right, then a specified intervention should produce a specified change, and a specified observation should disconfirm it. A formulation that cannot be wrong is not a formulation.

### Sharing the formulation

The formulation should be shared with the client in accessible language and revised in the light of their response. Clients frequently improve a formulation by correcting the assessor's account of their history, and the process of discussing it can itself be therapeutic, since it models curiosity about the pattern rather than judgement of the person.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 29-year-old woman is referred by her general practitioner after her third presentation at an emergency department in six months with self-harm. The general practitioner asks whether she has a personality disorder and what would help.

**The evidence.** She describes a longstanding pattern of intense relationships that end abruptly, a self-concept that changes depending on who she is with, chronic feelings of emptiness, and recurrent self-harm in response to perceived rejection. The pattern began in adolescence and has been present across contexts. She has a history of childhood emotional abuse and neglect. She currently meets criteria for a moderate depressive episode and reports daily cannabis use. She has had two courses of therapy, both of which ended after a few sessions when she felt the therapist was "losing interest". A structured interview confirms impairment in identity, self-direction, empathy and intimacy, with prominent negative affectivity and disinhibition.

**The reasoning.** The pattern is enduring, pervasive and impairing, and it predates the current depressive episode. Complex PTSD must be considered: she describes hypervigilance and avoidance but does not report re-experiencing or a sense of current threat of the kind required. The cannabis use may be exacerbating the affective instability but does not explain the developmental pattern. The repeated treatment endings are the most important clinical fact in the assessment, because they predict what will happen with the next treatment.

**The decision.** The report records a personality disorder of moderate severity with a provisional complex PTSD differential, documents the depressive episode and the substance use as co-occurring conditions requiring attention, and recommends a structured treatment with an explicit frame, agreement about contact between sessions, and a single named coordinator. It specifies that the repeated endings should be anticipated and used as clinical material rather than treated as treatment failure. It records the client's own explanation of the endings and her goals for treatment.

**The teaching point.** The diagnosis was less important than the pattern it described, and the pattern's most useful expression was in the treatment history. The recommendation followed from that history, not from the label.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Personality disorder is a disorder of functioning rather than a defect of character, and its assessment requires evidence of endurance, pervasiveness and impairment. Contemporary models describe severity of impairment in self and interpersonal functioning together with maladaptive traits, which is more informative than categorical diagnosis alone. Identity, self-direction, empathy and intimacy are assessed by interview, informant report and observation of the assessment relationship. Longitudinal and developmental evidence is essential, and the differential with state effects, trauma, neurodevelopmental conditions and substance use must be worked through before a diagnosis is made. Structured interviews improve reliability without replacing judgement. Feedback and formulation determine whether the assessment helps, and both should be explanatory, specific and free of stigmatising language.
