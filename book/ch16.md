# CHAPTER 16|Integrated Case Formulation, Differential Diagnosis, Report Writing and Feedback

:::panel Learning Objectives
After working through this chapter, the reader should be able to:
- integrate multimethod evidence using an explicit and auditable procedure;
- weigh conflicting findings by examining their provenance and quality;
- treat differential diagnosis as hypothesis testing rather than pattern matching;
- construct formulations that go beyond diagnostic labels and predict treatment response;
- write psychological reports that answer the referral question for each audience;
- communicate uncertainty in ways that support rather than undermine decisions;
- use feedback as a clinical intervention and as a source of calibration;
- appraise digital tools and artificial intelligence in assessment with appropriate scepticism.
:::

## Introduction

The preceding chapters have addressed the components of advanced assessment: questioning, interviewing, measurement, instrument-specific methods and particular clinical populations. This final chapter addresses the activity that binds them — integration — and the products through which integration reaches other people: the formulation, the report and the feedback conversation.

Integration is the point at which technical competence becomes clinical judgement, and it is the point at which most assessments either succeed or fail. An assessment that produces accurate scores but no coherent account of the person has not done its job; so has one that produces a coherent account unsupported by the evidence. The discipline required is to hold both: to construct a formulation that explains, predicts and can be tested, while remaining accountable to the data that produced it. ## 16.1|Integrating Multimethod Evidence

Integration is not the accumulation of findings but the construction of an argument. The assessor arrays the evidence, weighs it and states a conclusion whose confidence is proportionate to the evidence.

### The integration matrix

A practical device is a matrix in which the rows are the hypotheses under consideration and the columns are the sources of evidence: interview, observation, informant report, records, test scores and functional information. Each cell records whether the source supports, contradicts or is silent on the hypothesis. The matrix makes reasoning visible, exposes gaps, and identifies which hypotheses rest on a single source. It also prevents the common error of treating the most memorable finding as the most important one.

### Weighing evidence

Weighting depends on four considerations: the quality of the source (a well-validated instrument administered correctly versus an impression formed in a single interview); its independence (two sources that derive from the same informant are not independent); its relevance (evidence about a construct that is adjacent to the hypothesis is weaker than evidence about the construct itself); and its recency (for questions about current state, recent evidence is stronger).

### Reaching a conclusion

The conclusion should identify the best-supported hypothesis, the alternatives that remain open, the evidence that would change the conclusion, and the confidence with which it is held. Where the evidence does not support any hypothesis, that is a conclusion and should be stated. Where the evidence supports several, the conclusion should state which is primary for the purpose at hand — treatment, risk management, accommodation — since the priority may differ by purpose.

## 16.2|Weighing Conflicting Findings

Conflict among sources is the norm rather than the exception, and the response to conflict distinguishes competent from incompetent integration.

### Investigating rather than averaging

Averaging incompatible findings produces a conclusion that no source supports. The correct response is to investigate why the sources disagree: differences in setting, in informant perspective, in the conditions of administration, in the construct measured, or in the honesty of reporting. The investigation usually resolves the conflict, and when it does not, the unresolved conflict is itself a finding to be reported.

### Common patterns of conflict

Common patterns include self-report that is more severe than observed functioning (which may indicate concealment in the session, situational variation, or over-reporting), informant report that is more severe than self-report (which may indicate limited insight, concealment from the assessor, or an informant with their own investment in the severity), and test scores that are lower than functional performance (which may indicate invalid performance, an unfamiliar testing format, or genuine compensation).

### Reporting conflict

Where conflict remains unresolved, the report should present it, describe the competing explanations and their implications, and state which explanation the assessor considers more probable and why. A report that conceals the conflict in order to present a coherent narrative is less honest and less useful than one that presents it.

## 16.3|Differential Diagnosis as Hypothesis Testing

Differential diagnosis is the application of scientific reasoning to a clinical question. The assessor generates hypotheses, identifies the observations that would discriminate among them, gathers those observations, and revises the hypotheses accordingly.

### Generating the hypotheses

The hypothesis set should include the most likely explanation, the most consequential explanation that must not be missed, and the explanation that the client or referrer favours. The set should also include explanations that are not diagnoses: developmental conditions, substance effects, medical conditions, contextual and relational factors, and normal variation.

### Specifying the discriminating evidence

For each pair of hypotheses, the assessor specifies the findings that would separate them. This converts the assessment plan into a set of targeted questions and observations, and it makes the assessment auditable: a reader can see which discriminations were attempted and which were not.

### Revising rather than confirming

Hypotheses should be revised as evidence accumulates, and the assessor should record the revision. A record that shows a single hypothesis from beginning to end, with all evidence supporting it, is a record of confirmation bias rather than of assessment. The most valuable records show what was considered and discarded, and why.

## 16.4|Formulation beyond Diagnostic Labels

A diagnosis classifies; a formulation explains. The formulation is what makes the assessment useful for treatment, and it is the part of the report most often missing or perfunctory.

### Requirements of a formulation

A good formulation is explanatory (it accounts for why this presentation developed and persists), individual (it is specific to this person rather than a generic account of the disorder), testable (it predicts what will happen under specified conditions), useful (it identifies points of intervention) and collaborative (it can be discussed with the client).

### Structure

A workable structure addresses: the presenting problem and its development; predisposing factors; precipitating factors; maintaining factors; protective factors; the current context; the formulation itself as a narrative account; and the implications for intervention. The maintaining factors carry most of the clinical weight, because they are the most modifiable.

### Formulation in different traditions

Cognitive behavioural formulations identify maintaining cycles of thought, emotion and behaviour; psychodynamic formulations identify repeating relational patterns and defences; systemic formulations identify interactional patterns within relationships; narrative formulations identify the stories through which the person understands their difficulties. The assessor need not be committed to one tradition, but should be explicit about the one being used, since different traditions generate different interventions.

## 16.5|Linking Assessment to Treatment Planning

The link between assessment and treatment is the recommendation, and recommendations are where assessment is most often wasted.

### Specifying the target

Recommendations should identify what is to be changed, not merely what is to be done. "Cognitive behavioural therapy" is a treatment; "cognitive behavioural therapy targeting avoidance of social situations and the belief that others will judge him as incompetent" is a plan. The target follows from the formulation, and the formulation should make it obvious.

### Sequencing

Recommendations should be sequenced. Some targets must be addressed before others: substance use before trauma-focused work where the substance use prevents engagement; medical stabilisation before psychological intervention where a medical condition is contributing; safety planning before any intervention that might increase distress. The reasoning for the sequence should be recorded.

### Feasibility

Recommendations should be feasible in the setting where they will be implemented. Waiting lists, funding, the client's caring responsibilities, transport and language all constrain what is possible. A recommendation that cannot be implemented is not neutral; it can damage the client's trust and the referrer's confidence in assessment.

### Specifying what would count as progress

A plan should specify how progress will be recognised and what would prompt a revision. This is the practical expression of the formulation's testability, and it converts the assessment into something that can be evaluated.

## 16.6|Writing Clear Psychological Reports

The report is the assessment's public form. It is read by referrers, clients, families, tribunals and courts, and its quality determines whether the assessment has any effect.

### Structure

A conventional structure includes: identifying information; reason for referral; sources of information; background; behavioural observations and mental status; assessment methods; results by domain; formulation; diagnostic conclusions where justified; strengths and protective factors; recommendations; and limitations. The structure can be varied, but the elements should all be present, and the report should answer the referral question explicitly and early.

### Writing for the reader

Different audiences need different things. A general practitioner needs a clear summary, a diagnosis if one is warranted, and specific recommendations. A tribunal needs the evidence, the reasoning and the limits. A client needs language they can understand and a formulation they recognise. Where a report serves several audiences, a summary at the beginning and a clearly labelled section for the client can meet most needs.

### Language

Reports should be written in plain, precise professional English. Jargon should be explained or avoided. Diagnostic terms should be used consistently and defined at first use. Pejorative terms should be excluded. Sentences should be short enough to be understood on one reading, and the report should be free of unnecessary hedging and of unjustified certainty.

### Length and proportionality

The report should be as long as the question requires and no longer. A short report that answers the question is more useful than a long one that documents the process. Where an assessment has been extensive, the detail can be placed in appendices so that the main report remains readable.

## 16.7|Communicating Uncertainty

Uncertainty is inherent in assessment, and its communication is a professional skill rather than an admission of inadequacy.

### Why uncertainty must be communicated

Decisions made on the basis of overstated certainty can harm clients and mislead referrers. A report that presents a provisional conclusion as settled may lead to a treatment that is inappropriate, or to a decision — about custody, employment or liberty — that would have been different had the uncertainty been visible.

### How to communicate it

Uncertainty should be stated specifically: what is uncertain, why, how much it matters and what would resolve it. "The diagnosis is uncertain" is uninformative; "the evidence supports a moderate depressive episode, but the duration depends on the client's dating of onset, which conflicts with the general practitioner's record; a review in six weeks with a symptom diary would resolve it" is useful.

### The limits of hedging

Excessive hedging is also a failure. A report that hedges every statement leaves the reader unable to act. The appropriate balance is to be decisive where the evidence permits and explicit about uncertainty where it does not, and to distinguish clearly between the two.

## 16.8|Feedback as a Clinical Intervention

Feedback is not the delivery of a verdict but a structured conversation in which the findings are discussed, tested and connected to action.

### Principles

Feedback should be collaborative, specific, developmental and connected to the client's goals. It should begin with what the assessment found that is positive or resourceful, present the difficulties in behavioural terms, invite the client's perspective, and end with a plan that the client has contributed to.

### The evidence base

Research on therapeutic assessment has shown that collaborative feedback can reduce symptoms and improve engagement, particularly when the client is involved in interpreting the findings. The mechanism appears to involve the restoration of a coherent self-narrative and the experience of being understood. Feedback that is delivered as a lecture has no such effect and may be harmful.

### Handling disagreement

Where the client disagrees with the findings, the disagreement should be explored rather than dismissed. The client may be right; the assessment may have missed context; or the finding may be correct but premature. The assessor should record the client's response and any revision that follows.

### Feedback to families and systems

Where the assessment concerns a child, a couple or a family, feedback must be managed across multiple participants, with attention to safety, confidentiality and the relationships among them. Where the assessment has identified harm within the system, feedback must be handled with particular care and with reference to safeguarding procedures.

## 16.9|Ethical and Legal Responsibilities

The final chapter of a textbook on assessment is the appropriate place to state the responsibilities that frame everything else.

### Competence

The assessor should practise within their competence, seek supervision where the presentation or the question lies outside it, and decline referrals that require expertise they do not hold. Competence includes cultural competence, which requires ongoing learning rather than a fixed body of knowledge.

### Consent and confidentiality

Consent should be informed, specific and recorded. Confidentiality should be explained, including its limits. Where information must be disclosed without consent, the decision and its reasoning should be recorded.

### Duties to third parties

In most jurisdictions, the assessor owes duties to people other than the client: to warn or protect potential victims of violence, to report child abuse, to report risk to the public. The precise duties vary by jurisdiction, and the assessor should know the local framework.

### Record keeping

Records should be accurate, contemporaneous, legible and stored securely. They should be sufficient for another professional to understand what was done and why. They are increasingly accessible to clients and may be disclosed in legal proceedings.

### The limits of the role

The assessor is not an investigator, a judge or a police officer. The role is to assess, formulate and communicate, and to act within the legal and ethical framework when risk or harm is identified. Recognising the limits of the role is part of competence.

## 16.10|Digital Assessment and Artificial Intelligence

Digital tools are transforming assessment, and their use raises questions that the profession has not fully resolved.

### Current applications

Applications include online administration of questionnaires, computer-adaptive testing, automated scoring and interpretation, ecological momentary assessment, digital phenotyping through smartphone sensors, and machine learning models trained on clinical data to predict outcomes. Each has potential benefits: accessibility, frequency of measurement, reduced scoring error and the detection of patterns that clinicians miss.

### Risks and limitations

The risks include validation gaps (a tool validated in one population or format may not generalise), opacity (machine learning models whose reasoning cannot be inspected), automation bias (clinicians deferring to algorithmic output), data protection and privacy, unequal access, and the possibility that a model's predictions encode existing inequities. Interpretation generated automatically cannot know the client, the context or the purpose of the assessment.

### Professional responsibility

The professional obligation to justify conclusions in terms of evidence about this client cannot be delegated to software. Where digital tools are used, the assessor should know their validation, record their use, verify their output, and remain accountable for the interpretation. Regulatory frameworks for medical devices and for automated decision-making are developing, and practitioners should keep abreast of the requirements in their jurisdiction.

## 16.11|Quality Assurance and Peer Consultation

Individual competence is necessary but not sufficient; the quality of assessment depends also on the systems within which it is conducted.

### Individual practices

Individual quality assurance includes routine use of scoring checks, confidence intervals and base rates; recording of hypotheses and their revision; review of assessments in which the outcome differed from the prediction; and participation in continuing professional development that includes assessment specifically.

### Peer consultation

Peer consultation — the routine discussion of assessments with a colleague — is the most effective single practice for improving judgement. It exposes reasoning to challenge, identifies blind spots, and provides the calibration that individual reflection cannot. It should be routine rather than reserved for difficult cases, and it should include the cases that went well, since these are where overconfidence develops.

### Service-level practices

Services can support quality through audit of reports against standards, review of serious incidents, monitoring of assessment outcomes against later information, training in specific instruments, and supervision arrangements that include assessment. Where services measure the quality of their reports, the quality improves; where they do not, it varies with the individual practitioner.

## 16.12|Advanced Case Conference and Final Synthesis

The final section of the final chapter returns to the case conference format used throughout the book, this time as a synthesis of the whole.

### The method

A case conference presents the referral question, the evidence, the reasoning, the formulation, the conclusions and the recommendations, and invites challenge. Its purpose is to expose the reasoning to informed scrutiny before the report is finalised, and to give the assessor the benefit of perspectives they do not have.

### The discipline

The discipline of presenting a case to colleagues requires the assessor to make their reasoning explicit, which is the same discipline required by a well-written report. The case conference is therefore both a quality assurance mechanism and a training method, and services that hold them regularly produce better assessments.

### Closing

Advanced assessment is a craft that improves with deliberate practice, supervision and honest review. The instruments will change; the diagnostic systems will be revised; the digital tools will become more capable. What will not change is the requirement to reason carefully from evidence to conclusion, to state the limits of that conclusion, and to communicate it in a way that helps the person it concerns. That requirement is the subject of this book, and meeting it is the mark of the advanced practitioner.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 61-year-old woman is referred by her general practitioner for assessment before a decision about whether she can return to work as a primary school teacher. She has been absent for five months with what was initially diagnosed as depression, and then as "possible early dementia" after she reported memory difficulties. Two cognitive screens have been borderline. The question is whether she has a cognitive impairment and whether she can return to teaching.

**The evidence.** The interview establishes that she has been profoundly depressed since her husband's death eighteen months ago, with early waking, weight loss of ten kilograms, and pervasive anhedonia. Her memory complaints began at the same time and are worse when she is low. Cognitive assessment shows high-average verbal knowledge, average perceptual reasoning, low-average working memory and processing speed, and memory scores in the low-average range with a strong benefit from cueing. Performance validity is adequate. She slept four hours the night before the assessment and had not eaten. Two informants describe her as "not herself" since the bereavement but "sharp as anything" when discussing her grandchildren. A dementia screen completed by her daughter is in the normal range. There is no history of cognitive decline before the bereavement.

**The reasoning.** The presentation is consistent with a severe depressive episode with cognitive impairment, a well-recognised phenomenon sometimes described as pseudodementia, and inconsistent with a neurodegenerative process: the onset was abrupt and coincident with bereavement, the memory pattern is one of retrieval difficulty rather than encoding failure, verbal knowledge is preserved, informants describe preserved function in engaging contexts, and there is no progressive trajectory. The "possible early dementia" diagnosis appears to have been based on borderline screens obtained during the depressive episode, without premorbid comparison or informant data.

**The decision.** The report declines to diagnose a neurocognitive disorder, explains the basis for that conclusion, and recommends treatment for depression before any determination of cognitive status. On fitness to return to teaching, it states that a return is not currently indicated, that the prognosis is likely to be favourable with treatment, and that a repeat cognitive assessment should be conducted when the depression has remitted. It recommends that the "possible dementia" label be removed from the primary care record, and states why.

**The teaching point.** The most consequential intervention in this assessment was a negative conclusion: removing a diagnosis that was inaccurate, stigmatising and likely to follow the client for years. Advanced assessment is not only the production of findings; it is also the correction of conclusions that were reached without adequate evidence.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Integration is the construction of an argument from evidence, conducted through an explicit procedure that arrays hypotheses against sources, weighs evidence by quality, independence, relevance and recency, and states a conclusion with proportionate confidence. Conflicting findings should be investigated rather than averaged, and unresolved conflict should be reported. Differential diagnosis is hypothesis testing, and the record should show which hypotheses were considered and discarded. Formulations must explain, individualise, predict and identify points of intervention. Recommendations should specify targets, sequence, feasibility and what would count as progress. Reports should answer the referral question for their audiences, in plain professional prose and with explicit and specific statements of uncertainty. Feedback is a clinical intervention when it is collaborative. Ethical and legal responsibilities frame the whole enterprise, and digital tools change its mechanics without transferring professional accountability. Quality assurance and peer consultation are the practices that sustain competence over time.
