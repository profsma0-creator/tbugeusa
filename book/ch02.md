## Introduction

The interview is the most used and least examined instrument in mental health practice. It is used to establish rapport, gather history, observe behaviour, assess risk and arrive at a formulation, and it is expected to accomplish all of this within an hour and without a manual. Yet the interview is also a measurement procedure with known properties: it is sensitive to interviewer style, vulnerable to memory reconstruction on the part of both parties, and dependent for its validity on the questions that were asked and the questions that were not.

This chapter treats interviewing as a technical activity that can be designed, evaluated and improved. It sets out how to build an interview from the referral question, how to conduct and record a mental status examination, how to establish chronology, how to test alternative explanations, and how to document reasoning. The emphasis throughout is on the difference between a conversation that produces a warm feeling and an interview that produces defensible evidence. The two are not opposites; the best interviews produce both, but when they diverge, evidence must win.

## 2.1|Designing a Purposeful Clinical Interview

An interview should be designed before it is conducted. Design means deciding, in advance, which questions must be answered, which methods will answer them, how long each element will take and what will be sacrificed if time runs short. Interviews that are not designed tend to follow the client's narrative wherever it leads: informative, often humane, but unreliable as evidence, because the topics that were covered reflect conversational flow rather than the decision at hand.

### From question to interview plan

The design process begins with the referral question and works backwards. If the question concerns diagnostic status, the interview must cover criterion-relevant symptoms, their duration and their functional impact, and must probe the boundary with neighbouring conditions. If it concerns formulation for psychotherapy, the interview must establish developmental history, relational patterns, maintaining cycles and the client's own understanding. If it concerns risk, the interview must cover ideation, intent, plan, means, past behaviour, protective factors and the immediate environment. A single interview can serve more than one purpose, but only if the purposes are named and time is allocated to each.

### Structure and flexibility

Structure and responsiveness are usually presented as a tension; in practice they are complementary. A structured spine — a fixed sequence of domains that will be covered regardless of how the conversation develops — frees the interviewer to follow the client's account within each domain without fear of losing coverage. The spine protects against the common failure in which an emotionally charged opening topic consumes the session and the developmental history is never taken. Flexibility then operates inside the structure: the order of questions, the depth of probing and the use of silence are all adjustable.

### Time budgeting

Experienced interviewers budget time explicitly. A ninety-minute first interview might allocate fifteen minutes to the presenting problem and its chronology, fifteen to developmental and family history, ten to medical and substance history, fifteen to the mental status examination, ten to risk, ten to the client's goals and expectations, and fifteen to feedback and closure. Where the budget is exceeded in one domain, the interviewer decides consciously what will be deferred and records the decision. Unrecorded omissions become invisible gaps in the eventual report.

### The interviewer as an instrument

Every interviewer has a characteristic style and a characteristic set of blind spots. Some ask leading questions under time pressure; some avoid asking about suicide; some over-elaborate the areas they find interesting. Because the interviewer is the instrument, the instrument requires calibration: recording interviews, reviewing them with a supervisor, and comparing one's own conclusions with those of a colleague who saw the same client. These practices are standard in psychotherapy training and under-used in assessment training, where the assumption persists that interviewing is a natural skill rather than a disciplined one.

## 2.2|Opening the Interview and Establishing Rapport

Rapport in assessment is not a preliminary courtesy but a validity condition. Clients who are anxious, suspicious or uncertain about the purpose of the interview give shorter answers, under-report stigmatised experiences and withhold information about risk. The opening minutes therefore do substantive work: they establish why the assessment is taking place, who will see the results, what the limits of confidentiality are, and what the client can expect to happen.

### Explaining purpose and limits

The explanation should be given in plain language and should be specific to the setting. A formulation such as the following covers the essentials without jargon: "You have been referred by your general practitioner because of difficulty sleeping and low mood. I will ask about how this started, what has made it better or worse, and how it is affecting your life. I will write a report for your doctor, and I will show you what I have written before it is sent. What you tell me is confidential, with the exception of a serious risk to your safety or someone else's, which I would discuss with you first wherever possible."

Consent is then obtained for the assessment as described, and separately for any recording, collateral contact or release of information. Consent obtained at the start is not a substitute for consent at each subsequent stage; clients retain the right to decline particular lines of enquiry.

### Managing expectations and anxiety

Clients arrive with assumptions about assessment, often drawn from television, previous negative experiences or fear of being judged. Naming these assumptions early is usually more effective than reassurance. "Some people worry that this is a test they can fail, or that what they say will be used against them. It is not, and it will not be, unless there is a risk I have to act on; if that happens I will tell you." Where a client has had a previous assessment they experienced as dismissive, asking about it yields both rapport and information about how they are likely to present now.

### Culture, language and the etiquette of disclosure

The conventions of first meetings vary. Direct eye contact, the use of first names, questions about family or about income may be appropriate in one cultural setting and intrusive in another. Interpreters change the interpersonal geometry of the interview and require their own briefing. Clients from settings where mental distress is expressed somatically may open with bodily complaints, and the interviewer who redirects too quickly to psychological content may lose the account entirely. The practical rule is to ask about, rather than assume, the client's preferences on matters of address, accompaniment and disclosure.

## 2.3|History of Presenting Concerns

The history of the presenting concern is the evidential centre of the interview. It must establish what the problem is, when it began, how it has changed, what makes it better or worse, what the client has already tried, and what the client believes is happening. Each of these elements supports a different inference, and each is vulnerable to a characteristic distortion.

### Establishing the problem in behavioural terms

Clients describe problems in evaluative language: "I'm a failure", "I can't cope", "my anxiety is terrible". The interviewer's task is to convert these evaluations into descriptions that can be dated, counted and compared. What does "can't cope" look like on a Tuesday morning? How many days in the past month did the client leave the house? What does "terrible" mean on a scale where the worst week of the client's life is ten? Conversion is not pedantry; it is what makes change measurable and what allows a second clinician to understand the baseline.

### Onset, duration and course

Dating onset is more difficult than it appears, because clients reconstruct beginnings in the light of present understanding. Three techniques improve accuracy: anchoring to public events (a move, a bereavement, a job change, a pandemic); asking about the last period of normal functioning and working forwards; and obtaining collateral confirmation. The distinction between abrupt and gradual onset, between episodic and continuous course, and between stable and deteriorating trajectory carries substantial diagnostic weight. A first episode of psychosis that develops over two years is a different clinical object from one that develops over two weeks.

### Precipitants, maintaining factors and prior attempts

Having established the pattern, the interview turns to what surrounds it. Precipitants are events temporally associated with onset; maintaining factors are processes that keep the problem going, including avoidance, reassurance-seeking, sleep disruption, alcohol use, relationship conflict and occupational strain. Asking what the client has already tried — and what happened — yields information about both the problem and the client's model of it. Clients who have consulted five practitioners and been helped by none are telling the assessor something that should be examined rather than assumed.

### The client's explanatory model

Every client arrives with an explanation. It may be biomedical, moral, interpersonal, spiritual or none of these. Eliciting the explanation serves three purposes: it identifies beliefs that will shape engagement; it may contain information the assessor lacks; and it establishes the terms in which feedback will eventually have to be given. Explanations that are factually incorrect, such as a belief that low mood is caused by a permanently damaged brain, can often be modified through discussion of evidence, but only after they have been heard.

## 2.4|Developmental, Family and Social History

Developmental history is often deferred when time is short, and its absence is one of the most frequent weaknesses of assessment reports. Yet it supplies information that no current-state measure can: the trajectory of the person's functioning, the presence of longstanding difficulties that were previously tolerated, and the family and social context in which the present problem is embedded.

### Developmental milestones and early history

Pregnancy and birth complications, early illness, language and motor milestones, schooling and peer relationships, and the age at which difficulties were first noticed all belong in the record. The purpose is not to assemble a complete biography but to identify deviations with diagnostic relevance: a child whose language developed late and who has always found social rules opaque presents a different differential from one whose difficulties began abruptly at fourteen. Where the client cannot report early history, an informant or contemporaneous record should be sought, and the source of the information stated.

### Family history

Family history of mental disorder, substance use, suicide and neurological illness is relevant to both risk and formulation. It should be recorded with the same care as other clinical information: who, what, when, and whether the diagnosis was made clinically or reported by the family. Family history reported by a client who has never met the affected relative is weaker evidence than a documented admission, and the difference should be visible in the record.

### Social and occupational history

Education, employment, housing, income, relationships, caring responsibilities and migration history describe the conditions under which the person functions. They are not background colour; they are the context against which impairment is judged. A client who has stopped working because their employer closed has not necessarily deteriorated in functioning, and a client who continues to work while profoundly depressed may be doing so at unsustainable cost. Asking about the shape of an ordinary week is often the most efficient route into this material.

### Adverse experiences and trauma

Enquiry about adverse experiences should be routine, specific and paced. Routine, because clients rarely volunteer such material unprompted; specific, because "have you experienced trauma?" invites a considered answer about identity rather than a factual one; paced, because detailed trauma enquiry in a first interview can be destabilising when the purpose is assessment rather than treatment. The interviewer asks what happened, when, and whether it is currently affecting sleep, intrusions or avoidance, and defers detailed narrative to a context where it can be contained.

## 2.5|Mental Status Examination

The mental status examination (MSE) is the structured description of the client's current psychological functioning, observed and elicited during the interview. Its conventions derive from clinical psychiatry and have been refined over more than a century; its purpose is to make observable data comparable across examiners and over time. The MSE is not a diagnosis and should not be written as one. It is a record of findings, each of which must be described in terms specific enough to be contested.

### Domain structure

A complete examination covers appearance and behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form, thought content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. Table 2.1 lists the domains, the observations that belong to each, and the descriptive vocabulary conventionally used. The order is flexible, but all domains should be addressed, and negative findings should be recorded as explicitly as positive ones: "no perceptual abnormality elicited" is a finding; silence is not.

### Observation before enquiry

Much of the examination is observational and should be recorded before the client's self-report is reported. A client who describes severe agitation while sitting still for fifty minutes has supplied a discrepancy worth recording. Affect is observed; mood is reported; the two should never be conflated. The examiner records what was seen, what was heard and what was said, in language that distinguishes the three: "reported", "appeared", "described".

### Cognitive screening within the MSE

A brief cognitive screen belongs in every MSE where cognition is relevant to the question: orientation to time, place and person; attention by digit span or serial sevens; registration and delayed recall of three items; and a measure of executive function such as verbal fluency or a clock-drawing task. Screening is not assessment. A poor screening result indicates the need for formal cognitive assessment, and a good result does not exclude significant difficulty in a high-functioning person, particularly where the screen is affected by education, language or culture. Chapter 6 treats formal cognitive assessment in detail.

### Recording and its uses

The MSE should be written in the past tense, in prose, in the third person, and without diagnosis. It should be specific enough that another examiner could compare their own findings on a later occasion, which is the point of a standard vocabulary. Phrases such as "affect appropriate" or "insight poor" should be avoided unless the basis for the judgement is given. The most useful MSE records are those written with an eye to the next assessment rather than only to the present one.

## 2.6|Symptom Chronology and Course

Chronology is the skeleton of diagnosis. Most of the distinctions that matter clinically depend on sequence and duration: whether low mood preceded or followed heavy drinking; whether obsessions appeared before or after the onset of depression; whether attention difficulties were present before the age of twelve; whether a period of elevated mood lasted four days or four weeks. An interview that establishes the presence of symptoms without establishing their temporal relations has collected half the necessary evidence.

### Building a timeline

The most reliable technique is to construct a timeline with the client, working backwards from the present and anchoring each event to something memorable. A sheet of paper with a horizontal line, marked by years and significant events, converts a rambling history into a sequence that both parties can inspect. Discrepancies emerge naturally: the client who dated their low mood to a divorce is found, on the timeline, to have consulted their general practitioner two years earlier.

### Duration and episodicity

Duration matters both for diagnostic thresholds and for prognosis. Interviewers systematically under-estimate the duration of episodes and over-estimate the duration of remissions, because recent symptoms are more memorable than recent wellness. Asking "when was the last week in which you felt like your usual self?" is often more informative than asking about the beginning of the current episode.

### Course and its diagnostic implications

Course describes the trajectory over time: single episode with recovery, recurrent episodes with inter-episode recovery, continuous course, deteriorating course, or fluctuating course without clear episodes. Course is often more discriminating than symptom content. Rapid onset with full recovery and preserved inter-episode functioning supports a different set of explanations from a slow, insidious deterioration with progressive functional decline.

### Reconciling discrepant timelines

Where the client's timeline contradicts the records or an informant, the assessor should not simply prefer one source. Discrepancies usually have explanations: memory decay, shame, different definitions of the problem, or a genuine change in the client's understanding. Recording both accounts and the reason for preferring one is more valuable than silently adopting the more convenient version.

## 2.7|Collateral Information and Records

Collateral information is evidence supplied by someone other than the client, or documented before the current assessment. Its value is that it is independent of the client's current presentation; its limitation is that it is also independent of the client's current experience. Neither source is intrinsically superior, and the assessor's task is to weigh them.

### Obtaining collateral well

Collateral contact requires the client's consent in almost all circumstances, and the client should know what will be asked and what will be shared. Where consent is refused, the assessor can still proceed but should record the refusal and its effect on the confidence of the conclusions. Informants should be briefed on the purpose of the contact, asked for specific observations rather than global judgements, and told what will happen with the information. An informant asked "is he depressed?" will answer with an opinion; an informant asked "what have you noticed about his sleep, appetite and interest in things he used to enjoy over the past two months?" will answer with evidence.

### Records: uses and hazards

Records establish chronology, document prior assessments and reveal previous reasoning, including previous errors. They also transmit error with the authority of print: a diagnosis made in a fifteen-minute consultation and repeated in four subsequent letters acquires spurious solidity. The assessor should distinguish primary documentation from summarised reports, note the source and date of each, and treat an unsupported diagnosis in the records as a hypothesis rather than a finding.

### Weighing conflicting accounts

Where accounts conflict, the assessor examines three things: the opportunity of each source to observe the behaviour in question; the motive each might have for distortion; and the specificity of the description. A partner who has lived with the client for a decade and describes nightly waking at three in the morning is a stronger source on sleep than a client who reports sleeping "fine". A teacher who has known the child for six weeks is a weaker source on developmental history than a parent, though possibly stronger on classroom behaviour.

## 2.8|Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis is the disciplined comparison of competing explanations. It is not the production of a list; it is the identification of the evidence that would separate the items on the list. Performed well, it converts an open-ended interview into a sequence of discriminating questions and gives the eventual report a visible logic.

### Generating the differential

The differential should include, at minimum, the most likely explanation, the most serious explanation that must not be missed, and the explanation the client or referrer considers most likely. Neighbouring categories within the same diagnostic family should be considered explicitly, since these are the errors that occur most often: depressive episode versus persistent depressive disorder versus bipolar depression; generalised anxiety versus panic disorder with agoraphobic avoidance; obsessive-compulsive disorder versus generalised anxiety; post-traumatic stress disorder versus adjustment disorder.

### Discriminating evidence

For each pair of explanations, the assessor identifies the findings that would separate them: age of onset, episodicity, presence of elevated mood, response to previous treatment, family history, substance involvement, and the presence of a defining feature such as obsessions or flashbacks. These discriminating findings become the priority questions for the remainder of the interview, and their absence from the record is the clearest sign that differential reasoning did not take place.

### When the differential cannot be resolved

Some differentials cannot be resolved at a single assessment. Where the discriminating evidence requires observation over time — as it does for bipolar spectrum presentations, for personality difficulties in a young adult, and for the boundary between trauma-related and psychotic phenomena — the correct output is a provisional conclusion with a stated review point, not a forced choice. Recording the unresolved differential, and what would settle it, is the mark of a completed rather than an abandoned assessment.

## 2.9|Medical and Substance-Related Explanations

A substantial minority of presentations that appear psychological at first contact have a medical or substance-related contributor, and a further group has both. The assessor is not expected to practise medicine, but is expected to know which questions to ask, which findings prompt referral, and how medical and substance factors alter the interpretation of psychological data.

### Medical contributors to consider

Conditions that commonly present with psychiatric symptoms include thyroid dysfunction, anaemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, sleep apnoea, chronic pain, diabetes, autoimmune conditions, temporal lobe epilepsy, delirium and early dementia; medications including corticosteroids, beta-blockers, isotretinoin, interferon and some hormonal treatments may also contribute. The interview should establish recent physical investigations, current medication, changes in medication, chronic illness and unexplained physical symptoms. Findings that mandate prompt medical referral include new confusion, focal neurological signs, headache of recent onset with vomiting, unexplained weight loss and first presentation of psychosis in later life.

### Substance use

Substance use is systematically under-detected in routine interviews. Standard practice is to ask about tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, opioids, prescribed medication used outside prescription, and novel psychoactive substances, using a quantity–frequency approach and a validated screen where time allows. The relevant temporal questions are whether use preceded, coincided with or followed the onset of symptoms, and whether there has been a period of abstinence during which symptoms resolved. A presentation that has never been observed in the absence of substance use cannot be attributed confidently to a primary psychological disorder.

### Integrating medical and psychological evidence

Medical and psychological explanations are not competitors in most cases. Chronic illness produces psychological distress; psychological disorder worsens medical outcomes; medication produces symptoms that are then treated as psychiatric. The report should state the medical information available, note what is unknown, and specify what would change the interpretation. Where information is unavailable, the limitation belongs in the conclusions rather than in a footnote.

## 2.10|Interviewing Under Conditions of Distress

Much assessment occurs when the client is distressed, intoxicated, acutely psychotic, in pain, in crisis or in an emergency department at three in the morning. Under these conditions the standard interview must be adapted, and the assessor must decide what can reliably be assessed now and what must wait.

### Acuity and its consequences

Acute distress narrows attention, impairs recall and increases suggestibility. A client in crisis may not be able to give a coherent developmental history, and pressing for one wastes the opportunity to establish safety and engagement. The priority sequence is: immediate safety; physical needs such as pain, hunger, intoxication or withdrawal; orientation and coherence; the presenting crisis; and only then a fuller history, either later in the same contact or at a scheduled follow-up.

### Interviewing clients with psychosis or severe agitation

With psychotic presentations the interviewer adjusts pace, uses shorter sentences, avoids confrontation of delusional content where it is not necessary to the question, and records the content accurately without endorsing it. Agitation calls for environmental measures before verbal ones: reducing noise, ensuring an exit, sitting at an angle, keeping the door open, and involving colleagues where risk is escalating. The interviewer's own arousal is data; noticing fear is a reason to attend to safety rather than to suppress the observation.

### Emergency assessment and the limits of consent

In emergencies, assessment may proceed without full consent where necessary to protect life, and the legal basis will vary by jurisdiction. The assessor should record what was done, on what authority, and what the client was told. Capacity to consent to assessment should be considered rather than assumed, particularly where intoxication, delirium, intellectual disability or acute psychosis is present. A short, documented capacity assessment is preferable to a silent assumption either way.

## 2.11|Structured and Semi-Structured Interviews

Structured and semi-structured diagnostic interviews exist to improve the reliability of diagnosis by fixing the questions, the decision rules and the order of enquiry. Their use is one of the better-evidenced practices in diagnostic assessment, and their neglect in routine practice is one of the more persistent gaps between evidence and service delivery.

### The available formats

Fully structured interviews specify every question and every coding decision, allowing administration by trained interviewers without clinical judgement. Semi-structured interviews specify the domains and the decision rules but leave the wording and the follow-up probing to the clinician. Instruments in common use include the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5; First, Williams, Karg, & Spitzer, 2016), the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (Sheehan et al., 1998) and the ICD-11 based schedules developed for epidemiological and clinical use. Each has a defined training requirement and a defined scope; none assesses everything, and most are weak on personality, risk and functional impairment.

### Strengths and costs

The strengths are reliability, completeness and auditability: a structured interview ensures that criterion-relevant questions are asked and that the answers are recorded in a comparable form. The costs are time, training, rigidity and a tendency to produce diagnosis without formulation. The instruments were designed for research and epidemiological use, and their translation into clinical practice requires the addition of exactly the contextual and functional enquiry that they do not supply.

### Using them well

Used well, a semi-structured interview forms the spine of the diagnostic part of the assessment, with the assessor supplementing it with history, collateral and functional enquiry. Used badly, it becomes a checklist administered without attention, producing a diagnosis that is technically derived and clinically shallow. The difference is visible in the report: a checklist yields a list of criteria met; a well-conducted interview yields criteria met, alternatives excluded, and the reasoning that connects them.

## 2.12|Documenting Diagnostic Reasoning

The interview concludes with a written record. Its purpose is to allow another qualified professional to understand what was asked, what was found, what was concluded and how confident the conclusion should be. Documentation is therefore not an administrative afterthought but the final stage of the reasoning process, and it is often the stage at which weaknesses become visible.

### Structure of the interview record

A workable record contains: the source of referral and the question asked; the client's account of the presenting problem; chronological history; developmental, family, social and medical history; substance use; risk; the mental status examination; the methods used, including any structured interview; a formulation; a differential diagnosis with the evidence for and against each alternative; conclusions with confidence; and recommendations. Each section should distinguish reported information from observed information and from the assessor's inference.

### Recording uncertainty

Uncertainty should be recorded in specific terms. "Diagnosis uncertain" is uninformative. "Criteria for a moderate depressive episode appear to be met; the assessment of duration depends on the client's dating of onset, which conflicts with the general practitioner's record; a bipolar spectrum explanation cannot be excluded on current evidence" tells the reader exactly where the doubt lies and what would resolve it.

### Language, stigma and the client's access

Records are increasingly accessible to clients, and should be written in language that the client could read without harm. Pejorative descriptors, speculation about personality presented as fact, and irrelevant personal detail should be excluded. This is not only an ethical requirement but a clinical one: records written with the client as a potential reader tend to be more precise and less judgemental, and both qualities improve their professional usefulness.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 29-year-old woman is referred by her employer's occupational health service after three months of increasing absence. The stated question is whether she is fit to return to work and whether she has depression.

**The interview.** She describes low mood, early waking, loss of appetite and loss of confidence beginning nine months earlier after a reorganisation placed her under a manager she experiences as humiliating. She has not worked for eleven weeks. The mental status examination shows slowed speech, restricted but reactive affect, no perceptual abnormality, and intact cognition. She describes passive suicidal ideation without intent. She drinks two bottles of wine most evenings, which she dates to the same period; she has not had a drink-free week since the onset. PHQ-9 score is 18.

**The differential.** Major depressive episode; alcohol-induced mood disturbance; adjustment disorder with depressed mood; and an underlying anxiety disorder with secondary avoidance. The discriminating questions concern sequence and abstinence: mood symptoms and drinking began within the same month; she reports no period of abstinence long enough to observe mood; her account of "humiliation" includes panic symptoms in the manager's presence dating from before the reorganisation.

**The reasoning.** The depression is real and severe enough to warrant treatment, and the alcohol use is likely both a consequence and a maintaining factor. The presence of pre-existing panic-like symptoms weakens a pure adjustment explanation and raises the possibility of an anxiety disorder that the reorganisation exacerbated. Fitness for work cannot be determined from severity alone; it depends on the availability of adjustments and on whether the panic can be managed in the specific work setting.

**The decision.** The report records a moderate depressive episode with harmful alcohol use, notes the unresolved question of an underlying anxiety disorder, and recommends treatment for both before any determination of fitness. It specifies workplace adjustments that would be tested on a phased return, and it states that the assessment of fitness will need to be repeated once drinking has reduced and treatment is established. The occupational health service is advised that returning a diagnosis without a functional assessment would not answer the question it asked.

**The teaching point.** The interview established sequence, not just severity. The sequence changed the formulation, and the formulation changed the recommendation.
:::

## Chapter Summary

The clinical interview is a designed instrument. Its structure should follow from the referral question, its time should be budgeted, and its coverage should be fixed in advance so that emotionally salient material does not displace the domains that matter for the decision. The mental status examination converts observation into a standard vocabulary, and its value depends on the examiner distinguishing what was seen from what was reported. Chronology and course are the backbone of differential reasoning: many diagnostic questions are settled by sequence and duration rather than by symptom counts. Medical and substance-related explanations must be considered before a psychological one is adopted, and structured interviews improve reliability while supplying neither formulation nor functional assessment. Documentation is the final act of reasoning, and a record that states its uncertainty precisely is more useful than one that conceals it in confident prose.
