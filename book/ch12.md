# CHAPTER 12|Child and Adolescent Assessment

:::panel Learning Objectives
After working through this chapter, the reader should be able to:
- apply a developmental framework to assessment design and interpretation;
- gather and reconcile information from multiple informants across settings;
- interview children and adolescents in ways that are developmentally appropriate;
- use behavioural observation and school information systematically;
- assess neurodevelopmental presentations without over- or under-diagnosing;
- recognise and respond to safeguarding concerns arising during assessment;
- obtain consent and assent appropriately, and communicate findings to families.
:::

## Introduction

Assessment of children and adolescents differs from adult assessment in ways that go beyond the substitution of child-normed instruments. The child is developing, so a finding that would be stable in an adult may be transient, and a presentation that is atypical now may be typical in two years. The child is embedded in a family and a school, so the relevant environment is larger and the informants are more numerous. The child is legally a minor, so consent, confidentiality and the child's own voice must be managed within a framework that gives parents authority and the child rights.

These differences are not obstacles to assessment; they define it. A child assessment that applies adult methods to a smaller person produces conclusions that are technically derived and developmentally naive. This chapter sets out a framework for assessment that takes development, multiple contexts and family systems seriously, and that gives appropriate weight to the child's own account.

## 12.1|Developmentally Informed Assessment

A developmental framework asks not only what the child is doing but what would be expected at this age, what the trajectory has been, and what the child is likely to do next.

### Normative expectations and their limits

Developmental norms describe the range of typical functioning at a given age, and their use requires attention to the wide variation around the mean. A child who is at the fifteenth percentile for expressive language at three years may be entirely typical, or may be showing the first sign of a language disorder; the difference is established by the trajectory rather than the single point. The assessor should obtain information about earlier functioning wherever possible, because a single assessment cannot distinguish delay from variation.

### Trajectory and change

The most informative developmental question is usually about change: has the child's functioning diverged from the expected trajectory, become more discrepant over time, or lost previously acquired skills? Regression is always significant and warrants urgent assessment. Equally, a difficulty that has been stable and proportionate to the child's developmental level may not require a diagnostic label, only monitoring.

### The developmental context

Development occurs within relationships, and the child's functioning is inseparable from the caregiving environment. Attachment relationships, family stress, parental mental health, housing, poverty and exposure to adversity all shape development and all affect the presentation. The assessor should gather this context not as background but as explanatory information, while avoiding the twin errors of blaming families for difficulties that are neurodevelopmental and of ignoring family factors that are genuinely contributing.

## 12.2|Multi-Informant Assessment

Children behave differently in different settings, and the informants who observe them in each setting provide different information. Discrepancy among informants is the norm rather than the exception, and its pattern is informative.

### Who informs

Parents or carers provide developmental history, family context, and behaviour at home. Teachers provide information about learning, peer relationships and behaviour in a structured group setting. The child provides their own account, which is often the only source for internal experience such as worry, sadness, intrusive thoughts and bullying. Other informants may include grandparents, childminders, sports coaches and, where relevant, social workers and health visitors.

### Interpreting discrepancy

Discrepancies between home and school reports are common in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism, anxiety and trauma-related presentations, and they carry diagnostic weight: a child who is highly symptomatic at home but unremarkable at school may be masking, may be exhausted by the effort of coping, or may be experiencing a difficulty specific to the home environment. The assessor should record each informant's account separately, note the settings in which each observes the child, and then interpret the pattern rather than averaging the reports.

### Structured multi-informant measures

Standardised informant measures, completed by parents and teachers, quantify symptoms and permit comparison with norms. Their scores should be interpreted with attention to the informant's opportunity to observe, their own mental state, and the possibility of rater effects such as a parent's own anxiety inflating ratings of the child's anxiety. Discrepancy indices supplied by some instruments are useful but should be interpreted in the same way.

## 12.3|Parent and Caregiver Interviews

The parent interview is the principal source of developmental history and the principal opportunity to establish a working relationship with the family.

### Content

The interview covers the presenting concern; pregnancy, birth and early development; milestones; health history; family composition and relationships; educational history; peer relationships; the child's strengths and interests; previous assessments and interventions; parental understanding of the difficulty; and the family's goals. It should also cover parental mental and physical health, since these affect both the child's environment and the parent's capacity to participate in treatment.

### Process

The interview should be conducted with sensitivity to the parent's position. Many parents arrive feeling judged, having been told by schools or relatives that they are responsible for the child's difficulties. The assessor should convey that the purpose is understanding rather than blame, elicit the parent's own observations before offering any, and acknowledge the parent's expertise about their child. Where the parent's account is inconsistent with other evidence, the assessor should explore the inconsistency rather than confront it.

### Where parents disagree

Disagreement between parents about the child's difficulties is common and clinically important. It may reflect genuine differences in the child's behaviour with each parent, differences in the parents' own thresholds, or conflict in the parental relationship. The assessor should record both accounts and consider whether the disagreement itself needs to be part of the work.

## 12.4|Interviewing Children and Adolescents

Children are competent witnesses to their own experience, and their accounts are frequently the most valuable information in the assessment. The interviewer's task is to make the conversation possible.

### Developmental adaptation

The method must match the child's age, language and cognitive level. Younger children benefit from play-based methods, drawing, visual scales and concrete questions; adolescents prefer directness, respect for their autonomy and a clear explanation of confidentiality. Questions should be short, specific and free of abstraction. "Tell me about a time you felt worried this week" is more productive than "how would you rate your anxiety".

### Establishing the frame

Children should be told, in terms they can understand, who the assessor is, why they are there, what will happen, and who will be told what. The limits of confidentiality should be explained honestly: that their parents will be given information, but that the assessor will discuss with them first what will be shared, except where there is a safety concern. Children who understand the frame are more likely to disclose.

### Interviewing about sensitive topics

Disclosure of abuse, self-harm, suicidal thoughts and substance use requires a careful approach: open questions first, specific questions only where necessary, no leading questions, and accurate recording of the child's own words. Where a disclosure is made, the assessor should follow safeguarding procedures rather than investigating, and should not promise confidentiality that cannot be kept. The child's account should be recorded verbatim wherever possible, because it may become evidence.

### The adolescent's position

Adolescents often have legitimate concerns about privacy, about being pathologised and about the consequences of disclosure. The assessor should acknowledge these concerns, negotiate what will be shared with parents, and support the young person's increasing autonomy while maintaining safety. Where the young person's wishes and the parents' authority conflict, the assessor's task is to work with both and to be transparent about the limits.

## 12.5|Behavioural Observation

Observation provides information that no informant can supply: the child's behaviour in real time, in interaction with an adult, under the conditions of the assessment.

### Structured and unstructured observation

Structured observation uses standardised tasks that elicit the behaviour of interest, such as a parent–child interaction task or a structured play session. Unstructured observation records the child's spontaneous behaviour during the assessment: how they separate from the parent, how they respond to difficulty, how they manage transitions, how they interact with the assessor, and how they regulate their attention and emotion.

### What to record

Records should be descriptive and behavioural: what the child did, in what context, and with what consequence. Interpretations should be recorded separately and identified as such. The observation of a child who cannot sit still during a twenty-minute task is more informative when it records, alongside the behaviour, the nature of the task, the time of day, the child's sleep the previous night and the assessor's own expectations.

### Limitations

Observation in a clinic is observation in an unusual setting, and children often behave better or worse than usual there. The assessor should treat observation as one source among several and should be cautious about inferring typical behaviour from a single session. Where possible, observation in the natural setting — a home visit, a classroom observation — adds valuable information.

## 12.6|School Records and Teacher Reports

School is the setting in which most children are first identified as having difficulties, and school information is central to assessment.

### Records

School records include attendance, attainment, reports, behaviour logs, special educational provision, and records of exclusions or fixed-term suspensions. They establish the trajectory of the difficulty, the school's previous response, and the child's functioning in a structured group setting. The assessor should request records for the period before the referral as well as the current year, since the trajectory is the most informative element.

### Teacher reports

Teachers observe the child in a setting with specific demands: sustained attention, compliance with group norms, peer interaction and academic performance. Their reports are most useful when they are specific and behavioural rather than global. A structured teacher questionnaire supplies comparability; a brief conversation supplies detail. Where possible, the assessor should speak to more than one teacher, since behaviour varies by subject and by teacher.

### Classroom observation

Where the question concerns attention, behaviour or social participation, a classroom observation can be decisive. It should be conducted systematically, with an agreed focus, a structured recording method and, ideally, a comparison child of similar age. Observations conducted without a structure tend to confirm whatever the observer expected.

## 12.7|Neurodevelopmental Assessment

Neurodevelopmental presentations — autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, developmental language disorder, intellectual disability, specific learning disorders, tic disorders and developmental coordination disorder — are among the most common reasons for child assessment, and they are also among the most frequently misdiagnosed.

### The developmental history

In every case the developmental history is the foundation. The assessor should establish the timing of milestones, the pattern of early social communication, the presence of restricted interests or sensory differences, the trajectory of attention and activity across settings, and the age at which difficulties were first noticed. Where the informant's recall is uncertain, contemporaneous records such as health visitor notes or nursery reports are valuable.

### Assessment methods

Neurodevelopmental assessment combines interview, informant questionnaires, direct observation and, where indicated, standardised instruments such as the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (Lord et al., 2012) for autism or a cognitive assessment for intellectual disability. No single instrument is sufficient, and the assessor should be familiar with the specific training requirements of the instruments used.

### Diagnosis and its consequences

A neurodevelopmental diagnosis has consequences: access to support, explanation for the family, and implications for the child's self-understanding. It should therefore be made carefully, on converging evidence, with attention to differential explanations, and with a clear account of what the diagnosis does and does not mean. Where the evidence is insufficient, the assessor should say so and specify what further information would be needed, rather than making a diagnosis to secure services or withholding one to avoid labelling.

## 12.8|Emotional and Behavioural Problems

The most common presentations in child mental health are emotional and behavioural difficulties: anxiety, depression, oppositional behaviour, aggression, sleep problems, and difficulties following adverse experiences.

### Developmental expression of distress

Children express distress differently from adults. Depression may present as irritability, somatic complaints, school refusal or behavioural regression rather than as reported sadness. Anxiety may present as avoidance, clinginess, aggression or physical symptoms. Trauma may present as dysregulation, hypervigilance, dissociation or re-enactment in play. The assessor must know these developmental expressions, because a literal application of adult criteria will miss the majority of affected children.

### Differential considerations

The differential includes developmental conditions, family and environmental stressors, physical illness, and the effects of adversity. Behaviour that appears oppositional may be a response to an unsuitable classroom, a learning difficulty that makes school humiliating, or a symptom of anxiety. The assessor should generate these explanations and look for the evidence that discriminates them before concluding that the child has a behavioural disorder.

### Assessment of the family context

The child's difficulties occur in a family context, and the assessment should include the family's strengths as well as its difficulties. Parental mental health, parental conflict, sibling relationships, housing and financial stress all affect the child, and all are amenable to intervention. The assessor's report should identify these factors without attributing blame, and should recommend support for the family where it is needed.

## 12.9|Trauma and Safeguarding in Youth Assessment

Children are referred for assessment after adversity more often than adults, and the assessment is frequently the first occasion on which the extent of the adversity becomes known.

### Recognising adversity

The assessor should ask routinely about exposure to domestic abuse, physical and sexual abuse, neglect, parental substance use, parental mental illness, bereavement, separation, bullying, community violence and displacement. Routine enquiry is necessary because children rarely volunteer such information and because parents may not know or may not disclose it.

### Responding to disclosure

Where a child discloses abuse, the assessor's responsibility is to listen, record accurately in the child's words, reassure the child that they are not to blame, explain what will happen next, and follow the safeguarding procedure. The assessor should not investigate, should not promise confidentiality, and should not repeatedly question the child, since repeated questioning can compromise both the child's wellbeing and any subsequent legal process.

### Assessment after trauma

Where trauma is known, the assessment should establish the symptoms, the child's understanding, the current safety, the available support and the child's own coping. Trauma-focused assessment should be paced, should avoid unnecessary detailed re-telling, and should be conducted in a way that does not leave the child distressed and unsupported at the end of the session.

### Safeguarding as an assessment finding

Safeguarding concerns are relevant to the formulation and to the recommendations, even where they are not the reason for referral. The assessor should record them, refer them appropriately, and consider their implications for the child's difficulties. A formulation that omits known adversity is incomplete.

## 12.10|Family and Cultural Context

The family is the child's primary environment, and culture shapes both the family's understanding of the child's difficulties and the meaning of the behaviour.

### Family functioning

Assessment should include family composition, relationships, communication, conflict, discipline practices, and the family's strengths and supports. Where family functioning is a maintaining factor, the formulation should say so and the recommendations should include family-based intervention. Where the family is a source of support, the formulation should identify this as a resource to be mobilised.

### Cultural context

Cultural expectations shape what is considered typical for a child's age, what behaviour is considered problematic, and what help is acceptable. In some communities, expectations of independence, obedience or academic achievement differ markedly from those of the assessor's own background, and behaviour that appears disordered against one set of expectations may be unremarkable against another. The assessor should ask about the family's expectations and their own understanding of the difficulty, and should be cautious about diagnosing a disorder on the basis of a cultural mismatch.

### Language and interpretation

Where the family's first language differs from the language of assessment, the assessor should use a trained interpreter, should establish the child's language proficiency in both languages, and should be cautious about assessing the child in a language in which they are not fluent. Chapter 14 treats this in detail.

## 12.11|Consent, Assent and Confidentiality

Children cannot consent to assessment in most jurisdictions, but they have rights that the assessor must protect.

### Parental consent

Consent is normally obtained from a person with parental responsibility. The assessor should explain the purpose, the methods, the likely duration, the use of the findings and the limits of confidentiality, and should record the consent obtained. Where parents disagree, or where the child is looked after by the state, the legal position should be clarified before the assessment proceeds.

### The child's assent

Assent is the child's agreement to participate, given in a form appropriate to their understanding. It is not a legal substitute for parental consent, but it is ethically essential: a child who does not assent should not be assessed, except where the assessment is necessary to protect them. The assessor should explain the process in accessible language, check understanding, and respect refusal where possible.

### Confidentiality and its limits

Children and adolescents need to know what will be shared with their parents, and the assessor should negotiate this explicitly. A workable approach is to agree that the assessor will share the main findings with parents, will discuss the content of that feedback with the young person first, and will not disclose specific sensitive information unless there is a safety concern. This approach respects the young person's privacy while maintaining the parents' legitimate involvement.

## 12.12|Integrated Youth Formulation and Feedback

The assessment concludes with a formulation that integrates developmental history, multiple informants, observation, school information and the child's own account.

### Structure of the formulation

A youth formulation should describe the child's developmental trajectory, the current difficulties and their functional impact, the maintaining factors across home, school and peer contexts, the child's strengths and resources, and the family and cultural context. It should identify the points at which intervention is likely to be effective, and it should be written in language that parents and, where appropriate, the child can understand.

### Feedback to parents

Feedback should begin with the child's strengths, present the findings in plain language, explain the diagnosis or formulation and what it means, invite questions, and connect the findings to specific recommendations. Where the findings are unwelcome — a diagnosis of autism, a recommendation for special provision, a safeguarding referral — the assessor should allow time, acknowledge the emotional response, and offer follow-up.

### Feedback to the child

Children should receive feedback appropriate to their age, in language they can understand, with an emphasis on what is helpful rather than on what is wrong. A child who is told that their brain works differently and that there are things that will help is better served than one who is told they have a disorder and left to imagine what that means.

### Feedback to school

Where the school is involved, feedback should be practical: what the child needs, what has been shown to help, and what the school can do. Recommendations should be specific enough to be implemented, and the school's constraints should be acknowledged. A report that recommends provision the school cannot make is unlikely to change anything.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** An 8-year-old boy is referred by his general practitioner at his mother's request. The school has reported that he is "not coping" and has suggested he may have attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. His mother reports that he is "fine at home" but that he cries before school and has started refusing to go.

**The evidence.** The developmental history is unremarkable: milestones were met on time, early social communication was typical, and there were no concerns before starting school. Teacher reports describe inattention and frequent trips to the toilet during lessons; a structured teacher questionnaire is elevated on attention problems. A parent questionnaire on the same instrument is within normal limits. Observation shows a quiet, cooperative boy who is anxious on separation from his mother and who asks repeatedly whether he is doing the task correctly. Cognitive assessment is in the average range. On direct enquiry he describes being teased about his reading and says he "feels sick" in reading lessons. A reading assessment shows word-level reading well below the level expected for his age, with average comprehension when the text is read to him.

**The reasoning.** The teacher-reported attention problems and the school's hypothesis of ADHD are plausible but not supported by the full picture. The onset of difficulties coincided with the introduction of formal reading, the behaviour is situation-specific, and the child's own account identifies reading lessons as the source of distress. The apparent inattention is consistent with avoidance of a task he finds humiliating, and the trips to the toilet are consistent with escape behaviour. The discrepancy between home and school reports is explained by the fact that reading demands occur only at school.

**The decision.** The report identifies a specific word-reading difficulty, with secondary anxiety and school avoidance, and declines to diagnose ADHD on the current evidence while noting that the question could be revisited if difficulties persisted after reading support. It recommends a structured phonics intervention, reasonable adjustments in reading lessons, and a graded return-to-school plan with the school. It recommends that the child's anxiety be monitored and treated if it does not reduce once the reading difficulty is addressed.

**The teaching point.** The referral question was ADHD; the assessment found a reading difficulty and an understandable avoidance. The multi-informant discrepancy was the clue, and the child's own account supplied the explanation.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Child and adolescent assessment requires a developmental framework that considers trajectory, normative variation and the caregiving environment. Multiple informants are essential, and discrepancy among them is informative rather than problematic. The parent interview supplies developmental history and the opportunity to build a working relationship, while the child's own account supplies information available from no other source. Observation and school information add the contexts in which the child actually functions. Neurodevelopmental assessment rests on developmental history and converging evidence, and diagnoses should be made on evidence rather than to secure services. Emotional and behavioural difficulties present developmentally, and the differential must include learning difficulties, family context and adversity. Safeguarding concerns arising during assessment require immediate procedural response. Consent, assent and confidentiality must be managed within a framework that respects both parental authority and the child's rights.
