# CHAPTER 9|Assessment of Bipolar, Psychotic and Other Severe Mental Disorders

:::panel Learning Objectives
After working through this chapter, the reader should be able to:
- recognise manic and hypomanic presentations and distinguish them from other elevated states;
- assess psychotic experiences systematically across sensory modality, content and form;
- evaluate thought form and thought content without conflating observation with inference;
- distinguish negative symptoms from depression, demoralisation and medication effects;
- appraise developmental and cultural explanations for unusual experiences;
- assess insight, judgement and decision-making capacity;
- communicate findings in severe presentations with appropriate attention to risk and uncertainty.

## Introduction

Severe mental disorders present assessment challenges of a different order from those discussed in the previous chapter. Symptoms may be subtle, intermittently present, concealed or denied; the client may lack insight into the very experiences the assessor needs to describe; informants may be unavailable or may themselves be distressed; and the consequences of error — involuntary treatment, loss of liberty, misattributed psychosis — are substantial.

Assessment in this group is also where the distinction between description and inference is most consequential. "The client reports that the television is sending messages" is an observation. "The client has delusions of reference" is an inference, and it requires evidence about conviction, cultural explanations, context and the client's own understanding. This chapter develops the observational discipline that severe presentations demand, together with the reasoning required to place observations within a differential that includes medical, substance-related and cultural explanations. ## 9.1|Recognising Manic and Hypomanic Presentations

Mania and hypomania are defined by a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood, accompanied by increased energy and goal-directed activity, lasting at least one week for mania and at least four consecutive days for hypomania. Mania causes marked impairment, requires hospitalisation or includes psychotic features; hypomania does not.

### The clinical features

The features to establish are elevated or irritable mood; inflated self-esteem or grandiosity; decreased need for sleep; pressured speech; flight of ideas or subjective experience of racing thoughts; distractibility; increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation; and excessive involvement in activities with high potential for painful consequences. The assessor should establish the presence of each, its duration, its departure from the person's usual functioning and its consequences.

### The diagnostic difficulty

Two difficulties dominate. The first is retrospective recall: clients in a euthymic state frequently do not recall elevated periods accurately, and may describe them as their normal self. Informant report and contemporaneous records are therefore essential, and prospective mood charting is often the only reliable method. The second is the boundary with normality: increased energy and reduced sleep in the context of a new relationship, a creative project or a stimulant are not necessarily hypomania, and the distinction depends on departure from the person's baseline, persistence and consequences.

### Bipolar spectrum and its mimics

The differential includes bipolar I disorder, bipolar II disorder, cyclothymia, substance-induced mood disorder, mood disorder due to another medical condition, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, borderline personality disorder and major depression with agitated features. Antidepressant-induced hypomania is a recognised phenomenon and should be recorded where it occurs, since it alters the treatment implications substantially.

## 9.2|Assessing Psychotic Experiences

Psychosis is a descriptive term for experiences such as hallucinations, delusions and disorganised thinking that indicate a loss of contact with consensual reality. Assessment requires systematic description of the experience rather than classification by label.

### Hallucinations

The assessor establishes the modality (auditory, visual, tactile, olfactory, gustatory), the content, the location (inside or outside the head), the person's relationship to the voice or image, the degree of conviction, the frequency and the circumstances in which it occurs. Auditory verbal hallucinations are the most common in psychotic disorders; visual hallucinations are more often associated with medical and neurological conditions, substance use or delirium, and should prompt medical review.

### Delusions

A delusion is a fixed false belief that is not amenable to change in the light of conflicting evidence and is not explicable by the person's cultural context. The assessor records the content, the onset, the degree of conviction, the extent to which the belief preoccupies the person, the emotional response to it, and the behaviours that follow from it. Beliefs should be recorded in the person's own words before being characterised.

### Distinguishing psychotic from non-psychotic unusual experiences

Many people report unusual experiences without disorder: hearing one's name when falling asleep, sensing a presence, holding strongly held beliefs shared within a community. The discriminating features are frequency, distress, impairment, conviction despite disconfirmation, and the extent to which the experiences dominate the person's life. The assessor should also consider dissociation, obsessive-compulsive phenomena with poor insight, and culturally normative experiences such as contact with the deceased in the context of bereavement.

## 9.3|Thought Form and Thought Content

Thought form concerns the structure and flow of speech and writing; thought content concerns what is thought about. The distinction is central to the assessment of severe presentations and is frequently blurred in reports.

### Disorders of thought form

Thought form is assessed by observation of speech: rate, quantity, continuity, logical connection and goal direction. Descriptors include circumstantiality (over-inclusive but ultimately goal-directed), tangentiality (departing from the topic without returning), loosening of associations (connections between ideas that the listener cannot follow), derailment, incoherence or word salad, neologisms, clanging and perseveration. Each should be illustrated with a brief quoted example, since the descriptors are used inconsistently across examiners.

### Disorders of thought content

Thought content includes delusions, overvalued ideas, obsessions, preoccupations and suicidal or homicidal ideation. The distinction between a delusion and an overvalued idea is a matter of degree of conviction and resistance to evidence, and the assessor should record the degree rather than assigning a category at the boundary. Somatic, persecutory, grandiose, referential, religious, erotomanic, jealous and nihilistic contents each carry different differential implications.

### Recording accurately

The record should distinguish observation from inference explicitly. Quotation is the most reliable method: "He stated that his neighbours had installed equipment that 'reads my thoughts through the wall'" is more informative than "persecutory delusions". Where a quotation would identify the client or breach confidentiality, a close paraphrase should be used and marked as such.

## 9.4|Negative Symptoms and Functional Decline

Negative symptoms — blunted affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia and asociality — predict functional outcome in psychotic disorders more strongly than positive symptoms do. They are also easily confused with depression, with medication effects and with demoralisation.

### The distinction from depression

The overlap is substantial: both involve reduced motivation, reduced pleasure and social withdrawal. The discriminating features include the presence of depressed mood and guilt in depression, the presence of hopelessness and suicidality in depression, and the relative preservation of anticipatory pleasure in some negative symptom presentations. Structured instruments for negative symptoms improve reliability, but the distinction often requires longitudinal observation.

### The distinction from medication effects

Antipsychotic medications, particularly at higher doses, produce parkinsonism, akathisia and sedation that mimic negative symptoms. The assessor should review the medication regimen, look for extrapyramidal signs, and consider whether the symptoms appeared or worsened after a dose change. A rating of negative symptoms obtained without this review is unreliable.

### Functional decline

Functional decline is often the most consequential feature of severe disorders and the least well assessed. The assessor should establish the person's premorbid level of functioning, the trajectory of decline, the domains affected (work, relationships, self-care, independent living) and the extent to which the decline is attributable to symptoms, to medication, to stigma and lost opportunity, or to the illness's effects on motivation. This distinction matters because the interventions differ.

## 9.5|Mood Disorders with Psychotic Features

Psychotic symptoms occurring in the context of a mood episode raise the differential between psychotic disorders with mood symptoms, mood disorders with psychotic features and schizoaffective disorder. The distinction rests on the temporal relationship between mood and psychotic symptoms.

### The temporal rule

Where psychotic symptoms occur exclusively during mood episodes, a mood disorder with psychotic features is the more parsimonious diagnosis. Where psychotic symptoms persist for at least two weeks in the absence of a mood episode, the balance shifts towards a primary psychotic disorder. Where mood episodes are present for the majority of the total duration of illness and psychotic symptoms occur in their absence, schizoaffective disorder may be considered. These rules are applied to the longitudinal course, not to a single cross-section.

### The assessment implication

The implication is that a single assessment cannot usually resolve the differential. The assessor should obtain a longitudinal history from the client and an informant, review records, and construct a timeline of mood and psychotic symptoms across the illness. Where the timeline cannot be established, the correct output is a provisional diagnosis with a stated review point.

### Treatment implications

The distinction matters for treatment: mood stabilisers, antidepressants and antipsychotics are used differently, and the risk of antidepressant-induced mania or of relapse differs. The assessor's report should therefore state the differential explicitly and indicate what evidence would resolve it, rather than recording a single diagnosis that implies a certainty the evidence does not support.

## 9.6|Substance-Induced and Medical Presentations

Substance use and medical conditions are among the most common causes of psychotic symptoms, and excluding them is a prerequisite for diagnosing a primary psychotic disorder.

### Substance-induced psychosis

Stimulants, cannabis, hallucinogens, alcohol withdrawal and novel psychoactive substances can all produce psychotic symptoms. The assessor should establish the substance, the dose, the timing relative to symptom onset, the duration of symptoms after cessation, and whether previous episodes occurred in the absence of substance use. A first episode of psychosis in a heavy cannabis user requires a period of abstinence before a primary diagnosis can be made with confidence.

### Medical causes

Delirium, temporal lobe epilepsy, autoimmune encephalitis, thyroid disease, vitamin B12 deficiency, Wilson's disease, HIV-related illness, neurosyphilis, brain tumours and prescribed medications can all produce psychotic symptoms. Certain features should prompt urgent medical referral: first episode in later life, fluctuating consciousness, focal neurological signs, visual or tactile hallucinations, and rapid deterioration. The assessor should not manage these presentations alone.

### Recording the exclusion

The report should state what has been excluded, on what evidence and by whom. "Medical causes were considered; thyroid function and B12 were normal on testing reported by the general practitioner; no neuroimaging has been performed" is informative. Silence on the matter is not.

## 9.7|Developmental and Cultural Explanations

Unusual experiences occur in the context of developmental conditions and cultural frameworks, and both must be considered before a psychotic disorder is diagnosed.

### Autism and unusual experiences

Autistic adults may report sensory experiences, idiosyncratic beliefs and difficulty distinguishing intended meaning that are misread as psychotic. The assessor should consider developmental history, the lifelong pattern of social communication differences, and the person's own account of their experience. Misattributing autism to psychosis is a well-recognised source of harm, particularly for people who have not previously been assessed.

### Cultural and religious frameworks

Beliefs that would be delusional in one cultural context may be normative in another: spiritual possession, communication with ancestors, belief in the evil eye, and experiences interpreted within religious practice. The assessor must establish whether the belief is shared within the person's community, whether it is consistent with the person's cultural background, and whether it causes distress or impairment. The DSM-5-TR cultural formulation interview provides a structured method for this enquiry, and its use is appropriate whenever the assessor's cultural knowledge is limited.

### The limits of cultural explanation

Cultural explanation should not be used to dismiss genuine psychopathology, nor should psychopathology be diagnosed because a belief is unfamiliar to the assessor. The appropriate procedure is to generate both explanations, to seek information from the client's community where possible, and to record the reasoning. Consultation with a cultural informant or a clinician familiar with the client's background is often the most efficient way to resolve the question.

## 9.8|Collateral History and Longitudinal Evidence

Severe disorders are chronic conditions whose diagnosis depends on course. A single interview, however thorough, samples a moment; the diagnosis requires a trajectory. Collateral history and longitudinal evidence are therefore not optional supplements but core methods.

### Sources

Informants include partners, parents, siblings, friends, employers, landlords and clinicians who have known the person over time. Records include psychiatric admissions, general practice notes, school reports, occupational records and criminal justice records where lawful and relevant. Each source has its own perspective and its own limits, and the assessor should record who provided what and when.

### Constructing a longitudinal timeline

The timeline should record the onset of each symptom domain, the pattern of episodes, the response to treatment, the level of functioning between episodes, and the occurrence of any periods of remission. This timeline is the single most useful document in the assessment of a severe disorder, and constructing it with the client and an informant is often more informative than any instrument.

### When collateral is unavailable

Where collateral is refused or unavailable, the assessor should record the fact and its implications for confidence. In some circumstances — particularly where the client's account is the only evidence — the report should say so explicitly and should state what conclusions are and are not supported.

## 9.9|Insight, Judgement and Decision-Making Capacity

Insight and capacity are related but distinct concepts. Insight refers to the person's awareness and understanding of their condition; capacity refers to their ability to make a specific decision at a specific time. Confusing the two leads to the erroneous conclusion that a person who lacks insight into their illness also lacks capacity to make treatment decisions.

### Assessing insight

Insight is best assessed dimensionally: awareness of symptoms, attribution of symptoms to a condition, awareness of the need for treatment, and awareness of the likely consequences of non-treatment. Each dimension can be rated separately, and the pattern is often informative — a person may accept that they have an illness but reject medication, or deny the illness while complying with treatment for other reasons.

### Assessing capacity

Capacity assessment follows a functional test defined by the relevant jurisdiction, typically requiring that the person can understand the relevant information, retain it, weigh it in the decision, and communicate their decision. The assessor should use the statutory framework where one exists, record the specific decision being assessed, present information in accessible form, and repeat the assessment if the person's condition fluctuates. A general statement that the client "lacks capacity" is uninformative and usually wrong.

### The interface with risk

Where capacity is impaired and risk is significant, the legal framework for involuntary intervention will apply. The assessor's role is to provide the evidence on which that decision is made, not to make it. Documentation should distinguish the clinical findings from the legal determination.

## 9.10|Risk and Immediate Safety Needs

Risk assessment in severe presentations requires attention to suicide, self-neglect, violence, exploitation and vulnerability, each of which is treated in Chapter 15. The present section addresses the specific features of severe presentations that alter risk.

### Elevated risk in psychotic and manic states

Risk is elevated during acute psychosis, particularly where command hallucinations, persecutory beliefs involving specific individuals, or disorganised behaviour are present. Mania carries risk of harm through impulsive behaviour, financial ruin, sexual risk-taking and accidents. Depression with psychotic features carries particularly high suicide risk, especially where the person believes they deserve punishment or that their family would be better off without them.

### Assessment tasks

The assessor should establish ideation, intent, plan, means, previous attempts, protective factors, the person's beliefs about the future, and the availability of support. Where psychosis is present, the assessor should ask directly whether the voices or beliefs involve harming self or others, and whether the person feels they must comply. Where risk is elevated, the assessment should include a safety plan and clear arrangements for follow-up.

### Documentation

Risk documentation should record what was asked, what was disclosed, what was observed, the assessor's judgement and the reasoning, the actions taken and the communication with other parties. It should be specific enough to withstand later scrutiny and clear enough to be acted upon by a colleague who reads it in an emergency.

## 9.11|Diagnostic Uncertainty and Provisional Conclusions

Severe presentations frequently generate diagnostic uncertainty that cannot be resolved at assessment. The professional response is not to select the most plausible category and present it as settled, but to record the uncertainty in usable form.

### Forms of uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern the presence of a symptom (was that a hallucination or an intrusive thought?), the temporal relationship between symptom domains, the contribution of substances, the boundary with a personality disorder or a developmental condition, or the trajectory of the illness. Each form requires a different kind of evidence to resolve, and the report should specify which.

### Provisional diagnoses

A provisional diagnosis should be stated as such, with the evidence supporting it, the alternatives considered, and the observations that would change it. This is not a failure of confidence but an accurate description of the state of the evidence, and it is more useful to the treating team than a premature certainty that later has to be reversed — a reversal that damages the client's trust and the assessor's credibility.

### Review points

Every provisional conclusion should carry a review point: a date or a clinical event at which the diagnosis will be reconsidered. In early psychosis services, where diagnostic stability is low, this practice is standard; it is equally appropriate in general services.

## 9.12|Communicating Findings in Severe Presentations

Communication in severe presentations carries additional weight: the report may inform decisions about liberty, treatment and parenting, and may be read by the client at a time of considerable vulnerability.

### Language and stigma

Reports should describe behaviour and experience rather than using diagnostic labels as adjectives for the person. "He described hearing a voice commenting on his actions" is preferable to "he is psychotic", both because it is more informative and because it is less stigmatising. Terms such as "manipulative", "attention-seeking" and "non-compliant" should be avoided unless the behaviour is described and the inference justified.

### Communication with the client

Feedback to a person in an acute episode must be adapted: shorter, concrete, checked for comprehension and repeated later. Where the client disagrees with the conclusions, the disagreement should be recorded rather than overridden, and the client's own account should appear in the report. This is both an ethical requirement and a source of information; clients often identify errors in the factual record.

### Communication with other professionals

Reports should specify what the receiving team should do, what should be monitored, and what would constitute a deterioration. The most useful reports in severe presentations are those that a duty clinician can act on at two in the morning: clear, specific and prioritised.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 23-year-old man is brought to a crisis service by his parents. He has stopped attending university, is sleeping three hours a night and has told his parents that his neighbours are "testing a device" on him. The question is whether he should be admitted.

**The assessment.** He is cooperative but guarded. Speech is rapid and pressured; he describes his thoughts as "moving faster than I can speak". He reports hearing a man's voice commenting on his behaviour, which he locates outside his head and attributes to the neighbours' device. He denies any mood elevation when asked directly, but his parents describe three weeks of unusually elevated mood, increased spending and uncharacteristic irritability, and report that he has not slept more than four hours in that period. He has used cannabis weekly for two years but not in the past month. He denies suicidal ideation and any intention to harm his neighbours, though he says he has considered "confronting them". He does not believe he is unwell and does not want treatment.

**The reasoning.** The presentation includes both psychotic and manic features. The temporal relationship matters: the elevated mood and reduced need for sleep preceded the persecutory beliefs by approximately two weeks. If the mood features are primary, a manic episode with psychotic features is the more parsimonious diagnosis; if the psychosis persists beyond the mood episode, the differential would broaden. Substance contribution is possible but he reports a month of abstinence, which reduces its likelihood without excluding it. Medical causes have not been investigated.

**The decision.** The report records a provisional diagnosis of a manic episode with psychotic features, recommends admission for assessment and treatment, and specifies that medical investigation should include thyroid function, a toxicology screen and neuroimaging if clinically indicated. It records that capacity to consent to treatment is impaired for the specific decision of accepting admission, with the reasoning, and that the statutory framework for involuntary assessment has been invoked. It notes that the diagnosis should be reviewed once the acute episode has resolved and once a period of abstinence has been confirmed.

**The teaching point.** The assessment did not settle the diagnosis, and saying so was correct. What it did settle was the immediate decision, the framework for it, and the observations that would resolve the diagnostic question later.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Mania and hypomania are defined by episode duration, departure from baseline and consequences, and their assessment depends on informant report and longitudinal evidence. Psychotic experiences should be described before they are classified, with attention to modality, content, conviction and context. Thought form and thought content are distinct, and both are best recorded with examples. Negative symptoms must be distinguished from depression, demoralisation and medication effects. Mood–psychosis timing determines the differential among mood disorders with psychotic features, primary psychotic disorders and schizoaffective disorder, and usually requires longitudinal data. Substance and medical causes must be excluded and the exclusions recorded. Developmental and cultural explanations must be considered without being used to dismiss genuine disorder. Insight and capacity are distinct, and capacity is decision-specific. Uncertainty should be documented as provisional conclusions with review points, and communication should describe experience rather than label persons.
