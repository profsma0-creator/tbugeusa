# CHAPTER 13|Couples, Family and Systemic Assessment

:::panel Learning Objectives
After working through this chapter, the reader should be able to:
- identify the appropriate unit of assessment in relational work;
- gather relationship history and present concerns from multiple participants;
- assess dyadic interaction, communication and conflict patterns;
- evaluate attachment and relationship security in a systemic frame;
- assess family structure, boundaries and parenting without allocating blame;
- screen for violence, coercion and safety before relational work begins;
- formulate systemically and deliver feedback that involves all participants.

## Introduction

Relational assessment asks different questions from individual assessment. The object of assessment is not a person's internal state but a pattern of interaction between people, and the relevant evidence is what happens between them rather than what each reports about themselves. This shift in the unit of analysis changes the methods, the ethics and the risks.

The risks deserve particular attention. Relational assessment may bring to light violence, abuse or coercive control that was not disclosed in individual sessions; it may place a participant at risk by inviting disclosure in the presence of a partner; and it may be used, consciously or not, by one participant to establish that the other is the problem. The assessor's first obligations are therefore safety screening and clarity about the purpose and the limits of the assessment. This chapter sets out a framework for relational assessment that is both systematic and safe.

## 13.1|The Unit of Assessment in Relational Work

The first decision in relational assessment is what is being assessed: an individual within a relationship, a couple, a family, or a wider system. Each unit requires a different method and produces different conclusions.

### Individual within relationship

Where the referral concerns one person but the difficulty is relational, the assessment may focus on the person's experience of the relationship, their contribution to the pattern, and their options. This is common where a partner declines to attend. The assessor should be explicit that the resulting formulation is one-sided by design, and should avoid conclusions about the absent partner that cannot be evidenced.

### Couple and family

Where the unit is the couple or the family, assessment requires the participation of all relevant members, ideally in a combination of joint and individual sessions. Joint sessions reveal interaction; individual sessions allow disclosure that cannot occur in front of others. The balance between the two is a clinical decision, and the arrangements should be agreed at the outset with all participants.

### Wider systems

Some presentations require assessment of wider systems: the family and school together, a family and a social care team, or a multi-agency network around a child or an adult at risk. Systemic assessment then includes the relationships among professionals, the circulation of information, and the way the network itself may be maintaining the difficulty. This is demanding work and requires explicit agreement about roles and confidentiality.

## 13.2|Relationship History and Presenting Concerns

The relationship history provides the context against which present difficulties are understood. It should be gathered in a way that gives each participant a voice and that records disagreement rather than resolving it prematurely.

### Content

The history includes how the couple met, the early phase of the relationship, significant transitions (cohabitation, marriage, children, migration, illness, bereavement, retirement), previous difficulties and how they were resolved, previous help-seeking, and each partner's hopes for the relationship. Where children are present, the parenting history and the children's needs are part of the picture.

### Presenting concerns

Each participant should be asked separately what they see as the problem, what they want to change, and what they fear would happen if nothing changed. The differences among these accounts are the material for the assessment, and the assessor should not attempt to reconcile them at the first session.

### The referral relationship

The assessor should establish who initiated the referral, who is attending willingly, and whether there is any external pressure — from a court, a social worker, a family member. Where one partner attends under duress, the assessment will be affected, and the assessor should acknowledge this openly rather than pretending to a neutrality that the participants do not share.

## 13.3|Dyadic Interaction and Communication

Observing how a couple communicates in the session is often more informative than anything they say about their communication.

### What to observe

The assessor attends to turn-taking, interruption, escalation, repair, the ratio of positive to negative exchanges, non-verbal signals, and the way each partner responds when the other expresses distress. These observations should be recorded descriptively, without interpretation, and should be treated as data rather than as evidence of a partner's character.

### Demand–withdraw and negative reciprocity

Two patterns recur across the relational literature. The demand–withdraw pattern describes a cycle in which one partner pursues engagement while the other withdraws, and each responds to the other in a way that intensifies the pattern. Negative reciprocity describes the escalation of negative responses, in which hostility from one partner elicits hostility from the other and the exchange spirals. Both patterns are self-maintaining, and both are more usefully formulated as properties of the system than as deficits of either person.

### Repair

The most discriminating feature of distressed and non-distressed relationships is not the frequency of conflict but the capacity for repair. The assessor should observe whether conflict is followed by reconnection, whether humour or apology is available, and whether either partner can de-escalate. Where repair is absent, the prognosis for the relationship is poorer, and the assessment should note this without using it as a prediction about the couple's future.

## 13.4|Conflict Patterns and Repair

Conflict is not itself a sign of dysfunction. The assessment concerns the pattern of conflict: what it is about, how it starts, how it escalates, how it ends, and what it leaves behind.

### Mapping the conflict

The assessor should map a recent, specific conflict from beginning to end: the trigger, each partner's interpretation, the behaviour that followed, the escalation, the ending, and the aftermath. A specific episode is more informative than general descriptions, which tend to be shaped by each partner's global narrative about the other.

### Underlying issues

Conflicts about practical matters frequently encode conflicts about recognition, autonomy, fairness or belonging. A dispute about the division of household labour may be about exhaustion, about being taken for granted, or about a partner's relationship with their family of origin. The assessor should explore what the conflict means to each partner rather than treating the surface topic as the issue.

### Repair and its conditions

Repair requires that both partners can recognise the other's perspective, that at least one can de-escalate, and that there is a shared commitment to the relationship's continuation. Where these conditions are absent, the assessment should consider whether the couple's goal is to improve the relationship or to manage its ending, since the two require different work.

## 13.5|Attachment and Relationship Security

Attachment theory provides a framework for understanding how partners' expectations about availability and responsiveness shape their behaviour in the relationship.

### Attachment in the couple

Partners differ in their tolerance of closeness and distance, in their response to conflict, and in their expectations of the other's availability. These differences often produce the demand–withdraw cycle: an anxiously attached partner pursues, an avoidantly attached partner withdraws, and each confirms the other's expectation. The assessment should identify these patterns without assigning either partner a diagnostic label.

### Assessment methods

Attachment security in couples can be assessed by interview, by self-report questionnaires measuring attachment anxiety and avoidance, and by observation of the couple's interaction, particularly during a discussion of a disagreement. Self-report and observational methods assess different things and may disagree; the assessor should use both where possible and interpret the disagreement rather than choosing one.

### Caution

Attachment language is easily used to explain everything and to absolve anyone. The assessor should use it as a framework for understanding patterns rather than as a diagnosis of a partner, and should avoid the inference that a partner's attachment style means they cannot change. Where the pattern is used to justify harmful behaviour, the assessor should say so plainly.

## 13.6|Family Structure and Boundaries

Family assessment examines the organisation of the family: its structure, its boundaries, its hierarchy of authority and the alliances within it.

### Structure and boundaries

Boundaries may be diffuse, with insufficient separation between members and a loss of individual identity, or rigid, with insufficient communication and support. Healthy functioning requires boundaries that permit both closeness and autonomy, and that can change in response to developmental transitions such as a child entering adolescence or an adult child leaving home.

### Coalitions and triangulation

Coalitions occur when family members align against another member, and triangulation occurs when a two-person conflict is displaced onto a third. A child may be drawn into the parents' conflict, a grandparent may be recruited into a dispute, or a child's symptoms may serve to hold the family together. These patterns are often invisible in individual assessment and visible in family sessions.

### Authority and discipline

Where children are present, the assessment examines who holds authority, whether the adults are consistent with each other, and whether discipline is proportionate and safe. Parental disagreement about discipline is extremely common and often reflects a difference in temperament or in the parents' own upbringing; the assessment should identify it and, where appropriate, include parenting work in the recommendations.

## 13.7|Parenting and Co-Parenting

Parenting and co-parenting are distinct constructs. Parenting concerns the interaction between a parent and a child; co-parenting concerns the way the adults in a child's life co-ordinate their parenting, whether they live together or apart.

### Parenting assessment

The assessment of parenting includes the parent's capacity to recognise and respond to the child's needs, to provide appropriate structure and warmth, and to manage the child's behaviour without physical or emotional harm. Where the question is about parenting capacity — often in the context of child protection proceedings — the assessment requires direct observation of interaction, a parenting assessment instrument, and information about the parent's own history and current circumstances.

### Co-parenting after separation

Where parents are separated, co-parenting assessment concerns the quality of communication between them, the consistency of routines, the child's exposure to conflict, and the parents' support for the child's relationship with the other parent. A structured co-parenting programme or mediation may be more appropriate than psychological intervention, and the assessor should know when to recommend it.

### Parenting and the assessing system

Where an assessment is commissioned by a court or a social care team, the assessor should be clear about the question, the standard of evidence, the limits of the assessment and the potential consequences for the parent and the child. The parent should understand these from the outset. Assessment of parenting capacity is a specialised area, and it lies beyond the competence of general practice unless the assessor has specific training and supervision.

## 13.8|Violence, Coercion and Safety Screening

Safety screening is the first substantive assessment task in relational work, and it must precede any joint session or relational formulation.

### Why routine screening

Violence and coercive control occur in a substantial minority of relationships, and they are frequently not disclosed. The risk is concentrated where assessment involves joint sessions, because a partner may be at risk from disclosure, and because conflict in the session may escalate outside it. Routine screening, conducted with each participant individually at the outset, is therefore a standard of safe practice.

### Screening questions

Screening should be direct and specific: questions about fear, about physical violence, about threats, about economic and sexual control, about threats related to children or immigration status, and about escalation. A structured instrument such as the Relationship Assessment Tool or the HITS screening tool supports reliability, but the assessor's own direct enquiry matters more than the instrument.

### What follows a disclosure

Where violence or coercive control is disclosed, the assessment plan must change. Joint sessions are contraindicated where one partner is violent or controlling; the safety of the victim is the first priority; specialist domestic abuse services should be involved with consent where possible and without consent where required by law; and the assessor should record the disclosure, the actions taken, and the reasoning. The safety of any children in the household must be considered.

### The limits of couple therapy

Couple therapy is not appropriate where there is violence, coercion, active untreated substance dependence, untreated psychosis, or where one partner is being controlled. This is not a moral judgement but a safety and effectiveness judgement, and it should be recorded explicitly when a decision against relational work is made.

## 13.9|Individual Symptoms in Relational Context

Individual symptoms — depression, anxiety, substance use, chronic illness, neurodevelopmental conditions — occur within relationships and are affected by them. The assessment should capture that interaction without over-attributing the symptom to the relationship.

### Circular causality

The relationship between symptoms and relational patterns is usually circular: a partner's depression reduces engagement, which the other experiences as rejection, which leads to criticism, which worsens the depression. Formulating the pattern in this way is more useful than determining who started it, and it identifies multiple points for intervention.

### Avoiding blame

The risk in systemic formulation is that the identification of patterns is experienced as allocation of blame. The assessor should be explicit that the patterns described are not the fault of any individual, while also being honest where one person's behaviour is harmful. The distinction between a pattern that maintains difficulty and a behaviour that is abusive must be maintained: coercion is not a circular pattern.

### Symptoms as communication

Some symptoms carry meaning within the system: a child's behaviour that draws the parents together, a partner's symptoms that provide an acceptable way of expressing distress. The assessor should consider these meanings without over-interpreting, and should test interpretations with the family rather than presenting them as facts.

## 13.10|Cultural and Socioeconomic Context

Relational patterns are culturally patterned, and the assessor's own cultural assumptions are part of the assessment instrument.

### Cultural variation in family and couple norms

Expectations regarding gender roles, intergenerational cohabitation, obligations to family of origin, marital conflict, privacy and emotional expression vary widely. A pattern that appears enmeshed within one cultural frame may be normative within another, and a pattern that appears unproblematic within one frame may be harmful within another. The assessor should ask about the family's own expectations and should treat the family's frame as the starting point.

### Migration and family process

Migration creates relational patterns of its own: separation and reunification, differences in acculturation between generations, responsibilities placed on children as translators, and the loss of extended family support. The assessment should establish the migration history, the current family composition, and the distribution of roles, which often differ from the family's ideal.

### Socioeconomic context

Housing insecurity, poverty, shift work, unemployment and caring responsibilities shape relational life. A couple whose conflict arises from overcrowding and financial strain requires material intervention as well as relational work, and a formulation that ignores this will be both inaccurate and condescending.

## 13.11|Measures of Relationship Functioning

Standardised measures provide a comparison with norms, a baseline and a method for monitoring change. They supplement, and never replace, the interactional observation that is the core of relational assessment.

### Instruments in common use

The Dyadic Adjustment Scale and the Couples Satisfaction Index measure relationship satisfaction. The Oral History Interview is an observation-based method for assessing the couple's narrative of their relationship. Family assessment measures include the Family Assessment Device and observational coding systems for family interaction. Each has different evidence, different administration requirements and different purposes, and the assessor should match the instrument to the question.

### Multi-informant scoring

Relational measures should normally be completed by all participating members, and the discrepancy between them is itself informative. A couple in which one partner reports high satisfaction and the other reports low satisfaction is in a different position from one in which both report low satisfaction, and the formulation should reflect the difference.

### Monitoring

Where relational therapy is being conducted, repeated measures of satisfaction and of the specific problem can inform the therapist about progress. As with individual monitoring, the measure should be one with demonstrated sensitivity to change, and the score should be interpreted alongside the couple's own account.

## 13.12|Systemic Formulation and Feedback

The systemic formulation integrates interactional patterns, developmental history, individual symptoms, cultural context and the family's own understanding into an account that explains why this difficulty is occurring now and what would need to change.

### Structure

A useful systemic formulation describes the presenting pattern, the cycle that maintains it, the developmental and historical antecedents, the strengths and resources of the system, the wider context, and the possible points of intervention. It should be stated in language all participants can follow, and it should be presented as a hypothesis for the family to test rather than as a verdict delivered to them.

### Multi-person feedback

Feedback to a couple or family requires different skills from feedback to an individual. The assessor should manage the conversational turn-taking, ensure each person's account is reflected, avoid attributing blame, acknowledge disagreement, and keep the session safe. Where the assessment has identified a pattern that participants have not recognised, the assessor should present it tentatively and invite their view.

### Written reports

Written relational reports should describe patterns rather than persons, should balance strengths with difficulties, and should avoid allocating responsibility in ways that the report cannot evidence. Where a report will be read in a legal context, the assessor should be especially careful about the difference between observation, inference and opinion, and should state the limits of the assessment.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A couple in their forties are referred by their general practitioner after the wife's treatment for depression failed to improve. The referral describes "relationship difficulties" and asks for couple therapy.

**The assessment.** Each partner is seen individually first. The wife describes exhaustion, low mood and a sense that her husband is "not there" for her. The husband describes feeling criticised and says he "keeps the peace" by withdrawing. Both deny violence, but on direct screening the wife discloses that her husband has pushed her twice during arguments in the past year, has prevented her from seeing a friend, and controls all the household money. She says she is afraid of his reaction if he knew she had disclosed. In the joint session, the demand–withdraw cycle is visible: she raises a concern, he becomes silent and looks away, she escalates, he leaves the room.

**The reasoning.** The demands of the referral — couple therapy for relationship difficulties — cannot safely be met. The presence of physical violence and economic control is a contraindication to joint work. The individual presentations (depression in the wife, withdrawal and defensiveness in the husband) are embedded in a pattern that includes coercive control, and the circular formulation that would describe as mutual is not appropriate where one partner is afraid.

**The decision.** The report declines couple therapy, records the safety concerns and the contraindication, and refers to a specialist domestic abuse service with the wife's consent. It offers the husband an individual assessment with a focus on his use of control, and records that work with the couple could be reconsidered only if the violence and control were addressed and the wife wished it. It records the wife's account, her fear, and the safety plan agreed.

**The teaching point.** The routine individual screening changed the entire assessment. A couple formulation built on the joint session alone would have described a mutual cycle and missed the pattern of control.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Relational assessment examines patterns between people, and the unit of assessment must be decided explicitly. Relationship history and presenting concerns should be gathered from each participant separately as well as jointly, and discrepancy among accounts is informative. Dyadic interaction is observed directly, with attention to demand–withdraw cycles, negative reciprocity and repair. Family structure, boundaries, coalitions and parenting are assessed in the family session, with attention to cultural and socioeconomic context. Safety screening is the first substantive task, and violence or coercive control contraindicates joint work. Individual symptoms are formulated within the relational system without attributing blame or obscuring harmful behaviour. Standardised measures supplement observation and should be completed by all participants. Systemic formulation and multi-person feedback should be presented as hypotheses that the family can test.

2. A couple asks you not to raise the issue of violence because "it would make things worse". What do you do?
