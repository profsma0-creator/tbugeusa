# CHAPTER 11|Assessment of Substance Use, Behavioural Addictions and Risk Behaviour

:::panel Learning Objectives
After working through this chapter, the reader should be able to:
- describe patterns of substance use using quantity, frequency and consequence measures;
- select and interpret screening instruments and understand their limitations;
- assess intoxication and withdrawal states and recognise when medical referral is required;
- evaluate severity and functional consequences using current diagnostic frameworks;
- distinguish substance-induced from independent mental symptoms;
- assess behavioural addictions without over-extending the concept;
- evaluate motivation and readiness for change without treating stages as fixed;
- integrate substance-related and other findings into a coherent formulation.

## Introduction

Substance use is simultaneously one of the most prevalent clinical issues and one of the most poorly assessed. It is under-detected in general services, under-reported by clients who fear judgement, and frequently recorded as a binary — "uses alcohol: yes/no" — in records that require far more detail to be useful. The consequences of poor assessment are substantial: missed diagnoses, inappropriate treatment, unsafe prescribing and avoidable deaths.

This chapter sets out a systematic approach. It begins with description: what is being used, how much, how often, with what consequences and with what pattern of control. It proceeds to screening and case finding, to the assessment of intoxication and withdrawal, to severity and functional consequences, and to the distinction between substance-induced and independent mental symptoms. It then addresses behavioural addictions, motivation, risk and harm reduction, and closes with integrated formulation. Throughout, the emphasis is on the practical questions that determine what should happen next.

## 11.1|Patterns of Substance Use

Adequate description requires four elements: substance, quantity, frequency and pattern. Each element carries information that the others do not.

### Quantity and frequency

Quantity and frequency questions should be specific to the substance and expressed in units the client uses. For alcohol, standard units or drinks per week, together with the pattern of heavy episodic drinking, is more informative than "moderate" or "social". For cannabis, the quantity is often expressed in grams per week or joints per day, and the potency varies substantially. For stimulants, opioids and novel substances, the pattern of use — daily, binge, weekend — often matters more than the quantity.

### Pattern and control

The pattern includes the time of day, the context, the triggers, the company, the duration of a typical episode, and the person's capacity to stop once started. Loss of control is a defining feature of dependence and is best assessed by asking about specific occasions rather than about general capacity: the number of times in the past month the person intended to have one drink and had more.

### Consequences and their assessment

Consequences include health, family, occupational, financial, legal and psychological domains. The assessor should ask about each specifically, since clients tend to report the consequences they consider acceptable and to omit those that carry shame. Useful prompts include missed work, arguments, injuries, blackouts, driving after drinking, borrowing money, and symptoms on waking.

## 11.2|Screening and Case Finding

Screening identifies people who may have a substance problem and who warrant fuller assessment. It is not a diagnosis, and its value depends on the setting and on what follows a positive result.

### Instruments

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT; Saunders, Aasland, Babor, de la Fuente, & Grant, 1993) is the most widely validated alcohol screen and includes items on consumption, dependence symptoms and consequences. The AUDIT-C is a shorter consumption-only version suitable for high-volume settings. The Drug Abuse Screening Test provides a parallel instrument for other substances. Single-item screens are useful where time is very limited but have lower sensitivity, particularly for women and older adults.

### Interpretation in context

Screening accuracy varies by setting, by population and by the threshold used. A positive screen in a general hospital population warrants a brief intervention and a fuller assessment; a positive screen in a substance treatment service is nearly uninformative, because almost everyone will screen positive. The assessor should know the prevalence of problematic use in the setting and should interpret the screen accordingly, in the manner described in Chapter 3.

### What to do with a positive screen

A positive screen should lead to a structured assessment covering the four elements above, an assessment of risk, a brief intervention where appropriate, and a clear plan. Screening without follow-up is a waste of the client's time and can be actively harmful if it raises concerns that are not addressed.

## 11.3|Intoxication and Withdrawal

Intoxication and withdrawal are medical states with psychological consequences, and their assessment is a prerequisite for any psychological conclusion drawn during or shortly after them.

### Intoxication

Intoxication impairs cognition, emotion regulation and judgement, and it distorts self-report. Assessment during intoxication should be limited to safety, orientation and the immediate presenting problem; a full psychiatric assessment conducted during intoxication is unreliable and should be repeated when the client is sober. The assessor should record the substances involved, the amount, the time of last use, and the observed signs.

### Withdrawal

Withdrawal syndromes vary by substance: alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be life-threatening and require medical management; opioid withdrawal is extremely distressing but rarely fatal; stimulant withdrawal produces depression, anhedonia and fatigue that may be mistaken for a depressive disorder. The assessor should ask about the onset and pattern of withdrawal symptoms, the client's previous withdrawal experiences, and whether medical support is available.

### Medical risk and referral

Certain situations require urgent medical referral: suspected alcohol withdrawal with tremor, sweating and confusion; benzodiazepine withdrawal after high-dose use; opioid overdose or recent overdose; chest pain or arrhythmia in a stimulant user; and any presentation with fever, rigidity or altered consciousness. The assessor's responsibility is to recognise these and to act, not to manage them.

## 11.4|Severity and Functional Consequences

Both major classifications have revised their approach to substance-related disorders. DSM-5-TR uses a single category of substance use disorder, graded as mild, moderate or severe according to the number of criteria met, and removes the former distinction between abuse and dependence. ICD-11 distinguishes harmful patterns of use from dependence, with dependence defined by a smaller set of core features including impaired control, increased priority, physiological features and continued use despite harm.

### Assessing severity

Severity assessment should combine criteria counts with functional consequences, because the two are not perfectly correlated. A person meeting few criteria but experiencing severe social and occupational consequences has a serious problem. The assessor should record the criteria met, the consequences, and the client's own view of severity, which is often more conservative than the evidence warrants and sometimes more accurate.

### Functional consequences

The domains to assess are health (including injuries, infections and chronic disease), mental health, relationships, parenting, employment and education, finances and housing, and legal status. Each should be recorded specifically: not "social problems" but "two relationships ended in the past year following arguments while drinking; currently living alone after partner left".

### The social context

Substance use occurs in a context that shapes both the pattern and the options for change. Housing, employment, social networks, availability, criminal justice involvement and stigma all matter. An assessment that describes the individual without describing the context will produce recommendations that cannot be implemented.

## 11.5|Polysubstance Use

Most people in treatment for substance problems use more than one substance, and the combinations carry risks that single-substance assessment misses.

### Patterns

Common patterns include alcohol with benzodiazepines (a particularly dangerous combination), alcohol with cocaine (where the alcohol prolongs and intensifies the cocaine effect), opioids with stimulants, cannabis with alcohol, and the use of prescribed medication with alcohol or illicit substances. The assessor should ask about each substance separately, including prescribed medication used outside its prescription and over-the-counter medication.

### Assessment

A substance-by-substance grid recording substance, route, quantity, frequency, duration, last use and consequences is the most efficient method. The grid also reveals patterns that the narrative conceals, such as weekend stimulant use with weekday alcohol, and it is easy to share with the client for correction.

### Risk

The principal risks of polysubstance use are pharmacokinetic interaction (particularly respiratory depression with alcohol and opioids or benzodiazepines), increased toxicity, more severe withdrawal, and greater difficulty in identifying which substance is producing which symptom. The assessor should record the combinations and flag the ones that carry medical risk.

## 11.6|Substance-Induced Mental Symptoms

Substances produce psychiatric symptoms that are often indistinguishable from primary mental disorders at a single assessment. The distinction matters because the treatment differs, and because a misattributed disorder can persist for years in the records.

### Presentation

Substance-induced conditions include substance-induced depressive disorder, anxiety disorder, psychotic disorder and bipolar disorder, as well as substance intoxication delirium and substance withdrawal delirium. The temporal criteria require that symptoms develop during or shortly after intoxication or withdrawal, and that they are not better explained by an independent disorder.

### Establishing the relationship

The assessor should establish the temporal sequence, the substances involved, the dose and duration, the persistence of symptoms after cessation, and whether similar symptoms have occurred without substance use. A period of abstinence is usually necessary before the diagnosis can be made with confidence; where abstinence is not feasible, the diagnosis should be provisional and the uncertainty recorded.

### The reverse error

The reverse error is to attribute all symptoms to substance use when an independent disorder is present. Many people with severe mental illness use substances, and the substance use may be a consequence of the disorder, a form of self-medication, or an independent problem. The assessor should generate both explanations and look for the evidence that discriminates them, rather than defaulting to the substance explanation because it is simpler.

## 11.7|Behavioural Addictions

Behavioural addictions — gambling, gaming, problematic internet use, compulsive sexual behaviour, shopping and others — share features with substance use disorders but differ in important ways.

### Gambling disorder and gaming disorder

Gambling disorder is recognised in DSM-5-TR as a behavioural addiction within the substance-related and addictive disorders chapter, supported by evidence on phenomenology, neurobiology and treatment. Gaming disorder is recognised in ICD-11, with a definition requiring impaired control, increasing priority and continuation despite negative consequences for at least twelve months. Both have structured assessment instruments and evidence-based treatments.

### The boundaries of the concept

The extension of the addiction framework to other behaviours is contested. Criteria developed for substances do not map neatly onto behaviours that are, in moderation, normal and necessary, such as internet use, eating or sexual activity. The assessor should be cautious about applying an addiction label to behaviours where the evidence base is thin, and should focus instead on functional impairment and the pattern of the behaviour.

### Assessment approach

Whatever the behaviour, the assessment should establish the pattern (frequency, duration, escalation), the function (what it does for the person), the consequences, the person's attempts to control it and their outcome, the co-occurring conditions, and the context. This functional approach is more robust than the application of a checklist, and it produces information that informs treatment.

## 11.8|Motivation and Readiness for Change

Motivation is not a fixed trait but a fluctuating state influenced by the person's circumstances, their experience of treatment, and their relationship with the practitioner. The assessment of motivation should be an assessment of the person's own reasons, not of their compliance with the assessor's goals.

### Models and their status

The transtheoretical model of change, with its stages of precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance, has been enormously influential and is used in services throughout the world. Its empirical support as a predictive model is modest, and its stage categories are not reliably measurable; its practical value lies in reminding practitioners that change is a process and that interventions should match the person's current position. Motivational interviewing, which developed alongside the model, has much stronger evidence for effectiveness in substance misuse treatment.

### Assessing motivation

Useful questions explore the person's own view: what they like about using, what concerns them, what would need to change for them to consider stopping or reducing, what they have tried before, and what happened. The balance of these considerations, in the client's own words, is more informative than a readiness score. Where the client has no concerns and no intention to change, that is a finding, and the appropriate response is to maintain engagement and reduce harm rather than to press for a commitment the client does not have.

### The ethical dimension

Motivation assessment is easily corrupted into an assessment of whether the client will do what the service wants. The ethical position is that the client's goals set the frame, that the practitioner offers information and options, and that coercion — whether from the criminal justice system, an employer or a family — must be recognised explicitly and its effect on the assessment recorded.

## 11.9|Medical and Social Risk

Substance use carries risks beyond dependence, and these should be assessed systematically in every case.

### Medical risk

Risks include overdose, infection (including blood-borne viruses from injecting), injuries (including road traffic collisions and falls), liver disease, cardiovascular disease, neurological damage, and interactions with prescribed medication. The assessor should establish injecting status and injecting practices, sharing of equipment, history of overdose, and current medical conditions, and should ensure that testing and vaccination are offered where relevant.

### Social and legal risk

Risks include loss of employment or housing, relationship breakdown, child safeguarding concerns, involvement in acquisitive crime, exploitation and victimisation. Where children are present in the household, the assessor has an obligation to consider their welfare and to follow local safeguarding procedures. Where the client is involved in the criminal justice system, the assessment should record this and its implications for treatment options.

### Suicide risk

Substance use is one of the strongest risk factors for suicide, both acutely during intoxication and chronically through its association with depression, impulsivity and social isolation. Suicide risk should be assessed in every substance use assessment, and the assessment should record what was asked and what was disclosed.

## 11.10|Collateral Data and Biological Measures

Because self-report of substance use is affected by memory, shame and fear of consequences, collateral and biological data are valuable supplements. Neither is a substitute for the clinical interview.

### Collateral sources

Partners, family members, employers and treatment records can supply information that the client omits. Collateral should be obtained with consent and should focus on observable behaviour: what the informant has seen, when, and with what consequences. Where the client declines collateral contact, the assessor should record the refusal and its effect on confidence.

### Biological measures

Biological measures include blood alcohol concentration, breath testing, urine and hair drug testing, and biomarkers such as liver enzymes (gamma-glutamyl transferase, mean corpuscular volume) and carbohydrate-deficient transferrin. Each has a detection window, a sensitivity and a specificity, and each can be affected by factors unrelated to recent use. A positive test establishes exposure, not impairment, dependence or the pattern of use; a negative test does not establish abstinence over a long period, because the window may have closed.

### Interpretation and consent

Biological testing requires consent, an explanation of what will be tested and why, and clarity about who will see the results. In medico-legal and employment contexts, the consequences of a positive result can be severe, and the assessor should be explicit about the limits of what the test establishes. Where testing is used to monitor treatment, the results should be interpreted alongside clinical information rather than as a verdict.

## 11.11|Assessment for Harm Reduction Planning

Harm reduction accepts that some people will continue to use substances and aims to reduce the harms associated with use rather than to require abstinence as a precondition of help. Its evidence base is strong for a range of interventions, and its principles are relevant to assessment even where the service's model is abstinence-oriented.

### Assessment for harm reduction

The assessment identifies the specific harms the person is at risk of and the practical steps that would reduce them: overdose risk and the availability of naloxone; injecting practices and access to sterile equipment; driving and alcohol; mixing substances; sexual health; and the safety of the environments in which use occurs. It also identifies the person's own priorities, which may be housing, money or relationships rather than substance use itself.

### The clinical stance

Harm reduction requires a non-judgemental stance that nonetheless maintains accurate assessment. The practitioner should be honest about risk, specific about what can be done, and clear that any reduction in harm is a legitimate outcome. This stance is compatible with supporting abstinence where that is the client's goal; it is incompatible with withholding help from people who are not ready to stop.

### Integration with treatment

Harm reduction planning should be integrated with the rest of the treatment plan, including mental health treatment, medical care and social support. A plan that addresses substance use in isolation from housing, employment and mental health is unlikely to succeed.

## 11.12|Integrated Diagnostic Formulation

The output of assessment is a formulation that explains the relationship between substance use, mental health, context and consequences, and that identifies the points at which intervention is likely to be effective.

### The key questions

A useful formulation answers four questions. What is the pattern of use and what function does it serve? What are the co-occurring conditions, and how do they interact with the substance use? What maintains the pattern — dependence, social context, untreated symptoms, limited alternatives, or some combination? What would need to change for the pattern to change, and in what order?

### Sequencing

The formulation should inform sequencing. Where withdrawal is medically risky, medical management comes first. Where a co-occurring disorder is severe and the substance use is a form of self-medication, treating the disorder may reduce use. Where the substance use is primary and severe, substance treatment may need to precede psychological work. Where the pattern is maintained by housing instability or isolation, social intervention may be the most effective first step.

### Recording uncertainty and integration

As elsewhere, the formulation should state what is known, what is inferred and what remains uncertain, and it should specify the review points at which the formulation will be tested. The most useful reports in this area are those that a client could read, recognise and use, and that a treatment team could act on.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 38-year-old man is referred by his general practitioner with "depression and anxiety". He has been drinking heavily for two years and has had two short courses of antidepressants with no benefit.

**The assessment.** He drinks approximately sixty units of alcohol per week, mostly in the evening, with a pattern of heavy weekend drinking. He reports morning tremor relieved by drinking, has twice tried to stop and experienced sweating and palpitations, and has missed work on several days each month. He describes low mood, anhedonia and early waking, which he dates to the period after his divorce two years ago, and which he reports does not lift during periods of abstinence of a few weeks. He has panic symptoms in crowded places, which he manages by drinking before going out. His liver function tests are mildly abnormal. He reports no suicidal ideation but says he "would not mind not waking up".

**The reasoning.** Two explanations compete: a primary depressive and anxiety disorder with secondary alcohol use, and an alcohol-induced mood disorder with a dependence syndrome. The persistence of low mood during several weeks of abstinence and the onset after a major life event support the first. The morning drinking and withdrawal symptoms indicate dependence requiring medical management. The panic symptoms are situationally triggered and may be independent, exacerbated by alcohol, or a consequence of repeated cycles of intoxication and withdrawal.

**The decision.** The report records harmful alcohol use with dependence, a depressive episode that appears independent of drinking, and panic symptoms requiring separate assessment. It recommends medically supervised alcohol withdrawal before any change in antidepressant treatment, treatment for depression in parallel with substance treatment, and an assessment for panic disorder once drinking has stabilised. It records the suicide risk assessment and a safety plan.

**The teaching point.** The interaction between depression and alcohol is the whole assessment. The mistake would have been to treat either alone.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Substance use assessment requires description of substance, quantity, frequency and pattern, followed by consequences across health, social and occupational domains. Screening instruments identify probable cases and depend on the setting's prevalence for their interpretation. Intoxication and withdrawal are medical states that distort psychological assessment and may require urgent referral. Severity combines criteria counts with functional consequences, and both major classifications now describe a graded continuum rather than a categorical distinction between abuse and dependence. Polysubstance use requires a substance-by-substance approach. Substance-induced and independent mental symptoms must be distinguished, usually with a period of abstinence. Behavioural addictions are recognised where the evidence supports them and should not be extended beyond it. Motivation is a fluctuating state to be assessed from the client's own perspective, and harm reduction provides a framework for working with people who are not ready to stop. The integrated formulation should explain the relationships among use, mental health and context, and inform sequencing.
