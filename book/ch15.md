## Introduction

Risk assessment occupies a peculiar position in professional practice. It is the area in which the consequences of error are most severe, the evidence base is most contested, and the pressure to produce certainty is greatest. Practitioners are asked to forecast events that are statistically rare, for individuals about whom they have limited information, in circumstances where the outcome will be reviewed in hindsight by people who know what happened.

This chapter begins from a position that is now well established in the risk literature: the aim of clinical risk assessment is not prediction but formulation, planning and mitigation. The assessor cannot reliably predict whether a particular person will attempt suicide next month. The assessor can identify the factors that elevate and reduce risk, describe the circumstances in which risk is likely to increase, specify what should happen if it does, and ensure that the person and the system around them are prepared. That is what good risk assessment delivers, and it is what the client is entitled to receive.

## 15.1|Principles of Risk Assessment

Several principles distinguish contemporary risk practice from earlier approaches that attempted to classify people as high or low risk.

### Assessment is not prediction

Actuarial instruments can rank order groups by risk with modest accuracy, but their application to individuals is limited: most people in a high-risk group do not experience the outcome, and many who do were not in the high-risk group. The clinical task is to understand the particular person's risk in their particular circumstances, and to act on that understanding, rather than to assign a category that implies a forecast.

### Risk is dynamic and contextual

Risk fluctuates with mental state, substance use, relationships, finances, housing and access to means. A person assessed as low risk in a stable period may become high risk within hours after a loss. Assessment should therefore identify the conditions under which risk rises, which is more useful than a summary rating.

### Risk and protective factors interact

Protective factors are not simply the absence of risk factors. Reasons for living, responsibility to dependants, connection to community, engagement with treatment, and future orientation are independent contributors, and their identification is central to safety planning.

### Unstructured judgement is unreliable but necessary

Research on clinical judgement has shown that unstructured risk assessment is less accurate than structured methods, and that clinicians are overconfident in their predictions. Structured professional judgement instruments improve consistency without removing the need for clinical formulation. The appropriate position combines structure, evidence and formulation.

## 15.2|Suicidal Thoughts and Behaviour

Suicide risk assessment is the most common risk task in mental health practice and the one most often conducted superficially.

### Asking about suicidal ideation

Direct, specific questions are required: whether the person has thought about ending their life, whether they have thought about how, whether they have taken any steps, and whether they have made preparations. Asking does not increase risk; the evidence on this point is consistent and the myth that it does has cost lives. Questions should be asked in a way that invites disclosure rather than endorsement: "when things have been at their worst, have you thought about not being here?" rather than "you're not thinking of doing anything silly, are you?"

### Assessing intent, plan and means

Ideation alone is of limited predictive value; the combination of intent, a specific plan, preparation, access to means and a recent attempt carries substantially more risk. The assessor should establish whether the person wants to die or wants the pain to stop, whether they have made a plan, whether they have acquired means, whether they have written a note, and whether they have told anyone. Each element modifies the formulation and the plan.

### Previous attempts

A previous attempt is among the strongest predictors of a subsequent attempt, and the assessor should establish the number, method, medical severity, circumstances and aftermath of previous attempts. An attempt that the person describes as "a cry for help" is not thereby less significant; the intent of the attempt and its lethality are different dimensions.

### Warning signs and acute risk

Acute risk is elevated by agitation, severe insomnia, recent loss, intoxication, access to lethal means, withdrawal from support, a sense of being a burden, hopelessness and the capacity to act on a plan. The assessor should ask about each and should record the pattern rather than a single rating.

## 15.3|Non-Suicidal Self-Injury

Non-suicidal self-injury is intentional self-inflicted damage to the body without suicidal intent, most commonly cutting, burning, hitting and interfering with wound healing. It is distinct from suicidal behaviour and the distinction matters for assessment and treatment.

### Distinguishing the two

The functions differ: self-injury commonly serves to regulate emotion, to punish the self, to communicate distress or to feel real, whereas suicidal behaviour is oriented towards ending life. The methods differ in lethality and in body site. The two can co-occur, and self-injury is a risk factor for subsequent suicide, but treating them as equivalent leads to both over-reaction and under-recognition.

### Assessing self-injury

The assessment establishes the methods, frequency, function, contexts, injuries requiring medical attention, the person's understanding of the behaviour, and its relationship to suicidal ideation. The function is the key to treatment: a person who self-injures to reduce overwhelming emotion requires emotion regulation work; a person who self-injures to communicate distress requires attention to the relational context.

### Responding

The response should be non-judgemental and practical: medical care where needed, an explanation of the function of the behaviour, a plan for alternative strategies, and clear arrangements for what to do if the urge becomes overwhelming. Contracts for safety, historically used, are not evidence-based; safety planning, which is collaborative and specifies coping strategies and sources of support, is preferable.

## 15.4|Violence and Aggression Risk

Risk of violence arises in psychiatric, forensic, occupational and family settings, and its assessment requires a different structure from suicide risk assessment.

### Structured professional judgement

The dominant approach is structured professional judgement, exemplified by instruments such as the Historical-Clinical-Risk Management-20 (HCR-20) and the Short-Term Assessment of Risk and Treatability (START). These instruments guide the assessor through a set of empirically supported factors, require a judgement about risk rather than a score, and produce a risk formulation and management plan. Their value lies in consistency, transparency and the integration of static and dynamic factors.

### Historical and dynamic factors

Historical factors, such as previous violence, young age at first violence, substance use and childhood maltreatment, establish a baseline level of risk. Dynamic factors, such as current substance use, psychotic symptoms, hostility, impulsivity, lack of insight and unstable living situation, determine the current level. The assessment should identify which dynamic factors are present and modifiable.

### The formulation

A violence risk formulation describes the person's typical pattern of violence (if any), the circumstances that trigger it, the likely targets, the factors that would escalate it, and the factors that would reduce it. This formulation is more useful than a risk level, because it implies what to do.

## 15.5|Domestic Abuse and Coercive Control

Domestic abuse is a pattern of controlling behaviour that may include physical, sexual, psychological, economic and immigration-related abuse. Coercive control is the ongoing pattern of domination that underlies much domestic abuse and that causes severe psychological harm even in the absence of physical violence.

### Recognition

Recognition requires routine, direct enquiry, conducted in private and in a way that allows disclosure. Useful questions address fear, control over money, control over contact with friends and family, threats, and escalation. Indicators include injuries with inconsistent explanations, frequent appointments with vague complaints, a partner who insists on being present, and a client who seems anxious about the partner's reaction.

### Response

Where domestic abuse is disclosed, the assessment must change: the safety of the client and any children becomes the first priority; specialist services should be involved; and joint sessions or couple work are contraindicated. The assessor should record the disclosure, the actions taken and the reasoning, and should be aware of the legal framework for disclosure of information to protect a person at risk.

### Risk of homicide and escalation

Separation, pregnancy, threats to kill, strangulation, access to weapons and a history of violence are established risk markers for severe harm. Where these are present, the response should be immediate and should involve specialist domestic abuse services and, where the law permits, the police.

## 15.6|Child and Vulnerable Adult Safeguarding

Safeguarding is the process of protecting children and adults at risk from abuse and neglect. It is governed by law and by local procedures, and it applies to all practitioners regardless of their professional role.

### Children

Child safeguarding concerns include physical, sexual and emotional abuse, neglect, domestic abuse in the household, and exploitation. The assessor's obligation is to recognise indicators, to record them factually, and to refer to the local authority. The assessor does not investigate; the assessor reports. Where a child makes a disclosure, the response should be to listen, to reassure, to record in the child's words, and to explain what will happen next.

### Adults at risk

Adults may be at risk because of learning disability, mental illness, physical frailty, sensory impairment, substance dependence or social isolation. Safeguarding concerns include physical and sexual abuse, financial exploitation, neglect by carers, and self-neglect. The assessor should be familiar with the local framework, which in many jurisdictions includes a duty to make enquiries where an adult with care and support needs is at risk.

### Recording and referral

Records should be factual, specific and contemporaneous, and should distinguish observation from inference. Referrals should be made within the timescales specified by local procedures. The assessor should inform the client of the referral unless doing so would place them or another person at risk.

## 15.7|Static and Dynamic Risk Factors

The distinction between static and dynamic factors organises risk formulation and identifies what can be changed.

### Static factors

Static factors are historical and unchangeable: age at first offence, number of previous attempts, history of violence, childhood adversity, and previous admissions. They establish a baseline and are useful for stratifying risk, but they offer no points of intervention.

### Dynamic factors

Dynamic factors are current and modifiable: substance use, symptoms, employment, housing, relationships, treatment engagement and hopelessness. They are the targets of intervention and the elements that change between assessments. The assessor should identify which dynamic factors are present, which are modifiable, and which are priorities.

### Acute and stable dynamic factors

Dynamic factors may themselves be distinguished into stable factors that change over months (employment, relationships) and acute factors that change over hours or days (intoxication, agitation, recent loss). Acute factors are the ones that determine whether the person is safe today, and they are the ones most often omitted from risk summaries.

## 15.8|Protective Factors and Reasons for Living

Protective factors reduce the likelihood of an outcome even in the presence of risk, and they are the foundation of safety planning.

### Identifying protective factors

Protective factors include reasons for living, responsibility to children or dependants, religious or moral objections to suicide, connection to community, engagement with treatment, future orientation, problem-solving capacity, and social support. The assessor should elicit these explicitly, since clients often do not volunteer them and since their identification is itself therapeutic.

### Their limits

Protective factors do not cancel risk, and their presence should not be used to justify inaction. A person with strong reasons for living may still attempt suicide during an acute crisis. The assessor should record protective factors as resources to be mobilised, not as reasons that risk can be discounted.

### Using protective factors in formulation

Protective factors belong in the formulation as conditions that make certain plans feasible: a parent who is committed to their children may be more likely to engage with a plan that involves them, and a person with strong community ties may be more likely to use a support that is available through that community.

## 15.9|Structured Professional Judgement

Structured professional judgement combines the use of evidence-based risk factors, the assessor's clinical judgement and the involvement of the client in the formulation and plan.

### The method

The method proceeds in five steps: gather information from multiple sources; identify the relevant risk factors using a structured framework; formulate the risk in terms of pattern, circumstances, triggers and likely escalation; develop a management plan that addresses the modifiable factors; and record the reasoning and the review arrangements.

### The role of instruments

Instruments structure the process and improve consistency. They do not replace formulation, and their scores should not be reported as risk levels without interpretation. An instrument that identifies twelve risk factors present tells the assessor nothing unless it also tells them which factors matter for this person and what to do about them.

### The client's involvement

Where possible, the client should be involved in the risk formulation and the safety plan. Involvement improves the plan's feasibility, respects the client's autonomy, and provides information that the assessor would not otherwise have. Where the client cannot be involved — because of acute psychosis, intoxication or coercive control — the reason should be recorded.

## 15.10|Safety Planning and Immediate Intervention

Safety planning is a collaborative process that produces a written plan for what the person will do when risk escalates.

### Components of a safety plan

A safety plan typically includes: the person's warning signs; internal coping strategies; people and places that provide distraction; people the person can contact for help; professionals and services to contact in a crisis; and steps to make the environment safer, including means restriction. The plan should be written, should be in the person's own words where possible, and should be reviewed and updated.

### Means restriction

Access to means is one of the few modifiable factors with strong evidence for reducing suicide deaths. The assessor should discuss means restriction directly — medication, firearms, pesticides, ligature points — and should involve family members where the person consents and where their involvement is safe. Means restriction is more effective than contracts for safety, which have no evidence base.

### Immediate intervention

Where risk is imminent, the response should be immediate: continuous observation where the setting permits, urgent referral to crisis services or emergency department, involvement of emergency services, and removal of means. The assessor should record what was done, by whom, and what the client was told.

## 15.11|Documentation and Communication of Risk

Risk documentation is read in emergencies, in reviews and, occasionally, in courts. Its purpose is to support action.

### What to record

Records should state what was asked, what was disclosed, what was observed, the assessor's formulation, the reasoning behind it, the actions taken, the communication with others, and the review arrangements. They should distinguish fact from inference and should record uncertainty rather than concealing it.

### Communication with other parties

Risk information should be shared with relevant professionals, with the client's consent where possible and without it where necessary to protect life. The legal framework varies by jurisdiction, and the assessor should know the local rules on confidentiality and disclosure. Where information is shared without consent, the decision and its justification should be recorded.

### Communicating with the client

Clients should be told what the assessor is concerned about, what will happen next and why. This is both an ethical requirement and a clinical one: a client who understands the plan is more likely to engage with it. Where the assessor is obliged to act against the client's wishes, the client should be told what is happening and why, in language they can understand.

## 15.12|Reassessment and Continuity of Care

Risk assessment is not a single event. It should be repeated when circumstances change, when the person moves between services, and at specified intervals.

### Triggers for reassessment

Reassessment is triggered by a change in mental state, a new loss or stressor, a change in substance use, discharge from hospital, a change in living situation, a new relationship, and any incident of self-harm or aggression. Services should specify these triggers in their procedures, and individual plans should name them.

### Transitions of care

The period after discharge from inpatient care is a period of elevated risk, and it requires specific planning: an appointment within a defined period, contact details, a named professional, and a plan for what to do if the person cannot be reached. Transitions between services are similarly high-risk, and the receiving service should be given the risk formulation, not merely the risk level.

### Review and learning

Services should review serious incidents and near misses, not to allocate blame but to identify what could have been done differently. Individual practitioners should review their own risk assessments when outcomes differ from expectations, since this is the most effective form of calibration available.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 52-year-old man is referred by his general practitioner after a relationship breakdown. He has a history of two previous suicide attempts, both by overdose, the most recent eight years ago. He lives alone, has recently lost his job, and drinks heavily.

**The assessment.** He describes passive suicidal ideation — "I wouldn't mind not waking up" — but denies intent or plan. He reports that his ex-partner has stopped contact and that he has not seen his children for three weeks. He has a stock of prescribed medication at home, including a tricyclic antidepressant that is dangerous in overdose. He reports drinking a bottle of spirits daily and has had one episode of alcohol withdrawal. He describes his dog as "the only reason I get up". He says he would not want his children to find him.

**The formulation.** Static risk factors include two previous attempts and male sex; dynamic factors include recent loss, unemployment, social isolation, heavy drinking, access to a lethal means and a sense of burdensomeness. Protective factors include his children, his dog and his expressed wish not to be found by his children. Acute risk is elevated by intoxication and by the recent loss, but intent is currently low. The combination of a dangerous means, heavy drinking and social isolation means that risk could escalate rapidly with little warning.

**The decision.** The safety plan includes: removal of the tricyclic medication with the general practitioner's agreement and substitution with a safer alternative; a plan for the dog to be cared for by a neighbour if he is admitted; daily contact with a named professional for two weeks; an appointment with the substance service within a week; and a written crisis plan with the number of the local crisis line. The report records the formulation, the reasoning, the actions taken and the review date.

**The teaching point.** No prediction was made. The assessment identified the factors that would make escalation likely, modified the one with the strongest evidence — access to means — and built a plan around the factors that mattered to him.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Risk assessment is a process of formulation, planning and mitigation rather than prediction. Suicidal risk requires direct enquiry about ideation, intent, plan, means and previous attempts, and acute risk is elevated by agitation, insomnia, intoxication, loss and access to means. Non-suicidal self-injury is distinct from suicidal behaviour and requires assessment of function. Violence risk is best assessed through structured professional judgement, combining historical and dynamic factors in a formulation that specifies circumstances and targets. Domestic abuse and coercive control require routine enquiry, immediate safety planning and a prohibition on joint work. Safeguarding procedures apply to children and adults at risk and require factual recording and timely referral. Static factors establish baseline risk, dynamic factors identify what can change, and protective factors are resources to be mobilised rather than reasons to discount risk. Safety planning and means restriction are the interventions with the strongest evidence, and documentation should support action, continuity and review.
