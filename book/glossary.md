# |Glossary

Definitions are given for the sense in which each term is used in this book. Where a term has a different technical meaning in another tradition, the difference is noted.

**Accommodation.** A change to the conditions of assessment or of an activity that removes a barrier unrelated to the construct being measured, without altering the construct itself. Distinguished from a modification, which changes what is measured.

**Acculturation.** The process of change in values, practices and identifications that follows sustained contact between cultural groups; treated here as domain-specific rather than as a single dimension.

**Adaptive behaviour.** The conceptual, social and practical skills required for everyday functioning. A defining component of the diagnosis of intellectual disability.

**Adjustment disorder.** A maladaptive response to an identifiable stressor, with symptoms that do not meet criteria for another disorder, beginning within three months of the stressor.

**Anchoring.** A judgement bias in which an early piece of information, such as a previous diagnosis in the records, exerts disproportionate influence on subsequent reasoning.

**Anhedonia.** Loss of interest or pleasure in activities that were previously rewarding; a core feature of depressive episodes.

**Aptitude.** The capacity to acquire a skill or knowledge under specified conditions of training; interpreted as a forecast rather than a description of current ability.

**Assessment.** The broad process of answering a question about a person through the integration of interview, observation, informant report, records, testing and functional information. Not synonymous with testing.

**Base rate.** The prevalence of a condition in the setting in which the assessment takes place. Governs the interpretation of predictive values and the appropriate placement of decision thresholds.

**Behavioural addiction.** A pattern of repetitive behaviour, such as gambling or gaming, that shares phenomenological and neurobiological features with substance dependence. The concept is well established for some behaviours and contested for others.

**Broad-band inventory.** A personality or symptom measure that assesses multiple domains in a single administration.

**Capacity.** The ability to make a specific decision at a specific time, assessed by a functional test defined in the relevant jurisdiction. Distinct from insight.

**Case formulation.** An explanatory account of why this person, at this time, in this context, has developed and maintained these difficulties; testable and predictive.

**Coercive control.** An ongoing pattern of domination in an intimate relationship, encompassing physical, sexual, psychological, economic and immigration-related control, and harmful even in the absence of physical violence.

**Collateral information.** Evidence supplied by someone other than the client, or documented before the current assessment.

**Comorbidity.** The co-occurrence of two or more disorders in one person. The rule rather than the exception in clinical populations.

**Competing hypotheses.** The practice of holding several explanations in view simultaneously and seeking the evidence that discriminates among them.

**Compulsion.** A repetitive behaviour or mental act performed in response to an obsession, or according to rigid rules, in order to reduce distress or prevent a feared outcome.

**Construct validity.** The degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretation of scores as measures of a specified construct; in contemporary usage, the whole of the validity argument rather than one of its types.

**Cultural formulation.** A structured account of the client's explanatory model, cultural identity, stressors and supports, and the relationship between cultural factors and the presenting difficulty.

**Cut score.** The value on a score scale at which a decision changes; the operational expression of a decision rule.

**Differential diagnosis.** The disciplined comparison of competing explanations, conducted by identifying the evidence that would separate them.

**Differential item functioning.** A form of measurement bias in which respondents of equal standing on the construct but from different groups have different probabilities of endorsing a particular item.

**Ecological validity.** The degree to which performance on a test predicts functioning in the person's everyday environment.

**Evidence-based assessment.** The selection, interpretation and use of assessment methods justified by research evidence, clinical expertise and the client's characteristics, culture and preferences.

**Executive function.** The supervisory processes that organise goal-directed behaviour, including inhibition, shifting, updating and planning. Poorly captured by laboratory tasks in isolation.

**Formulation.** See case formulation.

**Functional analysis.** The identification of the antecedents, behaviour and consequences that maintain a presenting pattern, used particularly in the analysis of avoidance.

**Functional capacity.** What a person can do in specified activities of daily living, distinguished from what they actually do and from the support that makes performance possible.

**g factor.** The general factor that accounts for a substantial proportion of variance across diverse cognitive tasks.

**Hypomania.** A distinct period of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood with increased energy, lasting at least four days, without the marked impairment or psychosis that characterises mania.

**Idiom of distress.** A culturally patterned way of expressing and communicating distress, such as somatic complaint or spiritual attribution.

**Impairment.** The effect of a condition on the person's capacity to function in specified domains; distinguished from symptom severity.

**Incremental validity.** The degree to which a measure improves prediction or decision accuracy beyond information already available.

**Insight.** Awareness and understanding of one's condition, best assessed dimensionally (awareness of symptoms, attribution, need for treatment, awareness of consequences). Distinct from capacity.

**Interrater reliability.** The consistency of scores or judgements across independent raters.

**Intersectionality.** The interaction of multiple social positions in shaping experience and disadvantage; treated in assessment as a reason to examine specific configurations rather than to assume that one characteristic explains a presentation.

**Level of personality functioning.** The severity of impairment in self (identity, self-direction) and interpersonal (empathy, intimacy) functioning, used to grade personality disorder.

**Likelihood ratio.** The ratio of the probability of a test result in people with a condition to its probability in people without it; used to revise a pre-test probability.

**Measurement invariance.** The degree to which an instrument measures the same construct in the same metric across groups; a precondition for comparing group means.

**Mental status examination.** The structured description of a client's current psychological functioning, covering appearance, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement.

**Multi-informant assessment.** The collection of information from several people who observe the client in different settings; discrepancy among informants is treated as informative rather than as error.

**Negative symptoms.** Reductions in normal functions — blunted affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia and asociality — that predict functional outcome in psychotic disorders.

**Norms.** A description of the distribution of scores in a defined reference sample; a comparison, not a standard of health.

**Obsession.** A recurrent and persistent intrusive thought, image or urge that is experienced as unwanted and that the person attempts to ignore, suppress or neutralise.

**Performance validity.** The extent to which performance on cognitive testing reflects the person's true ability, assessed through dedicated and embedded measures.

**Premorbid functioning.** The level of cognitive or functional ability that a person is estimated to have had before the onset of a condition; estimated demographically, by reading-based measures or by best previous performance.

**Percentile rank.** The proportion of a reference sample scoring at or below an obtained score.

**Positive predictive value.** The proportion of positive test results that are true positives in a given setting; dependent on prevalence.

**Private events.** Internal experiences such as thoughts, images and sensations, which are accessible only through the client's report.

**Protective factor.** A characteristic or circumstance that reduces the likelihood of a harmful outcome, independently of risk factors.

**Provisional diagnosis.** A diagnosis offered on the available evidence with an explicit statement of its uncertainty and the observations that would confirm or refute it.

**Psychometrics.** The discipline concerned with the theory and technique of psychological measurement, including reliability, validity, norms and measurement error.

**Reification.** The treatment of a score, label or construct as a concrete entity; a recurring error in personality and cognitive reporting.

**Reliability.** The consistency of scores across replications of a measurement procedure; meaningful only in relation to the source of error addressed.

**Responsivity.** The extent to which a measure detects change when change occurs.

**Safeguarding.** The process of protecting children and adults at risk from abuse, neglect and exploitation, governed by law and local procedure.

**Safety planning.** A collaborative, written plan specifying the person's warning signs, coping strategies, sources of support and steps to reduce access to means.

**Scalar invariance.** The level of measurement invariance at which item intercepts as well as factor loadings are equal across groups; required before comparing group means.

**Sensitivity.** The proportion of people with a condition who are correctly identified by a test.

**Specificity.** The proportion of people without a condition who are correctly identified as negative by a test.

**Standard error of measurement.** The standard deviation of the hypothetical distribution of obtained scores around a person's true score; used to construct confidence intervals around individual scores.

**Standard error of the difference.** The standard error applicable to the difference between two scores, always larger than the standard error of either score alone.

**Standardised administration.** Fidelity to the manual's instructions on wording, order, timing, materials, prompting, recording and discontinuation, which is the condition under which norms apply.

**Static and dynamic risk factors.** Historical and unchangeable factors that establish baseline risk, as against current and modifiable factors that determine present risk.

**Structured professional judgement.** An approach to risk assessment that combines empirically supported risk factors, clinical judgement and a management plan, rather than producing a predictive score.

**Structured interview.** A diagnostic interview with fixed questions and coding rules, designed to improve the reliability of diagnosis.

**Systemic formulation.** An account of a difficulty in terms of patterns of interaction among family members or other participants, rather than in terms of individual deficits.

**Test–retest reliability.** The consistency of scores across two administrations separated by an interval.

**Thought form.** The structure, flow and coherence of thinking as observed in speech; distinguished from thought content.

**Validity.** See construct validity.

**Working memory.** The capacity to hold and manipulate information over short periods; a distinct cognitive domain and a frequent locus of impairment.
