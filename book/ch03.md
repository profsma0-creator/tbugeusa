## Introduction

Psychometrics is the discipline that allows an assessor to say how much confidence a score deserves. It is not an optional technical background to clinical practice but the grammar in which score-based claims are made. An assessor who cannot compute a confidence interval around an obtained score, or explain why a difference between two subtests may be unremarkable, is not in a position to interpret the instrument they have used, however well they administered it.

This chapter sets out the minimum technical apparatus required for advanced practice. It treats reliability as a property of scores rather than of tests, validity as an argument rather than a coefficient, and norms as a comparison sample rather than a standard of health. It also insists on a distinction that the clinical literature often blurs: the difference between the accuracy of a measure and the usefulness of a decision made with it. A test can be accurate and still be a poor basis for a decision, if the decision threshold is badly placed or the base rate in the setting is low.

## 3.1|Measurement Models and Observed Scores

Every score produced by a psychological test is an observation, not a quantity known without error. Classical test theory formalises this intuition: an observed score is the sum of a true score and an error component, where the true score is defined as the expected value of observed scores across hypothetical repeated administrations. The model is deliberately modest. It does not claim that the true score is a fixed property of the person, only that a given measurement procedure has an expected value for that person at that time.

### The classical model and its assumptions

The classical model assumes that error is random, uncorrelated with true score and uncorrelated across measurements. These assumptions are convenient rather than universally true: practice effects, fatigue, mood change and item exposure all produce error that is systematic rather than random. The practical consequence is that classical indices may over-state the stability of a score across occasions, which is why test–retest coefficients are usually lower than internal consistency coefficients for the same instrument.

### Domain-sampling and latent-variable models

Two other families of measurement model are worth distinguishing. Domain-sampling models treat a test as a sample from a universe of equivalent items; generalisability theory extends this by allowing the assessor to quantify how much of the error is attributable to particular facets, such as occasion, rater or item set. Latent-variable models treat the observed items as indicators of an unobserved construct and estimate the relationship between them. Item response theory, a latent-variable approach, has the practical advantage of placing persons and items on the same scale, which is why it underpins many modern adaptive tests and norming programmes.

### Why the model matters clinically

The model matters because interpretations differ according to which one is assumed. An internal-consistency interpretation treats a high coefficient as evidence that the items measure one thing; a test–retest interpretation treats it as evidence that the score is stable. Neither implies the other, and neither implies validity. The assessor who asks "what does this coefficient tell me about the claim I am about to make?" will avoid most of the misinterpretations that appear in reports.

## 3.2|Reliability and Score Consistency

Reliability refers to the consistency of scores across replications of a measurement procedure. It is not a single property but a family of properties, each defined by the source of error that is allowed to vary. A coefficient is meaningless unless the assessor knows which source of error it addresses.

### Internal consistency

Cronbach's alpha remains the most reported coefficient, and the most misused. It is a lower bound to reliability under restrictive assumptions of essential tau-equivalence, and it is inflated by test length and by item redundancy. Sijtsma (2009) argued that alpha's usefulness is limited and that its routine reporting has obscured the more relevant question of whether a set of items measures a single attribute; McNeish (2018) and Dunn, Baguley and Brunsden (2014) have made the same case and recommended alternatives such as omega. For the assessor, the practical implications are that a high alpha does not establish unidimensionality, that alpha should be reported for the specific sample used, and that a very high alpha (above .95) in a short scale may indicate redundant items rather than excellent measurement.

### Test–retest and stability

Test–retest reliability is relevant whenever a score will be interpreted as a stable characteristic or used to evaluate change. Coefficients are affected by the interval: too short and respondents remember their answers; too long and genuine change attenuates the coefficient. For state measures, low test–retest reliability is expected and appropriate, since the construct itself is meant to vary. Reporting a state measure's stability as though it were a virtue, or a trait measure's instability as though it were a defect, are both category errors.

### Inter-rater reliability and agreement

Where a score depends on clinical judgement — a diagnostic interview, a personality rating, a risk assessment — inter-rater reliability is central. Cohen's kappa corrects for chance agreement on categorical judgements and is conventionally interpreted with the benchmarks proposed by Landis and Koch (1977), although these are arbitrary and should be reported alongside raw agreement and prevalence. For continuous ratings, intraclass correlation coefficients are preferable to simple correlations because they account for systematic differences between raters as well as rank-order agreement.

## 3.3|Standard Error and Confidence Intervals

A reliability coefficient is not directly useful at the level of the individual case. What the assessor needs is an estimate of how far an individual's obtained score is likely to be from their true score. The standard error of measurement provides this: it is the standard deviation of the hypothetical distribution of obtained scores around a person's true score, and it is computed as the standard deviation of the test multiplied by the square root of one minus the reliability coefficient.

### Using the standard error

Because the error distribution is approximately normal, a 95 per cent confidence interval is obtained by adding and subtracting 1.96 standard errors from the obtained score. For an instrument with a standard deviation of 15 and a reliability of .90, the standard error is 4.74 and the 95 per cent interval around an obtained score of 100 runs from approximately 91 to 109. Two consequences follow. First, a score is a region, not a point, and reports should present it as such. Second, differences between scores that lie within overlapping intervals should not be interpreted as meaningful.

### The standard error of the difference

Comparisons between two scores require a different statistic, because each score carries its own error. The standard error of the difference incorporates the reliability and the correlation between the two measures, and it is always larger than the standard error of either score alone. This is why the difference between a verbal and a performance score of ten points is often unremarkable, and why the interpretation of subtest scatter requires the difference to exceed a threshold that the manual should specify. Assessors who interpret any difference as a profile are reading noise.

### Regression to the mean and extreme scores

Where a client is selected for assessment because their score was extreme — a high score on a screening questionnaire, a low score on an intake measure — a second administration will tend to be closer to the mean even if nothing has changed. This is a statistical property, not evidence of improvement or deterioration. Its practical implication is that change should be evaluated against a comparison group or a reliable change index rather than against the raw difference between two scores.

## 3.4|Validity as Evidence for Interpretation

Validity is the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations and uses of test scores. It is a property of an interpretation, not of an instrument, and it is never established once and for all. The current *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* frame validation as the accumulation of evidence across five sources: evidence based on test content, on response processes, on internal structure, on relations with other variables, and on the consequences of testing (American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, & National Council on Measurement in Education, 2014).

### The unified view

Messick's (1995) unified conception of validity integrated these sources into a single argument about score meaning, and it remains the dominant framework. In this view, construct validity is not one type among several but the whole of the argument, of which content, structure and external relations are components. Kane (2013) extended the approach by describing validation as the construction of an interpretive argument that can be evaluated for its inferences and assumptions. Both frameworks share a practical implication: the assessor should be able to state the argument that connects an observed score to the claim being made, and should be able to identify the weakest link in that argument.

### What validity is not

Validity is not the same as reliability, though reliability constrains it. It is not established by factor analysis alone, by correlation with another instrument alone, or by the test's widespread use. Nor is it a property that transfers automatically across populations, languages or purposes. An instrument validated for screening in primary care is not thereby validated for forensic determination, and an instrument validated in one cultural setting requires evidence before it is used in another.

### Threats to validity in practice

The most common threats in clinical practice are not statistical but procedural: administration that departs from the standard without being recorded; interpretation that ignores the reference group; use of a measure outside its validated age range; coaching or rehearsal of items; and the accumulation of multiple small deviations, none of which alone would invalidate the score. The assessor's protection is documentation specific enough to allow a reader to judge whether the interpretation was warranted.

## 3.5|Content and Response Process Evidence

Content evidence concerns the relationship between the test's items and the construct domain it is intended to represent. Response process evidence concerns what respondents actually do when they answer. Both are usually established during test development, and both are frequently assumed rather than examined in clinical practice.

### Content representativeness

A test's content should sample the domain it claims to measure, in proportions that reflect the domain's structure. This is a judgement informed by expert review, task analysis and, where relevant, curriculum or diagnostic specifications. The relevant question for the assessor is not whether the items look sensible but whether the domain they sample corresponds to the construct in which the referral question is framed. A measure of "depression" that omits anhedonia, or a measure of "executive function" that samples only one executive subdomain, will produce scores that are valid for something narrower than the label suggests.

### Response processes

Response process evidence asks whether respondents are doing what the test assumes they are doing. A reading comprehension item assumes decoding and comprehension; if the respondent cannot decode, the item measures something else. A self-report item about "worry" assumes that the respondent interprets worry in the intended sense, which varies with language, culture and clinical experience. Cognitive interviewing during test development, and think-aloud studies, are the standard methods for examining these assumptions. In clinical practice the equivalent is vigilance: the assessor watches how the client engages with the task, not only what score results.

### Fairness as a content issue

Content and response process evidence are also where fairness begins. Items that presuppose knowledge, vocabulary or experience unevenly distributed across groups introduce construct-irrelevant variance, which is a validity problem before it is an equity problem. The professional standards treat fairness as integral to validity rather than as a separate consideration, and this framing is the correct one.

## 3.6|Relations with Other Variables

Evidence based on relations with other variables examines whether scores behave as the construct implies they should. It includes convergent evidence (correlation with measures of the same or related constructs), discriminant evidence (low correlation with measures of different constructs), criterion-related evidence (prediction of a current or future outcome) and nomological evidence (fit within a network of theoretical relationships).

### Convergent and discriminant evidence

A new measure of anxiety should correlate substantially with established anxiety measures and less substantially with measures of, say, working memory. The pattern, not any single coefficient, carries the argument. Where correlations with everything are moderate, the measure may be assessing general distress rather than the specific construct claimed — a common problem in clinical self-report, where negative affectivity inflates correlations among all symptom scales.

### Criterion-related evidence

Criterion-related evidence addresses the practical question directly: does the score predict the outcome the decision concerns? For diagnostic instruments, this is expressed as sensitivity, specificity and predictive values, treated in Section 3.9. For prognostic instruments, it may be expressed as the proportion of variance in outcome explained, or as the area under a receiver operating characteristic curve. Criterion evidence is highly context-specific: a coefficient obtained in a specialist clinic may not hold in primary care, because the range of presentations and the base rate differ.

### Nomological networks and incremental validity

The strongest validity arguments place a measure within a network of expected relationships derived from theory, including relationships with variables it should *not* predict. Incremental validity is a special and practically important case: does the measure add predictive accuracy beyond information already available, such as a brief interview or an existing record? Many instruments with respectable validity have negligible incremental validity, and the assessor who asks this question is doing more for their clients than the one who asks only whether the instrument is "validated".

## 3.7|Internal Structure

Internal structure evidence concerns the relationships among items and subscales: whether the measure behaves as a unidimensional scale, a set of correlated factors or a hierarchical structure with a general factor. It is usually examined by exploratory factor analysis during development and confirmatory factor analysis thereafter.

### Dimensionality and its clinical meaning

Dimensionality is not an abstract statistical property. A measure that is unidimensional yields a total score that can be interpreted as a single quantity; a measure with several weakly correlated factors yields a total score that averages different things and may obscure clinically important variation. Many symptom inventories are best modelled as hierarchical, with a general distress factor and specific factors for depression, anxiety and somatic complaints. Interpreting the total score of such a measure as though it indexed a single disorder is a common error with practical consequences for treatment selection.

### Factor analysis: uses and limits

Confirmatory factor analysis tests a specified model against observed covariance, and its fit indices are sensitive to sample size, model complexity and the distribution of items. A model that fits adequately in one sample may fail in another, and adequate fit does not establish that the model is the correct one — only that it is not incompatible with the data. Exploratory analysis, conversely, can generate a structure from noise if sample size, extraction method and rotation are chosen carelessly. The assessor reading a manual should look for replication across independent samples rather than a single well-fitting model.

### Reliability at the factor level

Where a measure has subscales, reliability should be reported for each. A total score with excellent reliability can contain a subscale with reliability so low that its interpretation is unjustified. Reporting subscale means without subscale reliability is one of the more common defects of clinical reports, and it is particularly consequential when the subscale is the basis for a recommendation.

## 3.8|Norms, Percentiles and Standard Scores

A norm is a description of the distribution of scores in a defined reference sample. It converts a raw score into a statement about relative standing, and nothing more. The sample defines the comparison, and the comparison is only as meaningful as the sample's relevance to the person being assessed.

### Score scales

Standard scores express a person's position relative to the reference sample in units of the sample's standard deviation. Table 3.2 summarises the common scales and their interpretation. Percentile ranks express the proportion of the reference sample scoring at or below the obtained score; they are intuitive but not interval-scaled, so differences between percentiles at the extremes correspond to larger differences in the underlying trait than the same differences near the middle.

### The normative sample

Manuals should report the size, recruitment method, date of data collection, stratification variables and exclusion criteria of the normative sample, together with the reliability of scores in that sample. Three questions follow. Does the sample represent the population to which the client belongs? Is it current enough that secular changes have not invalidated it? And is the sample large enough that the norm tables are stable at the extremes, where clinical decisions often fall?

### Age, education and the Flynn effect

Normed scores decline in validity as the reference data age, a phenomenon associated with secular increases in measured ability across successive cohorts (Flynn, 1987). Instruments that are not renormed may therefore over-estimate ability in later cohorts. Age and education corrections improve comparability but can also mask genuine impairment in high-functioning individuals, particularly on cognitive measures where a client's demographic prediction may exceed their obtained score despite real decline. The assessor should know which norms were applied, and should record the basis for any demographic adjustment.

## 3.9|Sensitivity, Specificity and Predictive Values

Where a score is used to classify people — as above or below a threshold, as likely or unlikely to have a condition — the relevant psychometric properties are those of the classification, not those of the score's correlation with a criterion. The four indices are sensitivity (the proportion of people with the condition who are correctly identified), specificity (the proportion without the condition who are correctly identified), positive predictive value (the proportion of positive results that are true positives) and negative predictive value (the proportion of negative results that are true negatives).

### Why predictive values matter more than sensitivity

Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test; predictive values are properties of the test *in a setting*. As Figure 3.2 shows, for an instrument with sensitivity and specificity of .90, the positive predictive value falls steeply as prevalence falls. At a prevalence of 2 per cent, fewer than one in six positive results is a true positive; at 30 per cent, more than three in four are. The same instrument, the same cut score and the same patient can therefore yield very different conclusions depending on where the assessment takes place.

### Likelihood ratios

Likelihood ratios summarise the evidential weight of a result in a form that can be combined with a pre-test probability. The positive likelihood ratio is sensitivity divided by one minus specificity; the negative likelihood ratio is one minus sensitivity divided by specificity. A positive likelihood ratio of 5 raises the odds of the condition fivefold; a negative likelihood ratio of 0.2 reduces them fivefold. Likelihood ratios have the practical advantage of being independent of prevalence, which makes them transportable across settings, and the practical disadvantage of requiring the assessor to estimate a pre-test probability from other information.

### From detection to decision

The step from detection to decision requires attention to the consequences of each kind of error. Where a false negative is dangerous and a false positive is manageable, a lower threshold is appropriate; where a false positive triggers irreversible action, a higher threshold is required, together with converging evidence. Naming the asymmetry of consequences is part of the assessor's duty, because it is the part of threshold setting that no statistic can supply.

## 3.10|Cut Scores and Classification Accuracy

Cut scores are the operational expression of a decision rule. Their selection is a judgement about the relative costs of false positives and false negatives, informed by the distribution of scores in the relevant populations and by the purpose of the classification.

### How cut scores are derived

Cut scores may be derived by criterion-keying (choosing the score that best separates groups defined by an external criterion), by receiver operating characteristic analysis (choosing the point that maximises the balance of sensitivity and specificity, or that satisfies a pre-specified constraint), or by normative convention (choosing a score at a fixed percentile). Each method embeds an assumption about what matters. Criterion-keying depends on the quality of the criterion; ROC analysis depends on the choice of the balance point; normative convention depends on the relevance of the norm group.

### Banding and its uses

Because scores carry error, many instruments report interpretive bands rather than exact values. Banding reduces over-interpretation of small differences but can create false discontinuities at band boundaries, where a one-point difference places a client in a different descriptive category. Reporting the obtained score, its confidence interval and the band it falls within is more informative than reporting the band alone.

### Classification accuracy in context

Classification accuracy statistics are conditional on the sample in which they were derived. A cut score validated in a sample with a mean score well above the clinical threshold will perform differently in a general population sample. Where local validation is impossible, the assessor should report the published accuracy statistics, the base rate in their own setting, and the resulting predictive values, and should state that the latter are estimates.

## 3.11|Fairness and Measurement Invariance

Fairness concerns the extent to which score interpretations are comparable across groups defined by characteristics irrelevant to the construct, such as sex, age, language, culture, disability or socioeconomic status. In psychometric terms, the central question is measurement invariance: whether the instrument measures the same construct in the same metric across groups.

### Levels of invariance

Configural invariance requires that the same items load on the same factors in each group. Metric (weak) invariance requires that factor loadings be equal, which permits comparison of correlations and regression coefficients. Scalar (strong) invariance requires that item intercepts also be equal, which is necessary before comparing means. A measure may show configural but not scalar invariance in a given language, in which case group differences in total scores are ambiguous — they may reflect the construct, the response style, or the translation.

### Differential item functioning

Differential item functioning occurs when two people of equal standing on the construct have different probabilities of endorsing a particular item because of group membership. It is detected by methods ranging from the Mantel–Haenszel procedure to item response theory based approaches, and its practical remedy is either item revision or interpretation of the scale with the affected items removed. The assessor consulting a manual should look for evidence of invariance across the populations in which the measure will be used, not merely across the whole normative sample.

### Translation and adaptation

Translating an instrument is not a matter of linguistic equivalence alone. The International Test Commission's guidelines require documented forward and backward translation, pilot testing, cognitive interviews and evidence of equivalence, together with attention to cultural appropriateness of item content. Where an instrument has been translated without such evidence, its scores should be interpreted with caution, and the limitation should be stated in the report. Chapter 14 extends this discussion to the broader question of culturally responsible assessment.

## 3.12|Evaluating Technical Manuals

The technical manual is the assessor's primary source for everything in this chapter. Reading it critically is a professional skill, and the criteria are reasonably straightforward once the questions are known.

### A reading protocol

A defensible protocol asks the following. What is the construct, and how is it defined? What is the normative sample, when was it collected and how was it stratified? What reliability evidence is reported, for which samples and which score levels? What validity evidence is reported, and against which criteria? Is there evidence of measurement invariance across the groups to which the client belongs? What is the standard error, and how should it be used in reporting? What are the stated limits of interpretation, and what uses does the manual explicitly disclaim?

### Common deficiencies

Common deficiencies include norming samples that are large but unrepresentative; reliabilities reported only for the total score; validity evidence confined to correlations with instruments measuring the same construct; absence of invariance evidence for the populations in which the test is marketed; and cut scores presented without their derivation or their accuracy statistics. Reading for these gaps takes less time than reading the marketing material and is considerably more useful.

### What the manual cannot tell you

Finally, the manual cannot tell the assessor whether the instrument is appropriate for the particular question, client and setting. That judgement requires clinical knowledge of the presentation, awareness of the service context and attention to the consequences of the decision. The manual constrains and informs that judgement; it does not replace it.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A rehabilitation service asks for assessment of a 47-year-old man four months after a moderate traumatic brain injury. The question is whether his reported memory difficulties reflect injury-related impairment or a depressive presentation, and what support he needs on returning to work.

**The measures.** Two memory measures are administered. The first has a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15, with an internal consistency of .93 and a standard error of measurement of 4.0; his score is 84. The second has a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, with a test–retest reliability of .81 and a standard error of 5.6; his T-score is 38. A depression inventory yields a score in the moderately severe range, and a performance validity measure is within normal limits.

**The reasoning.** The two memory scores are consistent: both lie approximately one standard deviation below the mean, and their confidence intervals overlap substantially. Neither is low enough to suggest severe amnesia. The depression score is high, and the performance validity measure reduces, though does not eliminate, the likelihood that effort accounts for the memory findings. The critical question is whether the memory scores are lower than would be expected from his premorbid level, which requires demographic estimation rather than a comparison with the population mean; his education and occupational history suggest a premorbid level in the above-average range.

**The decision.** The report states that current memory performance is below estimated premorbid level and below the normative mean, but that the confidence intervals do not permit a precise estimate of the deficit. It records depression as a probable contributor and recommends treatment before a final determination of cognitive impairment. It recommends a phased return with written instructions and reduced memory load, reviewable in three months with repeat testing, and states explicitly that a second assessment will be more informative than the first for estimating change.

**The teaching point.** The psychometrics did not produce the conclusion; they constrained it. The overlapping confidence intervals prevented an over-interpretation of the difference between two memory scores, and the demographic comparison supplied the question that the norm-referenced comparison could not answer.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Reliability and validity are properties of scores and interpretations, not of instruments. Reliability coefficients are meaningful only in relation to the source of error they address, and the standard error of measurement translates a coefficient into a statement about a single client's score. Validity is an argument assembled from content, response process, internal structure, external relations and consequences, and the assessor should be able to identify its weakest link. Norms describe relative standing in a defined sample, and their relevance depends on the sample's correspondence to the client. Sensitivity and specificity describe tests, while predictive values describe tests in settings; the base rate governs the translation, and no instrument can escape it. Finally, fairness requires evidence of measurement invariance, without which group differences in scores cannot be interpreted.
