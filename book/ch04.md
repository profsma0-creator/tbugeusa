# CHAPTER 4|Test Selection, Administration, Scoring and Interpretation

:::panel Learning Objectives
After working through this chapter, the reader should be able to:
- select instruments by reference to the referral question, the construct and the population;
- evaluate test quality, suitability and the qualifications required for competent use;
- prepare an assessment environment and session that protect the validity of results;
- administer standardised instruments and document any departure from standard procedure;
- apply scoring checks that catch the errors most likely to distort conclusions;
- recognise response styles and validity indicators without over-interpreting them;
- interpret profiles and score patterns conservatively and integrate them with other evidence.
:::

**Key terms:** incremental validity · test–user qualification · standardised administration · accommodation · scoring reliability · response style · social desirability · validity scale · profile analysis · base-rate error · computer-based assessment

## Introduction

Between the decision to assess and the interpretation of a score lies a sequence of practical choices that determine whether the score means anything at all. Which instrument, administered by whom, under what conditions, scored how, checked against what? These questions are unglamorous and decisive. Most of the serious errors in score-based assessment originate here rather than in the sophistication of the interpretation: an instrument chosen because it was available, administered in an unsuitable room, scored with an outdated norm, and interpreted without attention to the client's response style.

This chapter treats these choices as a connected whole. Selection follows from the question and the population. Administration is a validity condition that can be protected or destroyed. Scoring is a quality-control process with known failure modes. Interpretation begins with what the score is designed to measure, proceeds through a consideration of response style and comparison data, and ends with an integration that draws on evidence outside the test. The chapter closes with the two areas where practice has changed most rapidly: technology-assisted assessment and the recurring errors that experienced assessors make as often as novices.

## 4.1|Matching Measures to Referral Questions

Instrument selection is a matching problem: the properties of the measure must correspond to the requirements of the question, the characteristics of the client and the constraints of the setting. When the match is poor, no amount of interpretive skill will recover the assessment.

### Specifying the requirement

The first step is to state, in operational terms, what the instrument must deliver. A question about diagnostic status requires a measure with criterion-referenced accuracy data in a comparable population. A question about severity and change requires a measure with demonstrated sensitivity to change and a reliable change index. A question about functional capacity requires a measure of everyday performance rather than symptom report. A question about personality structure requires a measure with evidence on the relevant dimensional or categorical model. Writing the requirement down before consulting catalogues prevents the common pattern in which the available instrument defines the question.

### Breadth, depth and sequence

Where several constructs are relevant, the assessor decides the order of enquiry. A common and defensible sequence is broad screening followed by focused measurement: a general symptom inventory to identify elevations, then specific instruments to characterise the domains that matter for the decision. The sequence saves time and reduces the number of instruments administered to every client, but it must be planned so that the screening stage does not determine which hypotheses are entertained. Screening that is used as a hypothesis-generating device is efficient; screening used as a conclusion is not.

### Incremental validity and redundancy

Before adding an instrument, the assessor should ask what it will add to what is already known. Two instruments that measure the same construct with similar method variance usually add little; an instrument that measures a different construct, or the same construct by a different method, often adds a great deal. Redundancy is not always a defect — corroboration has value where the decision is consequential and the constructs are difficult to measure — but it should be a deliberate choice recorded in the rationale.

## 4.2|Evaluating Test Quality and Suitability

Having identified candidate instruments, the assessor evaluates them against a consistent set of criteria. The criteria are technical, practical and ethical at once, and Table 4.1 sets them out with the question each is designed to answer.

### Evidence beyond the manual

Independent evidence matters, because manuals are published by test developers and are not required to report unfavourable findings. Systematic reviews, independent validation studies and post-publication critiques may reveal limitations that the manual passes over. Where independent evidence is sparse, the assessor should record that fact and calibrate confidence accordingly, rather than treating the manual as a neutral summary of the literature.

### Suitability for the individual client

Suitability is a judgement about this person, not about the population. Reading level, visual and hearing acuity, motor control, stamina, language, cultural familiarity with test content and current distress all affect whether the instrument can be administered as designed. Where suitability is marginal, the assessor has three options: choose a different instrument, adapt the administration and document the adaptation as a threat to standardisation, or proceed and interpret with explicit caution. Choosing the third option silently is the error to avoid.

## 4.3|Qualifications and Scope of Practice

Test publishers classify instruments by the training required for competent use, and professional bodies treat use outside one's competence as an ethical violation regardless of what the publisher permits. The classifications are a floor, not a ceiling: possessing the credential to purchase an instrument does not establish competence to interpret it in a particular context.

### Reading the qualification framework

Most frameworks distinguish instruments that require no special qualification, those requiring a professional qualification in psychology or a related field, and those restricted to practitioners with specific training in the instrument and its interpretation. Within the third category, the relevant questions are whether the practitioner has completed the publisher's training, whether they have supervised experience with the population in question, and whether they maintain competence through continuing professional development and, where relevant, recalibration of scoring judgements.

### Boundaries around specialist measures

Certain domains carry boundary requirements that a general doctoral qualification does not satisfy: neuropsychological assessment in medico-legal contexts, forensic risk assessment, assessment for court on parenting capacity, and assessment of capacity to make specific decisions under mental health or capacity legislation. In each case the assessor must know the statutory framework, the reporting conventions and the standards of the relevant specialty. Where the referral requires expertise the assessor does not hold, the correct response is to say so and to contribute what is within competence.

### Supervision and delegation

Doctoral trainees administer instruments under supervision, and the supervisor carries responsibility for the interpretation even where the trainee conducted the session. Good supervisory practice includes review of the raw data, independent scoring of a sample of protocols, and discussion of the reasoning that connects scores to conclusions. Where psychometricians or technicians administer tests, the interpreting psychologist remains responsible for selecting the instrument, specifying the conditions of administration, and interpreting the results.

## 4.4|Preparing the Assessment Environment

The assessment environment is part of the measurement procedure. Noise, interruption, temperature, seating, lighting and the presence of observers all affect performance, and their effects are largest for tasks requiring sustained attention and for clients who are anxious or fatigued.

### Physical and interpersonal conditions

A quiet room with a table of appropriate height, seating that allows the assessor to observe without hovering, and freedom from interruption are baseline requirements. The assessor should arrange the room so that test materials are not visible before administration and so that the client cannot see scoring sheets. Where observation by a third party is required — a trainee, an interpreter, a family member — the effect on performance should be considered and, where relevant, measured, since the presence of an observer reliably changes performance on some tasks.

### Scheduling and fatigue

Cognitive assessment should be scheduled when the client is likely to be at their best, which for many people is the morning, and should avoid periods immediately after night shifts, during acute pain or in the post-ictal period. Sessions longer than ninety minutes without a break produce fatigue effects that are indistinguishable from impairment. Where a battery requires more time than a single session allows, splitting it across sessions is preferable to compressing it, and the split should be documented.

### Preparing the client

Preparation includes explaining what will happen, how long it will take, what the client should do if they need a break, and what will be done with the results. Clients should be told that some tasks will be difficult and that difficulty is expected. They should also be told, in specific terms, what will happen if they decline a task: in most instruments partial administration invalidates the score, and the client has a right to know this before deciding.

## 4.5|Standardised Administration

Standardisation is the feature that makes norms applicable. The normative sample was tested under specified conditions, and departures from those conditions mean that the comparison is no longer exact. Standardised administration is therefore not bureaucratic rigidity but the condition under which a score can be compared with a norm.

### What standardisation requires

It requires fidelity to the manual's instructions on wording, order, timing, materials, prompting, recording and discontinuation rules. It requires that the assessor knows the instructions well enough not to read them mechanically while attending to something else. And it requires that any departure be recorded at the time, with its reason, rather than reconstructed from memory afterwards.

### Common departures and their effects

The common departures are familiar: rephrasing a question to be helpful, giving extra time on a timed task, allowing a second attempt, prompting an answer, or continuing past a discontinue rule because the client seems to be improving. Each may be clinically kind and psychometrically costly. A client who is helped to a correct answer has produced a score that reflects the help as well as the ability, and the norm no longer applies. Where an adaptation is clinically necessary, the appropriate response is to record it, to state its likely direction of effect, and to interpret the score qualitatively rather than normatively.

### Recording during administration

Contemporary practice records responses verbatim where the instrument requires it, notes latencies and prompts, and records behavioural observations that bear on validity: effort, frustration, guessing, off-task behaviour, and signs of sensory or motor difficulty. These observations often explain discrepancies that would otherwise appear as profile anomalies. Digital recording, where consented to, improves accuracy and supports supervision.

## 4.6|Accommodations and Accessibility

An accommodation is a change to the standard procedure intended to remove a barrier unrelated to the construct being measured. The distinction between accommodation and modification matters: an accommodation preserves the construct, whereas a modification changes it. Giving a large-print version of a reading passage to a client with low vision is an accommodation; reading the passage aloud when the construct is reading comprehension is a modification.

### Principles

Four principles govern accommodations. They should address a documented functional limitation rather than a diagnosis. They should preserve the construct, or the change should be declared and interpreted. They should be applied consistently across the assessment so that comparisons between scores remain meaningful. And they should be documented in enough detail that a reader could reproduce the conditions.

### Common accommodations

Common accommodations include extended time, separate room, rest breaks, large print, screen magnification, sign language interpretation for instructions, written rather than verbal instructions, and alternative response formats such as pointing or dictation. Each has an evidence base of variable quality for the specific instrument, and the assessor should know whether the accommodation has been shown to preserve score meaning for that instrument, or is being applied on general principle.

### Documentation and interpretation

Where an accommodation has been applied, the report should state what was changed, why, and how the score should be interpreted as a result. Where no validated norms exist for the accommodated administration, the score should be reported as descriptive rather than norm-referenced, and the assessor should say so. This is more honest than either refusing the accommodation or presenting an accommodated score as though it were standard.

## 4.7|Scoring and Quality Control

Scoring errors are common, avoidable and consequential. Studies of hand-scored protocols have repeatedly found non-trivial error rates, and the errors are not random: they cluster where rules are complex, where reversals are required, and where the scorer is tired.

### A scoring protocol

A defensible protocol has four elements. Score from the record, not from memory, and record raw responses before applying rules. Apply the manual's rules exactly, including item-level reversal, basal and ceiling rules, and prorating rules where permitted. Score blind to the hypothesis where possible, or have a second person score a sample blind to the expected result. Recompute totals independently, either by hand or with a second entry into the scoring software, and compare.

### Checking the output

Automated scoring reduces arithmetic error but introduces its own failures: wrong normative option selected, wrong age entered, wrong test form, wrong scoring key, or software version mismatch. The assessor should verify that the demographic data entered match the client, that the correct norms were applied, and that the output is internally consistent — that subscale scores sum to totals, that percentile and standard scores correspond, and that the reported score falls within the possible range. A short checklist applied to every protocol prevents most errors.

### Reporting scores

Reports should give the instrument, the form, the date of administration, the norm group used, the obtained score with its confidence interval and percentile, and a plain-language description of what the score indicates. Where a score is described qualitatively, the descriptor should be tied to a stated range rather than to a general impression: "a score in the low-average range, between the 9th and 24th percentiles" is verifiable; "borderline functioning" is not.

## 4.8|Response Styles and Validity Indicators

Not every protocol is interpretable. Respondents may under-report symptoms to appear competent, over-report to obtain help or compensation, answer randomly, agree with everything, or misunderstand the task. Instruments with validity scales, and performance validity tests in cognitive assessment, exist to detect these patterns, and their interpretation requires as much care as the interpretation of clinical scales.

### The main response styles

Social desirability describes the tendency to present oneself favourably; it is not deception, and it is often adaptive. Malingering describes the intentional production of false or exaggerated symptoms for an external incentive; it is a clinical and legal judgement, not a test result. Over-reporting may also reflect genuine distress, help-seeking, or a personality style characterised by dramatic presentation. Random and acquiescent responding are detectable by inconsistency and infrequency indices.

### Interpreting validity indicators

Validity indicators are probabilistic. A single elevated validity scale raises a hypothesis; a pattern of elevations across independent indicators, combined with inconsistent performance on cognitive tasks, raises it further. No indicator establishes malingering, and a normal profile does not exclude exaggeration. The assessor should avoid the two symmetrical errors: dismissing every elevation as "just distress", and treating an elevation as proof of deception.

### What follows from an invalid protocol

Where validity is seriously compromised, the appropriate response is to report the finding, describe its probable effect on the scores, and avoid drawing conclusions from the affected data. Where a performance validity test is failed, the interpretation of cognitive scores should be suspended rather than adjusted. In forensic contexts, the finding itself may be material, and the assessor should be familiar with the relevant standards of evidence.

## 4.9|Interpreting Profiles and Score Patterns

Profile interpretation — the comparison of scores within a battery — is where psychometric discipline most often gives way to narrative invention. Differences between scores are interpreted as evidence of specific strengths and weaknesses, when many such differences are within the range that measurement error and normal variability would produce.

### The base-rate problem in profile analysis

The central problem is that patterns are common in the normal population. A two-point elevation on one subscale, a difference of ten points between two indices, or a combination of three moderate scores may occur in a substantial minority of people without any clinical condition. Before interpreting a pattern, the assessor should know its base rate in the relevant population. Where that is unknown, the pattern should be described as an observation rather than interpreted as a finding.

### Reliable differences and clinically meaningful differences

A difference must first be reliable (larger than the standard error of the difference) before it can be clinically meaningful. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient: a reliable difference may have no functional consequence. The assessor should ask whether the difference corresponds to something observable in the client's life — a task they avoid, a setting in which they struggle, a report from an informant. Differences that cannot be connected to functioning are usually noise with a narrative attached.

### Integrating across instruments

Scores from different instruments should be compared only when the constructs correspond. A high score on a depression inventory and a moderate elevation on a personality scale measuring negative affectivity may reflect the same underlying variance rather than two separate findings. Integration requires a model of what the instruments share, and the assessor should be explicit about which findings are independent and which are likely to be redundant.

## 4.10|Combining Tests with Other Evidence

Test scores acquire meaning in combination with interview, observation, collateral report and records. The combination is not an average but an argument: each source contributes different information, and the assessor's task is to explain how they fit, where they conflict and which source carries more weight for the specific conclusion.

### Convergence, divergence and priority

Where sources converge, confidence rises, provided the sources are genuinely independent. Where they diverge, the assessor investigates rather than averages, and the investigation usually turns on the conditions under which each source was produced: a self-report completed at home during a crisis, an informant report from a relative who sees the client weekly, a test administered after a night without sleep. Specifying those conditions often resolves the discrepancy without any need to prefer one source.

### Weighting evidence

Weighting depends on the question. For a question about current symptom severity, a standardised self-report administered recently may carry more weight than an informant's impression formed over months. For a question about lifelong functioning, informant report and records may carry more weight than a self-report completed in an acute state. For a question about capacity to perform a specific task, direct observation of the task is usually the best evidence available.

### Presenting integration in a report

Integrated reporting does not place test results in one section and interview findings in another. It organises the report around the questions the assessment was asked to answer, and within each section presents the evidence from all sources that bears on the question. This structure is harder to write and far more useful to the reader, and it makes contradictions visible rather than hiding them in separate sections.

## 4.11|Technology-Assisted Assessment

Digital administration, online questionnaires, computer-adaptive testing, ecological momentary assessment and automated scoring are now routine. Each offers advantages and introduces failure modes that the assessor must anticipate.

### Advantages and risks

Digital administration improves standardisation of timing and presentation, reduces scoring error, enables remote access for clients who cannot attend, and supports repeated measurement. The risks include uncontrolled environments, device variability, connectivity failure, item exposure, and the temptation to administer instruments outside their validated mode. A measure validated in paper format and administered online is not automatically equivalent, and equivalence should be established rather than assumed.

### Remote assessment

Remote assessment requires attention to the environment the client is in, the reliability of the connection, the client's familiarity with the technology, and the security of the platform. Cognitive tests that depend on precise timing are particularly vulnerable. Where remote administration is necessary, the assessor should record the conditions and interpret accordingly, and should avoid comparing remote scores with in-person norms unless equivalence has been demonstrated.

### Automated interpretation

Automated interpretive reports produce narrative text from score patterns. They are useful as checklists and dangerous as conclusions, because they cannot know the client, the setting or the question, and because their base-rate assumptions are opaque. An assessor who copies an interpretive paragraph into a report without verifying that each claim is supported by the data, and applicable to this client, has transferred responsibility to software. The professional obligation to justify conclusions cannot be delegated in this way.

## 4.12|Avoiding Common Interpretation Errors

The recurring errors in test interpretation are well documented and largely preventable. They are not signs of inexperience; several become more likely with confidence.

### The principal errors

Confirmatory interpretation: selecting the scores that support the initial hypothesis and discounting those that do not. Base-rate neglect: interpreting a pattern without knowing how common it is. Over-interpretation of small differences: treating scores within the standard error of the difference as meaningful. Reification: treating a label such as "executive dysfunction" as an explanation rather than a description. Context neglect: interpreting a score without reference to the conditions of administration, the client's state, or the demands of their environment. And premature closure: ceasing to gather evidence once a coherent story has formed.

### Corrective practices

The correctives are procedural. State the hypothesis before examining the scores and state what would disconfirm it. Compute and report confidence intervals. Consult base-rate tables where available. Write the limitations section before the conclusions section, so that the conclusions must accommodate them. Submit high-stakes conclusions to a colleague for independent review. Keep a record of assessments in which the eventual outcome contradicted the initial interpretation; this is the most effective calibration available to a practitioner.

### A discipline of humility

Finally, interpretation should be conducted with explicit awareness of what the instrument cannot show. A test can establish that a person's performance is below that of a reference group; it cannot, by itself, establish why, whether the difference matters in their life, or what should be done. Those conclusions require the clinical, contextual and ethical reasoning that the rest of this book describes.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A university counselling service asks for assessment of a 21-year-old undergraduate who has failed two consecutive years and reports difficulty concentrating. The question is whether a specific learning difficulty is present and whether examination accommodations are warranted.

**The assessment.** A cognitive battery yields a full-scale score in the average range, with a relative weakness in working memory and processing speed. An achievement battery shows reading accuracy and comprehension in the average range and mathematics in the low-average range. A self-report attention measure is elevated. Performance validity testing is within normal limits. The client reports sleeping five hours a night, working thirty hours a week in addition to study, and describes severe anxiety before examinations.

**The reasoning.** The profile is common in the general population: a difference between working memory and verbal comprehension of this size occurs frequently and does not by itself indicate a disorder. The achievement scores are consistent with the client's educational history and do not show the discrepancy between ability and attainment that a specific learning difficulty would require. The elevated attention self-report is confounded by sleep restriction and anxiety, and self-report attention measures are known to correlate strongly with distress. The assessment was also conducted during the week before an examination period.

**The decision.** The report declines to diagnose a specific learning difficulty, explains why the evidence does not support it, and identifies sleep restriction, workload and examination anxiety as the more probable contributors. It recommends a sleep and workload intervention, treatment for anxiety, and a re-assessment after a term if difficulties persist. On accommodations, it recommends limited, function-based provisions — a separate room and rest breaks — while stating that extended time is not supported by the evidence obtained, and that the recommendation may be revisited after reassessment.

**The teaching point.** The most useful part of the report was the explanation of what the assessment could not establish. A report that had diagnosed a learning difficulty on the basis of an elevated self-report and a common profile would have been plausible, contestable and wrong.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Selection, administration, scoring and interpretation form a chain in which each link constrains the meaning of the next. Instruments should be chosen for the question and the population, evaluated against technical and practical criteria, and used only within the assessor's competence. Standardisation is the condition under which norms apply; departures must be documented and their effects stated. Scoring requires independent checks, and automated scoring requires verification rather than trust. Response styles must be considered before clinical scales are interpreted, and validity indicators are probabilistic rather than probative. Profile interpretation should be constrained by base rates and by the standard error of the difference, and integration across sources is an argument rather than an average. Technology changes the mechanics of assessment without changing the professional obligation to justify conclusions in terms of evidence about this client.
