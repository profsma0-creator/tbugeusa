## Introduction

The assessments described in this chapter share a focus on what a person can do: academically, occupationally and in the activities of everyday life. They are frequently commissioned for decisions with immediate material consequences — examination access arrangements, special educational provision, disability benefits, occupational fitness, vocational rehabilitation — and they are therefore subject to a particular kind of pressure. The assessor is asked not only what the evidence shows but what conclusion would help.

Counselling psychologists encounter this work in educational settings, in occupational health, in vocational rehabilitation and in disability determination. The technical demands differ from clinical assessment, but the underlying discipline is the same: constructs must be defined, instruments matched to the question, norms checked for relevance, and conclusions tied to evidence. What is distinctive is the centrality of functioning and environment. A score describes a capacity; a decision requires an account of the demands the person faces and the support that would allow them to meet those demands. ## 7.1|Academic Achievement Assessment

Achievement assessment measures what a person has learned in specified domains relative to a reference group or a curriculum. Its purpose is usually to identify a learning difficulty, to establish a baseline for intervention, or to evaluate whether an intervention has worked.

### Achievement and ability are different constructs

Achievement measures developed skill; ability measures developed capacity. The distinction matters because the two are highly correlated but not interchangeable, and because some measures described as achievement tests are in fact measures of reasoning applied to academic content. The assessor should know which construct a given instrument measures, since the interpretation differs: a low achievement score with average ability suggests a skill-specific difficulty, whereas a low achievement score with low ability suggests a general learning difficulty requiring a different response.

### Curriculum-based and norm-referenced approaches

Norm-referenced achievement tests compare a student with a national or regional sample. They are useful for identifying significant under-attainment and for establishing eligibility for services, and they are insensitive to what a particular student has actually been taught. Curriculum-based measurement compares performance with expected progress through a specified curriculum and is more sensitive to short-term change and to instructional effects. The two approaches answer different questions and are complementary; an assessment that uses only one will have a blind spot.

### Assessing the conditions of learning

Achievement scores describe performance under the conditions of testing. The assessor should record the conditions of learning that produced that performance: attendance, language of instruction, teaching quality, class size, disruption, vision and hearing, and the student's own account of what they find difficult. A student who has missed a third of the school year has an attendance problem that no test can diagnose, and an assessment that fails to record this has produced a number without a meaning.

## 7.2|Reading, Writing and Mathematics

Each of the three core academic domains has a structure that determines what must be assessed and how findings translate into instruction.

### Reading

Reading comprises word-level skills (phonological awareness, decoding, sight-word recognition, fluency) and comprehension (vocabulary, inference, background knowledge, working memory). Assessment must establish which component is impaired, because the interventions differ: a decoding difficulty requires systematic phonics instruction, whereas a comprehension difficulty in a fluent decoder requires vocabulary and strategy work. A composite reading score conceals the distinction, and reporting only a composite score is a common and consequential error.

### Writing

Writing involves transcription (handwriting, spelling), text generation (idea production, organisation) and executive control (planning, revising). Difficulties at the transcription level consume resources that would otherwise serve composition, so a student with slow handwriting or poor spelling may produce weak written work despite adequate ideas. Assessment should therefore separate transcription from composition, and should include at least one task that allows the student to dictate or use a keyboard where that is the accommodation under consideration.

### Mathematics

Mathematics comprises number sense, procedural fluency, fact retrieval, problem representation and reasoning. Difficulties in fact retrieval and in procedural fluency are distinguishable from difficulties in mathematical reasoning, and the distinction predicts response to intervention. Assessment should include untimed reasoning tasks as well as timed calculation, because a student whose reasoning is intact but whose retrieval is slow requires a different accommodation from one whose reasoning is impaired. ## 7.3|Aptitude and Learning Potential

Aptitude measures predict the capacity to acquire skills under specified conditions of training or instruction. They are used in educational selection, in occupational settings and, in counselling psychology, in the assessment of learning potential for people whose educational history does not permit a straightforward interpretation of achievement scores.

### Aptitude, ability and achievement

The boundaries between the three constructs are partly conventional. A test of verbal reasoning used to predict academic success is an aptitude measure; the same test used to estimate current cognitive capacity is an ability measure. What distinguishes aptitude is the predictive purpose: the score is interpreted as a forecast rather than a description. Forecasts are probabilistic and depend on the criterion, the training conditions and the range of abilities in the group to which the forecast applies.

### Dynamic assessment and learning potential

Dynamic assessment measures the degree to which a person benefits from structured mediation, usually by comparing performance before and after a teaching phase. Its rationale is that learning potential is a better predictor of response to instruction than current attainment, particularly for people whose prior opportunities to learn have been limited. The approach has a substantial literature and a modest evidence base for incremental prediction; its principal value in counselling psychology is in assessment of people with atypical educational histories, where static scores under-estimate capacity.

### Cautions

Aptitude scores should not be used to foreclose opportunity. The prediction of a group's average performance is a poor basis for a decision about an individual, and the lower the score, the less reliable the prediction of what that individual can achieve given adequate support. Where aptitude scores are used in selection, the assessor should know the selection utility research for the specific instrument and setting, and should recommend the use of multiple predictors.

## 7.4|Career Interests and Values

Career assessment addresses the fit between a person's interests, values, abilities and the demands and rewards of occupations. It is used in career counselling, in vocational rehabilitation, in educational guidance and in occupational settings where career transitions are being planned.

### Interest inventories

Interest inventories, most of which are built on Holland's (1997) RIASEC model of realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising and conventional themes, measure the similarity between a person's pattern of interests and the patterns characteristic of occupational groups. Their predictive validity for occupational choice and satisfaction is moderate and well established. Their limitations are equally established: interests are shaped by opportunity and by gender and class socialisation; they describe preferences, not capacities; and they can be constrained by what a person knows about the world of work.

### Work values and motivators

Values inventories assess what a person wants from work: security, autonomy, recognition, service, income, work–life balance, intellectual challenge. Values predict satisfaction and turnover better than interests predict performance, and they are particularly useful in mid-career transitions where the person's priorities have changed. The assessor should treat values as context-dependent and revisable rather than as fixed traits.

### Interpreting career data

Career assessment is most useful when it broadens rather than narrows the client's options. Interpreting a profile as a prescription — "your profile indicates accountancy" — is a misuse. The appropriate interpretation identifies themes, explores how they have been expressed, considers the constraints of the local labour market, and generates options for exploration. The client's own knowledge of their circumstances should carry substantial weight.

## 7.5|Work Personality and Work Adjustment

Beyond interests and values, certain personal characteristics predict adjustment to work: conscientiousness, emotional stability, interpersonal style, tolerance for stress and capacity for co-operation. These are the substance of what is sometimes called work personality.

### Assessment approaches

Work personality is assessed through broad personality inventories, through specialised measures of work-related behaviour, through interviews about work history, and through structured references. Work history is often the most informative source and the most neglected: a pattern of short tenures, repeated conflicts with supervisors, or rapid disengagement after promotion carries more predictive weight than a trait score, provided the pattern is examined for situational explanations.

### Work adjustment and its determinants

Work adjustment is a function of the person, the job demands, the supervisory relationship, the organisational climate and the fit between them. Assessment that characterises the person without characterising the job is incomplete. Where possible, the assessor should obtain a description of the role's demands, the physical and interpersonal environment, and any changes that preceded the difficulty; occupational health services, job descriptions and supervisor accounts are the usual sources.

### Reporting work-related findings

Reports for occupational audiences should be behavioural and practical. Statements about personality traits are of limited use to a manager; statements about the conditions under which the person performs effectively, and the accommodations that would support performance, are of direct use. This is also the form of reporting least likely to stigmatise.

## 7.6|Adaptive Behaviour

Adaptive behaviour describes the conceptual, social and practical skills required for everyday functioning. It is central to the diagnosis of intellectual disability, where significant limitations in adaptive functioning are a defining requirement, and it is relevant wherever functional capacity is at issue.

### Conceptual, social and practical domains

Conceptual skills include literacy, numeracy, reasoning and self-direction. Social skills include interpersonal communication, social judgement, and the capacity to follow rules and avoid victimisation. Practical skills include personal care, domestic tasks, use of money, use of transport and occupational functioning. Assessment requires informant report, because self-report in this area is systematically unreliable, and it requires attention to the environments in which the person actually functions.

### Instruments and methods

Standardised adaptive behaviour instruments, such as the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (Sparrow, Cicchetti, & Saulnier, 2016) and the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System, are completed by an informant who knows the person well and yield standard scores by domain. Direct observation and task-based assessment supplement informant report, and are particularly useful where informants disagree or where the person lives in a highly structured setting that may mask or compensate for limitations.

### Distinguishing adaptive behaviour from ability

Adaptive behaviour and intellectual ability are correlated but distinct, and the discrepancy between them is clinically important. A person with mild intellectual disability may function independently with support; a person with average ability may have severe adaptive limitations due to autism, mental illness or institutionalisation. Diagnosis of intellectual disability requires both, and assessment of only one is a recurring source of error.

## 7.7|Functional Capacity and Daily Activities

Functional capacity assessment evaluates what a person can do in the activities of daily living, usually for the purpose of a decision about support, accommodation or eligibility. It differs from adaptive behaviour assessment in its focus on specific tasks and specific environments rather than on developmental skill level.

### Defining the activity

The first task is to define the activity precisely. "Can he manage his finances?" is not assessable; "can he pay household bills on time, keep track of a bank balance and avoid fraudulent transactions?" is. Domains commonly assessed include self-care, mobility, meal preparation, household management, medication management, financial management, communication, transport, social participation and work-related tasks.

### Methods

Functional capacity is assessed through interview, informant report, direct observation and, where possible, task performance in a realistic environment. Standardised functional measures and structured checklists improve reliability; occupational therapy assessment is often the most thorough source. Self-report is the weakest method for this purpose, since people systematically over-estimate or under-estimate their capacity, sometimes for understandable reasons.

### The capacity–performance distinction

A person may have the capacity to perform an activity but not perform it in practice, because of environmental barriers, mental state, motivation or lack of opportunity. Conversely, a person may perform an activity only because of extensive support that is invisible in the assessment. Reports should distinguish capacity, performance and support, because decisions about support depend on all three.

## 7.8|Disability and Accommodation Decisions

Psychological evidence frequently contributes to decisions about disability status, reasonable accommodations and eligibility for services. These decisions are legal and administrative, not clinical, and the assessor's role is to supply evidence relevant to the criteria rather than to make the determination.

### Knowing the framework

Each jurisdiction and institution defines disability and accommodation differently. The assessor should know the relevant definitions, the evidence required, the standard of proof and the decision-maker's criteria. Producing a clinical report that does not address the criteria is a common failure, and it is avoidable by reading the guidance before conducting the assessment.

### The accommodation question

Accommodation decisions turn on three questions: whether the person has a functional limitation relative to the demands of the setting; whether an adjustment would remove or reduce the barrier; and whether the adjustment would fundamentally alter the nature of the activity or impose disproportionate cost. The assessor can contribute evidence on the first two and should be cautious about the third, which is usually a legal or managerial judgement.

### Evidence quality

Where the decision has significant consequences, the evidence should be convergent: standardised measures, functional observation, history and, where relevant, medical documentation. Single-measure evidence is weak, and the assessor should say so rather than overstate confidence. Where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous, the report should present the ambiguity rather than resolve it in whichever direction seems kinder.

## 7.9|Career Assessment with Adults in Transition

Career transitions — redundancy, injury, migration, caring responsibilities, mid-life reassessment — are among the most common reasons for functional and vocational assessment in counselling psychology. They require an approach that integrates capacity, interest, values, context and opportunity.

### The assessment frame

Transition assessment begins with the person's own account of the change and its meaning. A forced exit from a long-held role involves loss and identity disruption that will affect how any assessment is received. The assessor should establish what the person wants, what constraints they face, what resources they have and what they have already tried, before introducing standardised measures.

### Integrating data

The integration combines three sources: the person's history of what they have done and enjoyed; standardised measures of interest, values and aptitude; and information about the labour market and training opportunities available to them. Where these conflict, the conflict is the material for counselling rather than for a verdict. A person whose interests point towards work their health cannot support requires a conversation about adaptation, not a recommendation that ignores one side of the evidence.

### Realistic optimism

The assessor's responsibility is to be realistic without being discouraging. Evidence about prognosis, labour market conditions and the effects of support should be presented honestly, together with the evidence that targeted support substantially improves return-to-work outcomes for many groups. A report that presents only constraints is as unhelpful as one that presents only encouragement.

## 7.10|Integrating Educational and Career Data

Where an assessment covers both educational and occupational domains, integration is required to prevent contradictory recommendations. The most common contradiction arises where academic recommendations assume a level of support that the occupational recommendations do not, or where career recommendations assume a capacity that the cognitive findings do not support.

### A coherence check

Before finalising such a report, the assessor should check that the recommendations are consistent with each other, consistent with the evidence, and feasible in the settings concerned. Where a recommendation depends on an assumption — that a school will provide a reader, that an employer will permit flexible hours — the assumption should be stated so that the reader can judge whether it holds.

### Involving the person and the setting

Integration is improved by involving the client and, where appropriate, the receiving setting in the planning of recommendations. A recommendation developed with a school's special educational needs coordinator, or with an employer's occupational health adviser, is more likely to be implemented than one written in isolation. This is not a compromise of independence; it is an application of the principle that assessment should be useful.

## 7.11|Ethical Use of Occupational Tests

Occupational testing raises ethical issues that are less prominent in clinical assessment because the test-taker is often not the client, the results affect selection, and the assessor may be retained by the organisation.

### Consent, feedback and access

Candidates have a right to know what is being assessed, to receive feedback in a form they can understand, and to have their data handled confidentially. These rights are frequently poorly met in selection contexts, where tests are administered at scale and results are reported only to the employer. The psychologist's obligations do not disappear because the organisation is the client; they require negotiation at the outset.

### Fairness and adverse impact

Selection tests that produce substantially different pass rates for different groups require justification on grounds of job relevance. The assessor should know whether the instrument has evidence of validity for the specific role and whether adverse impact has been examined, and should advise against instruments and cut scores that have not been justified. This is both an ethical and a legal requirement in many jurisdictions.

### Prediction, not prophecy

Finally, occupational tests predict probabilities, not destinies. A test score that predicts a 60 per cent chance of satisfactory performance says nothing definitive about an individual, and using it as a cut-off has consequences that the assessor should be prepared to defend. Where the assessor is asked to make an individual decision from group data, the appropriate response is to state the limits of the prediction and to recommend the use of additional evidence.

### Principles

A strong recommendation names the action, the agent, the setting and the review point. It distinguishes what is required from what is desirable. It is proportionate to the evidence, and it does not smuggle in clinical claims that the assessment did not establish. It is written in language the implementing party can act on without further translation.

### Implementation and review

Recommendations should be reviewed. A recommendation that has not been implemented is not evidence that the assessment was wrong, but it is information that the assessor should seek, because the reasons for non-implementation — cost, feasibility, misunderstanding — are usually remediable. Follow-up of a sample of recommendations is one of the most effective ways for a service to improve its reports.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A vocational rehabilitation service asks for assessment of a 41-year-old man who sustained a moderate traumatic brain injury two years ago in a road traffic collision. He wishes to return to work as a site electrician. The question is what support he would need and whether a return is realistic.

**The assessment.** Cognitive assessment shows high-average verbal ability, average perceptual reasoning, low-average working memory and borderline processing speed. Memory is average on recognition but weak on free recall, improving with structure. Executive measures show difficulty with planning and with inhibiting responses under time pressure. Fatigue is marked: performance declines across the session, and he reports needing two hours of rest each afternoon. He has not worked since the injury and has completed a part-time retraining course with support. Performance validity is adequate.

**The reasoning.** His technical knowledge is preserved and his recognition memory is adequate, so a return to work involving familiar procedures with written reference materials is plausible. The limiting factors are processing speed under time pressure, planning under novel conditions, and fatigue, all of which are hazardous on a construction site. A return to the full role without adjustment would place him and others at risk; a return to a modified role with supervision, written procedures and reduced hours is consistent with the evidence.

**The decision.** The report recommends a phased return over sixteen weeks, with a maximum six-hour day initially, no work at height or on live equipment until reviewed, written job specifications and a named mentor. It recommends that a risk assessment be conducted jointly with the employer, and that fatigue be monitored with a simple daily log. It states that the prognosis for a return to the full role is uncertain, and that the review at sixteen weeks should determine whether the modification becomes permanent.

**The teaching point.** The assessment's value lay in the specificity of the recommendation and in its explicit engagement with risk. A report that had said "fit for light duties" would have been unusable, and possibly dangerous.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Achievement, aptitude, adaptive behaviour and functional capacity are distinct constructs, and the assessor must match the instrument to the construct the decision requires. Academic assessment should identify the component skill that is impaired, because the instructional implication differs by component. Interest and values inventories are useful for broadening career exploration and should not be used prescriptively. Adaptive behaviour requires informant report and is conceptually distinct from ability. Functional capacity assessment must define the activity, distinguish capacity from performance and support, and engage with the legal or administrative criteria that frame the decision. Recommendations are the practical output of this work, and their quality depends on specificity, proportionality and a named review point.
