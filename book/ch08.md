# CHAPTER 8|Assessment of Mood, Anxiety, Obsessive-Compulsive, Trauma and Stress-Related Problems

## Introduction

The internalising disorders — depression, the anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder and the trauma- and stressor-related disorders — account for a large share of presentations in counselling psychology services. They are also the group for which the evidence base for assessment is strongest: there are well-validated diagnostic interviews, severity measures with published norms, and a substantial literature on measurement-based care.

That strength carries a risk. Because these conditions are familiar, assessment is often conducted by pattern recognition, and because validated questionnaires are widely available, scores are often used as diagnoses. This chapter argues for a more disciplined approach: structured differential reasoning that considers the neighbouring conditions, careful attention to the boundary between disorder and ordinary distress, and the use of severity measures for monitoring and comparison rather than for labelling.

## 8.1|Assessing Depressive Presentations

Depression is the most common presentation in most adult mental health services and the one most often assessed inadequately. The core features — persistent low mood and loss of interest or pleasure — are widely known; what distinguishes a competent assessment is attention to course, severity, subtype, differential and risk.

### Core features and their assessment

Low mood must be distinguished from the reactive sadness of ordinary disappointment, and anhedonia from the temporary withdrawal that follows loss. The assessor establishes pervasiveness across contexts, persistence across the day, and the presence of biological features: early waking, appetite change, psychomotor change and diurnal variation. These features have diagnostic and prognostic significance: early waking with diurnal worsening of mood suggests a melancholic picture, which responds differently to treatment from a reactive depression with preserved reactivity.

### Course and subtype

Course questions establish whether the presentation is a first episode, a recurrence or a chronic state. The distinction between a major depressive episode superimposed on persistent depressive disorder and a first episode matters for prognosis and for the length of treatment recommended. Subtype questions — melancholic, atypical with hypersomnia and leaden paralysis, psychotic, seasonal, peripartum, catatonic — carry implications for treatment choice and for risk.

### Severity and functional impact

Severity should be established by symptom count, functional impact and risk, not by a questionnaire score alone. A client with moderate symptoms who has stopped working, is neglecting self-care and has begun drinking heavily has a severe presentation whatever the score suggests. Severity measures are useful as a baseline for monitoring and as a check on clinical impression; they are not a substitute for either.

### Differential and comorbidity

The differential includes bipolar disorder, substance-induced mood disorder, adjustment disorder, grief, thyroid disease and anaemia, medication effects, and the negative symptoms of schizophrenia. Bipolar disorder is the most consequential item to miss, and missing it can lead to harmful treatment. Any history of elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, increased goal-directed activity or a family history of bipolar disorder requires specific enquiry, ideally with informant corroboration and prospective mood charting.

## 8.2|Assessing Anxiety Presentations

Anxiety disorders are heterogeneous, frequently comorbid with each other, and easily confused in unstructured assessment. The assessor's task is to identify the specific fear structure rather than to record a general elevation of anxiety.

### The specific phobic and anxiety presentations

Panic disorder is characterised by recurrent unexpected panic attacks and persistent concern about further attacks or their consequences. Agoraphobia is characterised by fear of situations from which escape might be difficult or help unavailable. Social anxiety disorder involves fear of negative evaluation in social or performance situations. Generalised anxiety disorder involves excessive, difficult-to-control worry across multiple domains, accompanied by arousal symptoms. Separation anxiety disorder and selective mutism are relevant in younger populations.

### Establishing the fear structure

For each presentation, the assessor establishes what is feared, what triggers it, what the person does to prevent or escape it, and what the consequences are. This information is more useful than a severity score, because it determines the treatment target. A client who fears the physical sensations of panic requires interoceptive work; a client who fears negative evaluation requires behavioural experiments in social situations; a client who fears that worry prevents disaster requires metacognitive work.

### Generalised anxiety and its boundary with worry

Generalised anxiety disorder is the most difficult of the anxiety disorders to assess reliably, because worry is a universal experience and the boundary between normal and pathological worry is quantitative rather than qualitative. The discriminating features are uncontrollability, pervasiveness across domains, duration of at least six months, and associated symptoms. The assessor should also consider whether the worry is secondary to another condition, such as health anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder or a depressive episode with ruminative thinking.

## 8.3|Panic and Agoraphobic Symptoms

Panic attacks are discrete episodes of intense fear accompanied by somatic and cognitive symptoms that peak within minutes. They are a feature of several disorders and are not a diagnosis in themselves; the diagnostic question concerns the pattern and the person's response to it.

### Assessing the attack

A careful description establishes the symptoms present, their speed of onset, their peak intensity, their duration and the person's interpretation of them. The interpretation is central: a person who believes that palpitations signify a heart attack has a different maintaining mechanism from one who believes they are going to faint. The assessor should also establish whether attacks are unexpected, situationally bound or situationally predisposed, since this distinction shapes diagnosis.

### Panic disorder with and without agoraphobia

The distinction between panic disorder and agoraphobia is not merely a matter of symptom count. Agoraphobic avoidance can occur without panic, and panic can occur without avoidance. Where avoidance is present, the assessor should map the avoided situations, the safety behaviours used, and the beliefs that sustain them. This map is the treatment plan in embryo.

### Medical differential

Panic symptoms overlap with cardiac, respiratory, endocrine and neurological conditions, and with the effects of caffeine, stimulants and withdrawal states. A first presentation of panic in later life, panic with atypical features, or panic unresponsive to treatment warrants medical review. The assessor should record what medical evaluation has occurred and what remains outstanding.

## 8.4|Obsessions and Compulsions

Obsessive-compulsive disorder is defined by obsessions (intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges) and compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to reduce distress or prevent a feared outcome). It is frequently misdiagnosed as generalised anxiety, as depression, or as a personality characteristic such as perfectionism.

### Distinguishing obsessions from ordinary intrusive thoughts

Intrusive thoughts are near-universal; what distinguishes clinical obsessions is their frequency, their appraisal as significant and threatening, the distress they cause, and the effort expended to neutralise them. The assessor should ask whether the person believes the thought is meaningful about them (thought–action fusion), whether they attempt to suppress or neutralise it, and how much time it consumes.

### Assessing compulsions, including mental rituals

Compulsions are not always visible. Mental rituals — counting, praying, reviewing, replacing a thought with a safe thought, mentally checking — are common and easily missed. The assessor should ask directly about covert rituals and should ask about avoidance, since avoidance is functionally equivalent to a compulsion and often more disabling.

### Subtypes, insight and related presentations

Subtypes include contamination, symmetry and ordering, forbidden or taboo thoughts, and hoarding, which is classified separately. The assessor should record the degree of insight: many people with OCD recognise that their fears are excessive, and a minority do not, which affects both diagnosis and engagement. Body dysmorphic disorder, trichotillomania, skin-picking disorder and olfactory reference syndrome are related presentations that should be screened where relevant.

## 8.5|Trauma Exposure and Post-Traumatic Symptoms

Trauma-related assessment requires attention to what happened, how the person has responded, and what the current symptom profile is. It also requires a discipline that is often absent: distinguishing the presence of trauma from the presence of post-traumatic stress disorder, since most people who experience trauma do not develop the disorder.

### Establishing exposure

Exposure is established by asking about specific categories of event — serious accidents, physical or sexual assault, combat, natural disaster, sudden bereavement, childhood abuse, witnessing violence, and events experienced in a professional capacity — with attention to the person's own definition of what was traumatic. The assessor should record the nature of the event, its duration, the person's age at the time, the degree of threat to life or bodily integrity, and whether the event was interpersonal, since interpersonal trauma carries different prognostic and relational implications.

### The symptom clusters

DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 organise post-traumatic symptoms differently. The DSM-5-TR clusters are intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and alterations in arousal and reactivity, with a dissociative subtype; ICD-11 uses a narrower set of core symptoms (re-experiencing, avoidance and a sense of current threat) with optional disturbances in affect regulation, self-concept and relationships. The assessor should know which framework is being applied, because the two produce different thresholds for the same presentation.

### Differential and comorbidity

The differential includes acute stress disorder, adjustment disorder, complex post-traumatic stress disorder, dissociative disorders, borderline personality disorder, psychotic disorders with trauma-related content, and traumatic brain injury. Comorbidity with depression, substance use and chronic pain is the rule. Assessment should establish which condition is primary for treatment purposes, rather than assuming that all symptoms are trauma-related because a trauma is present.

## 8.6|Stress and Adjustment Problems

Adjustment disorder describes a maladaptive response to an identifiable stressor, with symptoms that do not meet criteria for another disorder, beginning within three months of the stressor and resolving within six months of its termination. Its inclusion in both DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 has been controversial, and its boundaries are genuinely uncertain.

### The clinical problem

The central difficulty is that adjustment disorder is defined by exclusion: it applies when symptoms are clinically significant but do not meet criteria for another condition. This makes it both common and vulnerable to over-use, particularly in services where a diagnosis is required for access and the presentation is subthreshold. The assessor should therefore be explicit about which criteria are met and which conditions have been excluded, rather than using adjustment disorder as a default.

### Distinguishing disorder from distress

The distinction between an understandable reaction and a disorder rests on severity, functional impairment, duration and the presence of symptoms beyond those expected for the stressor. Grief is the paradigm case: the DSM-5-TR removed the former bereavement exclusion from major depression, while retaining a category of prolonged grief disorder for reactions that persist and impair. The assessor should not pathologise normal grief, and should not dismiss persistent, impairing grief as normal.

### Assessment tasks

Assessment establishes the nature and timing of the stressor, the person's appraisal of it, the symptoms, the functional impact, the available supports, and the person's own understanding. It should also establish what the person has already tried and what they believe would help, since adjustment difficulties often respond to brief, practical intervention.

## 8.7|Somatic Overlap and Medical Explanations

Many presentations in this chapter include prominent somatic symptoms, and some are better explained by a medical condition. The assessor's obligation is to consider medical explanations, to record what has been excluded, and to avoid attributing to psychology what has not been medically evaluated.

### Somatic presentations

Somatic symptom disorder, illness anxiety disorder, functional neurological symptoms and pain syndromes overlap substantially with anxiety and depression. The assessor should establish the history of medical investigation, the person's beliefs about their symptoms, the degree of functional impairment, and the pattern of health-care use. Where symptoms are disproportionate to identifiable disease, the assessment should address the psychological maintaining factors without implying that the symptoms are imaginary.

### Medical mimics and contributors

Thyroid disease, anaemia, vitamin deficiency, sleep apnoea, chronic pain, medication effects, substance withdrawal and neurological conditions can all present with depressive or anxiety symptoms. The assessor should ask about recent investigations and should recommend medical review where symptoms are atypical, refractory or associated with physical signs. A collaborative relationship with the referring physician is the most efficient safeguard.

## 8.8|Severity Measures and Symptom Monitoring

Standardised severity measures improve the reliability of monitoring and support measurement-based care, in which treatment decisions are informed by repeated measurement rather than by clinical impression alone. Their value depends on using them correctly.

### Selecting a measure

The instrument should match the construct, the population and the purpose. Widely used examples include the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 for depression (Kroenke, Spitzer, & Williams, 2001), the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 for anxiety (Spitzer, Kroenke, Williams, & Löwe, 2006), the Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale for OCD, and the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5. Each has published norms, cut scores and, in some cases, reliable change indices. The assessor should know the instrument's accuracy in the population being assessed, which frequently differs from the population in which it was validated.

### Using measures for monitoring

For monitoring, the relevant properties are sensitivity to change, test–retest reliability in stable clients, and a reliable change index. A change that exceeds the reliable change index is unlikely to be measurement error; a change that does not may still be clinically meaningful, and the assessor should combine the score with functional information. Weekly or fortnightly administration is typical in treatment monitoring; the same instrument should be used throughout to permit comparison.

### Avoiding the score-as-diagnosis error

A severity score is not a diagnosis. Scores above a cut-off indicate probable caseness and warrant diagnostic assessment; scores below it do not exclude significant difficulty, particularly where the instrument does not cover the person's presentation. The assessor should report scores with their confidence intervals, their percentile or severity band, and their date, and should never present a score as the basis for a diagnosis without a diagnostic interview.

## 8.9|Functional Analysis of Avoidance

Avoidance is the most important maintaining process across the internalising disorders. A functional analysis of avoidance identifies the situations avoided, the behaviours used to avoid or escape them, the short-term consequences that reinforce the avoidance, and the long-term costs that sustain the disorder.

### The analysis

The analysis proceeds through four questions. What is the feared outcome? In which situations is it expected? What does the person do to prevent or escape it? What happens afterwards? The answers yield a formulation in which the avoidance is not a symptom to be removed but a solution that has become the problem: it reduces anxiety immediately and prevents disconfirmation in the long run.

### Safety behaviours and subtle avoidance

Avoidance includes not only overt non-participation but subtle safety behaviours: gripping a handrail, carrying medication, rehearsing sentences, sitting near the exit, drinking alcohol before social events. These behaviours are often not reported unless specifically asked about, because they are experienced as sensible precautions rather than as avoidance. Their identification is essential, since treatment requires them to be dropped.

### From analysis to plan

The functional analysis translates directly into a treatment plan: graded exposure to the avoided situations, elimination of safety behaviours, behavioural experiments designed to test the feared outcome, and monitoring of the predictions and their outcomes. Where the analysis cannot be conducted — because the client is unable to describe the pattern, or because the presentation is too acute — the assessment should record that and specify what needs to be established before treatment planning can proceed.

## 8.10|Differential Diagnosis Across Internalising Problems

The internalising disorders overlap substantially in symptoms, in comorbidity and in underlying temperament. Distinguishing among them requires attention to the specific content of fear and to the temporal pattern, rather than to symptom counts.

### The common differentials

The most frequently confused pairs are generalised anxiety and major depression (both characterised by worry, sleep disturbance and fatigue); panic disorder and generalised anxiety (both involve arousal symptoms); social anxiety and avoidant personality disorder (both involve social fear and avoidance); obsessive-compulsive disorder and generalised anxiety (both involve repetitive worry); and post-traumatic stress disorder and adjustment disorder (both follow stressors).

### Discriminating features

The discriminating features are usually specific: the presence of unexpected panic attacks; the presence of obsessions and compulsions; the presence of re-experiencing and avoidance of trauma reminders; the presence of a stable, lifelong pattern of social fear beginning in adolescence; the temporal relationship to a stressor. ### Comorbidity and hierarchy

Where criteria for several disorders are met, the assessor should record all of them rather than choosing one, and should identify which is primary for treatment. Diagnostic hierarchies — rules that give one disorder precedence — have been largely removed from the current classifications because they obscured clinically important comorbidity, and their removal requires the assessor to reason about priority explicitly.

## 8.11|Comorbidity and Diagnostic Hierarchy

Comorbidity is the norm in this group. Depression with anxiety, anxiety with substance use, OCD with depression, and PTSD with all of the above are common patterns rather than exceptions. The assessor's task is not to reduce the diagnoses to one but to establish which condition should be treated first and why.

### Reasons for comorbidity

Comorbidity arises from shared risk factors (neuroticism, childhood adversity, genetic liability), from causal relationships (anxiety leading to depression, trauma leading to substance use), from overlapping diagnostic criteria, and from help-seeking patterns that bring comorbid people into services. Distinguishing these mechanisms matters for treatment: where one condition causes another, treating the cause may resolve both; where they are independent, both may require treatment.

### Establishing priority

Priority is established by severity, by functional impact, by the client's own goals, by the presence of risk, and by the evidence on sequencing. Where depression is severe and anxiety moderate, treating depression first is usually appropriate; where panic is preventing engagement in treatment, treating panic first may be necessary. The reasoning should be recorded, because the next clinician will need to know why the order was chosen.

### The single-diagnosis temptation

Services that require a single diagnosis for access create pressure to record only one condition. The assessor should resist this where the evidence supports comorbidity, and should record the additional diagnoses in a way that the service can accommodate — for example, as secondary diagnoses or as clinical formulation. Deliberately omitting a diagnosis to satisfy an administrative category is a misrepresentation of the evidence.

## 8.12|Integrated Formulation and Feedback

The output of assessment in this group is a formulation that links the presentation to its development, its maintenance and its treatment. It should explain why this person, at this time, has developed this particular pattern, and it should identify the points at which intervention is most likely to be effective.

### Structure of the formulation

A workable structure moves through predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors, and then specifies the maintaining mechanisms. The mechanisms should be stated in a form that predicts treatment response: if avoidance is the principal maintaining process, exposure should reduce symptoms; if rumination is maintaining depression, rumination-focused work should help.

### Feedback

Feedback should present the formulation as a shared account rather than a verdict, use the client's own language where possible, invite correction, and connect the formulation to the treatment options. Where the assessment has identified a condition the client did not expect — bipolar disorder, OCD, PTSD — the feedback should include information about the condition, its treatability and the rationale for the proposed approach. Where it has excluded a condition the client expected, the exclusion should be explained rather than simply recorded.

### Monitoring and review

The assessment should end with a monitoring plan: which measures will be repeated, at what intervals, and what change would prompt a revision of the formulation or the treatment. Measurement-based care is only as good as the arrangement that sustains it, and the assessment report is the natural place to establish that arrangement.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 26-year-old postgraduate student is referred by her general practitioner with "anxiety and low mood". She reports six months of difficulty sleeping, poor concentration and reluctance to attend seminars.

**The assessment.** Interview establishes that the difficulty began after a panic attack in a lecture theatre eight months earlier. She now avoids lecture theatres, travels by a longer route that avoids the underground, and has stopped attending seminars. She carries water at all times and sits near exits. She has not had a second full panic attack, but experiences near-attacks several times a week. She describes low mood and anhedonia over the last three months, and scores in the moderately severe range on a depression measure and the moderate range on an anxiety measure. She reports no elevated mood, no obsessions or compulsions, and no trauma history. She drinks alcohol only socially.

**The reasoning.** The presentation is best explained by panic disorder with agoraphobic avoidance, with a secondary depressive episode that developed as the avoidance restricted her life. The reverse order — primary depression with secondary anxiety — is less consistent with the clear onset at a panic attack and the specific situational avoidance. The near-attacks and safety behaviours indicate that the panic disorder remains active despite the absence of full attacks. The depression is clinically significant and would need treatment in its own right if it persisted after the panic was addressed.

**The decision.** The report records panic disorder with agoraphobia and a moderate depressive episode, identifies the avoidance and safety behaviours as the principal maintaining processes, and recommends a course of cognitive behavioural therapy with interoceptive and situational exposure, alongside monitoring of mood. It recommends review at eight weeks, with a decision about separate treatment for depression if mood has not improved as the avoidance reduces.

**The teaching point.** The presenting complaint was "anxiety and low mood"; the assessment found a specific, treatable fear structure. The functional analysis of avoidance generated the treatment plan, and the diagnostic sequence determined which condition to treat first.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Assessment of the internalising disorders requires specific differential reasoning rather than general enquiry. Depression must be assessed for course, subtype, severity and bipolarity, and bipolar disorder must be actively excluded. Anxiety presentations are distinguished by their fear structure, which also determines the treatment target. Obsessive-compulsive disorder is defined by obsessions and by rituals that are frequently covert. Trauma exposure is not equivalent to post-traumatic stress disorder, and assessment must establish both. Adjustment disorder is defined by exclusion and is vulnerable to over-use. Severity measures support monitoring but do not establish diagnosis. The functional analysis of avoidance links assessment to treatment, and the integrated formulation should state maintaining mechanisms in a form that predicts response.
