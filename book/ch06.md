## Introduction

Cognitive assessment addresses a distinctive class of questions: what can this person do with their intellectual resources, how does that capacity compare with what would be expected for them, and what does the difference imply for their daily functioning, education or work? These questions matter in developmental assessment, in neurological and psychiatric conditions affecting cognition, in educational and occupational decisions, and in legal contexts where capacity or injury is at issue.

The technical demands are high. Cognitive measures have the most elaborate normative structures in psychology, the strongest evidence on reliability, and the most developed methods for estimating change. They are also the measures most vulnerable to misuse: a low score can be read as a diagnosis, a discrepancy as a deficit, and a profile as an explanation, when none of these inferences is warranted without context. This chapter sets out the theory, the methods and the interpretive discipline required to make cognitive findings both accurate and useful. ## 6.1|Questions Addressed by Cognitive Assessment

Cognitive assessment answers four broad types of question, and the assessor should know which is being asked before selecting an instrument.

### Descriptive questions

Descriptive questions concern the person's current level of performance relative to a reference group: how does this child's reading accuracy compare with others of the same age; how does this adult's processing speed compare with the general population? Descriptive questions are the easiest to answer and the most often confused with the other three.

### Diagnostic and aetiological questions

Diagnostic questions ask whether a recognised condition is present: intellectual disability, a specific learning disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, a neurocognitive disorder, or an acquired brain injury. Aetiological questions ask what caused an observed impairment. Cognitive tests contribute to both, but rarely settle either alone: a test score is compatible with many causes, and diagnosis usually requires history, course, medical investigation and functional evidence.

### Change and monitoring questions

Change questions ask whether functioning has altered: has cognition declined since a previous assessment; is treatment affecting attention; is rehabilitation producing recovery? These questions require instruments with demonstrated sensitivity to change, reliable change indices, and either a previous assessment or a well-justified estimate of premorbid functioning.

### Functional and decision questions

Functional questions ask what the person can do in their own environment, and decision questions ask what should follow: what support is needed at school, whether the person can manage their affairs, whether they can return to work. Cognitive tests inform these questions but do not answer them directly. The relationship between test performance and everyday functioning — ecological validity — is moderate at best, and the assessor must supplement test data with behavioural and informant evidence.

## 6.2|Theories of Intellectual Functioning

Interpretation of cognitive scores presupposes a model of what intelligence is. Three families of model dominate, and each carries different implications for how a profile should be read.

### The general factor

Spearman's observation that performance on seemingly diverse cognitive tasks is positively correlated led to the concept of a general factor, *g*, which accounts for a substantial proportion of variance across tasks. The finding has been replicated many times and remains one of the most robust in differential psychology. Its interpretive implication is conservative: because all cognitive tasks share variance with *g*, differences between tasks are smaller than they appear, and a profile of strengths and weaknesses may reflect measurement noise more than cognitive structure.

### Hierarchical models

The Cattell–Horn–Cattell model proposes a hierarchy in which *g* sits above broad abilities — fluid reasoning, crystallised knowledge, visual–spatial processing, auditory processing, short-term working memory, long-term storage and retrieval, and processing speed — which in turn sit above narrow abilities. This model underpins most contemporary intelligence tests, and it supplies the vocabulary in which profiles are described. Its practical contribution is the identification of broad domains that have distinct developmental trajectories, distinct neural substrates and distinct implications for learning.

### Alternative and process accounts

Alternative accounts emphasise processing rather than structure. The process overlap theory of Kovacs and Conway (2016) explains positive correlations among diverse tasks through the overlap of executive processes they recruit, rather than through a single underlying capacity; other accounts emphasise working memory capacity, relational processing or neural efficiency. These models are not yet the basis of clinical instruments, but they matter for interpretation: they caution against treating a broad-ability score as a thing, and they direct attention to the processes that produce performance.

### Implications for the assessor

The practical implications are threefold. First, broad scores should be reported and interpreted before narrow ones, because they are more reliable and account for more variance. Second, profile differences should be interpreted only where they exceed the standard error of the difference and where there is corroborating functional evidence. Third, the choice of model should be stated, because instruments built on different models produce scores that are not directly comparable.

## 6.3|Interpreting Cognitive Profiles

Profile interpretation is the most contested practice in cognitive assessment. The idea that a pattern of relative strengths and weaknesses identifies a specific condition has intuitive appeal and limited empirical support.

### The discrepancy approach and its limits

Discrepancy analysis compares a person's attainment or domain scores with an estimate of their general ability. The approach is used in the identification of specific learning disorders, where the defining feature is attainment substantially below the level predicted by ability, and in the identification of cognitive decline, where current performance is compared with estimated premorbid functioning. Its limitations are well known: the comparison is between two fallible estimates, so the standard error of the difference applies to the discrepancy as much as to either score; the base rate of discrepancies in the general population is high; and the decision about how large a discrepancy must be is a policy choice rather than an empirical discovery.

### Estimated premorbid functioning

Estimating premorbid ability is necessary whenever decline is suspected. Three methods are available: demographic estimation from age, sex, education and occupation; reading-based estimation using tests of irregular word reading, which are relatively resistant to neurological decline; and best-performance estimation using the highest score obtained across the history. Each has limitations: demographic estimates are imprecise at the individual level; reading-based estimates are invalid in people with pre-existing reading difficulties, non-native speakers or limited education; and best-performance estimates assume that the person's best performance was genuinely their optimum.

### Base rates and the clinical significance of profiles

The base-rate problem is acute here. A difference of one standard deviation between verbal and visuospatial ability, or between a full-scale score and a single index, occurs in a substantial proportion of the healthy population. Before interpreting such a difference as a finding, the assessor should consult published base-rate tables for the instrument and should ask whether the pattern corresponds to something observable: a task the person avoids, a domain in which they have always struggled, or a functional consequence that others have noticed.

## 6.4|Attention and Processing Speed

Attention is not a single capacity but a family of processes: alerting and arousal, sustained attention or vigilance, selective attention, divided attention and attentional control. Processing speed describes the rate at which cognitive operations are performed. Together they underpin almost every other cognitive task, which is why their assessment must precede the interpretation of domain-specific scores.

### Distinguishing the components

Sustained attention is assessed by continuous performance tasks requiring detection of infrequent targets over time. Selective attention is assessed by tasks requiring suppression of distractors, such as Stroop-type procedures. Divided attention is assessed by dual-task paradigms. Processing speed is assessed by simple and choice reaction time tasks, symbol substitution and timed coding. Because these components are separable, a general complaint of "poor concentration" requires specification before it can be assessed.

### Confounds

Performance on attention tasks is exquisitely sensitive to sleep, pain, medication, anxiety, depression, motivation and substance use. A single assessment conducted during a period of sleep restriction cannot distinguish an attention disorder from the effects of sleep restriction, and the assessor should establish sleep and substance history before interpreting any attention score. Effort and engagement must be evaluated with performance validity measures, because attention tasks are the easiest to underperform deliberately.

### Reporting attention findings

Reports should specify which component was impaired, under what conditions, with what confidence, and with what functional consequence. "Sustained attention was reduced relative to estimated premorbid functioning; the client reports difficulty reading for more than ten minutes and has stopped driving on motorways" is a finding with implications. "Attention deficits" is a label without one.

## 6.5|Learning and Memory

Memory assessment distinguishes encoding, storage and retrieval, and separates verbal from visual material and immediate from delayed recall. Recognition procedures distinguish retrieval failure from storage failure, and interference paradigms examine susceptibility to proactive and retroactive interference.

### Domains and methods

Verbal learning is typically assessed by list-learning tasks that measure acquisition across trials, recall after interference, and recognition. Visual memory is assessed by reproduction or recognition of figures. Working memory is assessed by digit span, spatial span and complex span tasks that require simultaneous storage and processing. Prospective memory — remembering to do something in the future — is often more relevant to everyday functioning than retrospective memory and is often omitted from batteries.

### Interpreting memory scores

Three interpretive questions structure the analysis. Is the memory difficulty disproportionate to the person's overall cognitive level, or part of a general decline? Is it material-specific, affecting verbal but not visual memory, which may suggest lateralised dysfunction and warrants medical referral? Is it a retrieval problem, in which case cues improve performance, or an encoding problem, in which case they do not? Each question has implications for both aetiology and rehabilitation.

### Functional relevance and everyday memory

Laboratory memory tests predict everyday memory complaints weakly. The assessor should therefore supplement them with self-report and informant measures of everyday memory, and with behavioural observation during the assessment, such as whether the client remembers instructions across tasks. Where test performance and everyday functioning diverge, the divergence is itself a finding that requires explanation, often in terms of strategy use, environmental support or emotional factors.

## 6.6|Executive Functions

Executive functions are the supervisory processes that organise goal-directed behaviour: inhibition, shifting, updating of working memory, planning, monitoring and emotional regulation. They are among the most ecologically important cognitive functions and among the most difficult to measure.

### The measurement problem

The central difficulty is that executive function is defined by the demands of a task rather than by its content, and structured test settings supply exactly the structure that executive function is required to provide in everyday life. A client may perform normally on a formal planning task in a quiet room while being unable to organise a shopping trip. This is the "executive paradox": laboratory measures of executive function correlate modestly with everyday executive behaviour, and informant ratings often predict real-world outcomes better than tests do.

### Assessment strategy

A defensible strategy combines three sources: performance-based tests of specific executive components; informant-rated questionnaires of everyday executive behaviour, completed by someone who knows the client well; and observation of the client's behaviour during the assessment itself, including their ability to follow multi-step instructions, to shift between tasks, and to manage frustration. The convergence of these sources, or the absence of convergence, is the finding.

### Reporting executive findings

Because executive difficulties are most visible in unstructured, novel or emotionally demanding situations, reports should describe the conditions under which the difficulty appears rather than stating a global deficit. This has direct implications for recommendations, which should focus on external structure, environmental modification and routines rather than on the client's effort.

## 6.7|Language and Visuospatial Skills

Language and visuospatial ability are the two broad domains most often lateralised and most often affected differentially by neurological events. Their assessment contributes both to diagnosis and to rehabilitation planning.

### Language

Language assessment covers naming, fluency, comprehension, repetition, reading and writing. In counselling psychology settings, the most common reasons for assessing language are suspected neurodevelopmental language disorder, suspected aphasia after stroke or injury, and the need to establish whether a client can comprehend an instrument administered in the test language. Brief measures of verbal fluency and naming are sensitive to a range of conditions and are worth including in most cognitive batteries.

### Visuospatial and constructional skills

Visuospatial assessment covers visual perception, spatial judgement, construction, visual memory and, in some batteries, navigation and neglect. Copying tasks, block design, judgement of line orientation and spatial span are standard methods. Difficulties may reflect primary visual impairment, which should be excluded by a vision check, or constructional apraxia, or a broader non-verbal learning difficulty.

### The verbal–non-verbal discrepancy

The discrepancy between verbal and visuospatial performance is commonly interpreted as evidence of a non-verbal learning disability or of right-hemisphere dysfunction. The interpretation is weak when based on a single administration and strong only when the discrepancy is large, stable, corroborated by functional evidence and consistent with history. Where the discrepancy reflects an artefact of language dominance in a multilingual client, the error can be substantial.

## 6.8|Neuropsychological Screening

Not every assessment requires a full neuropsychological battery. Screening instruments exist to identify clients who need fuller assessment, to monitor change, and to answer simple questions efficiently.

### Screening versus assessment

A screen is a brief procedure with high sensitivity and moderate specificity, designed to ensure that no significant case is missed. It is not designed to characterise a profile or to establish a diagnosis. Instruments such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (Nasreddine et al., 2005) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (Folstein, Folstein, & McHugh, 1975) are widely used screens; both are affected by education, language and culture, and both have limited sensitivity to executive and visuospatial impairment in high-functioning people.

### When to refer

Referral for full neuropsychological assessment is indicated where a screen is abnormal, where cognitive complaints are accompanied by functional decline, where a neurological condition is suspected, where a decision with significant consequences (capacity, fitness, guardianship) depends on cognitive status, and where the client's presentation is inconsistent with the available information. The assessor should state the reason for referral explicitly, since a vague referral produces a vague assessment.

## 6.9|Performance Validity

Performance validity refers to the extent to which a person's performance on cognitive testing reflects their true ability. It is distinct from response bias on self-report questionnaires, and it must be assessed in any context where secondary gain is plausible and, increasingly, in all cognitive assessments as a matter of routine.

### The rationale

Performance validity tests are designed to be failed by almost no one with genuine cognitive impairment of the severity being assessed, but to be failed by a substantial proportion of people who are deliberately underperforming. Their logic rests on forced-choice paradigms, in which the probability of scoring below chance can be calculated, and on embedded indices within standard tests that detect improbable patterns of performance.

### Interpretation

Interpretation requires attention to the base rate of invalid performance in the relevant setting, which ranges from near zero in routine clinical samples to substantial proportions in medico-legal and disability evaluation contexts. A single failed measure is generally insufficient; current consensus statements recommend multiple indicators and consideration of the overall pattern (Sweet et al., 2021). Importantly, invalid performance does not establish malingering: it establishes only that the scores cannot be interpreted as estimates of ability.

### What the assessor must report

Where validity is compromised, the assessor must state which measures were failed, the base rate of such failure in comparable samples, the probable effect on other scores, and the limits this imposes on any conclusion. Failing to report an invalid performance, or interpreting cognitive scores despite it, is a serious professional error.

## 6.10|Medical and Neurological Context

Cognitive findings cannot be interpreted apart from medical and neurological information. The same score carries different implications depending on whether it was obtained before or after a neurological event, during or between episodes of illness, on or off medication, and in the presence or absence of pain and sleep disturbance.

### Conditions that shape cognitive findings

Relevant conditions include traumatic brain injury, stroke, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, dementia and other neurodegenerative conditions, HIV, thyroid disease, vitamin deficiencies, chronic pain, sleep apnoea, and the cognitive effects of psychiatric disorders themselves. Medications with cognitive side effects are common, particularly anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, anticonvulsants and some antidepressants. Substance use, both acute and chronic, affects nearly every domain assessed.

### Integrating medical information

The assessor should obtain the medical history, current diagnoses, medication list, results of neuroimaging and other investigations where available, and the chronology relating medical events to cognitive change. Where such information is unavailable, the report must say so, and conclusions about aetiology should be correspondingly cautious. The boundary of competence is important: the identification of a neurological condition is a medical judgement, and the psychologist's contribution is the characterisation of cognitive and functional consequences.

### Medical red flags

Certain findings require prompt medical referral rather than psychological formulation: acute confusion, new focal neurological signs, recent-onset seizures, severe headache with vomiting, rapid cognitive decline, and cognitive change in the context of fever or systemic illness. The assessor should know these and act on them without delay.

## 6.11|Functional Implications of Cognitive Findings

The value of a cognitive assessment to the client depends on its translation into functional implications. This is a separate step of reasoning, and it is the step most often skipped.

### From score to activity to participation

A useful translation moves through three levels. The score describes performance on a task. The task maps onto an activity of daily living, such as managing medication or following a conversation in noise. The activity maps onto participation, such as living independently or sustaining employment. Each mapping requires evidence, ideally from observation or informant report, and each introduces uncertainty that should be stated.

### Ecological validity and its limits

Ecological validity research shows moderate relationships between cognitive test performance and everyday functioning, stronger for some domains than others. Memory and executive measures tend to predict instrumental activities better than processing speed or simple attention. The relationship is improved by using multiple measures, by including informant ratings, and by assessing the person in or near their own environment.

### Recommendations that follow

Functional recommendations should be specific to the activity and the setting, and should name the support rather than the deficit: a written medication schedule and a weekly check with a pharmacist, rather than "needs support with medication"; a reduced-distraction workspace and written instructions, rather than "has attentional difficulties". Recommendations should also indicate what would prompt review, since cognitive function and environmental demands both change.

## 6.12|Communicating Cognitive Results

Cognitive reports are read by clients, families, teachers, employers, insurers and tribunals. Each audience needs the same core information in a different form, and the assessor's task is to make the report accurate, accessible and honest about its limits.

### Reporting scores

A score should be reported with its name, its scale, its percentile and its confidence interval, together with a plain-language descriptor. Reports should make clear what the score measures and what it does not: a working memory index does not measure intelligence, and a full-scale score does not measure potential. Where normative comparisons are uncertain because of language, culture, education or sensory factors, the report should say so at the point where the score is presented, not in a final paragraph.

### Explaining uncertainty

Clients and referrers often prefer certainty, and assessors often supply it. The professional obligation is to explain uncertainty in terms the recipient can use. "Your scores on these tasks were lower than we would expect given your education and previous work, but the difference is within the range that could be produced by the fatigue and pain you described. I would like to repeat the assessment when you are sleeping better, because that will tell us more than a single test can." This is honest, respectful and actionable.

### Feedback as part of the assessment

Feedback on cognitive findings should include the client's own experience of the tasks, the assessor's observations, the functional implications and the recommended next steps. Where findings are unwelcome, the assessor should allow time for reaction and should avoid presenting conclusions as fixed. And where the client's account differs from the assessment, the difference should be explored, since the client often holds information that the test room could not supply.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A 58-year-old man is referred by his employer's occupational health service after increasing errors at work over eighteen months. His job requires managing multiple projects and remembering detailed specifications. The question is whether he has a cognitive impairment and whether he can continue in his current role.

**The assessment.** He obtains a full-scale score in the average range. Verbal comprehension is high average, perceptual reasoning average, working memory low average and processing speed borderline. Delayed memory is low average, with a large primacy effect and poor free recall that improves substantially with cueing. Executive measures show reduced planning efficiency and increased perseveration. Performance validity is within normal limits. A depression inventory is in the moderate range; he reports sleeping five hours and waking early since his wife's death two years ago. He takes a prescribed antihistamine nightly and drinks two to three glasses of wine most evenings.

**The reasoning.** The profile is not consistent with a generalised dementia: verbal knowledge is preserved, memory shows a retrieval pattern rather than a storage failure, and validity is adequate. It is consistent with a depression-related attentional and executive difficulty, compounded by sleep restriction, anticholinergic medication and alcohol. The memory pattern is the key discriminator: cortical memory disorders typically show poor encoding and limited benefit from cueing, whereas the pattern observed here is more typical of reduced effortful retrieval.

**The decision.** The report describes the profile, declines to diagnose a neurocognitive disorder, and identifies depression, sleep and substance factors as probable contributors requiring treatment before any conclusion about dementia. It recommends a review of the antihistamine with his general practitioner, treatment for depression, and reassessment in six months. On fitness for work, it recommends temporary redistribution of the most memory-demanding projects, with a review, and states that a determination of permanent capacity would be premature.

**The teaching point.** The most important finding was a pattern, not a score. The pattern directed attention to reversible contributors, and the recommendation was to treat them before concluding anything about permanent decline.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Cognitive assessment answers descriptive, diagnostic, change-related and functional questions, and the assessor must know which is being asked. Theories of intelligence supply the reference structure for interpretation, and hierarchical models are the most useful clinically. Profile interpretation is constrained by the standard error of the difference, by base rates in the general population and by the need for functional corroboration. Attention, memory and executive function each require domain-specific assessment and attention to confounds; executive function in particular is poorly captured by laboratory tasks alone. Performance validity must be evaluated in every cognitive assessment and reported when compromised. Medical and neurological information is essential to aetiological conclusions, and referral is mandatory when red flags are present. The final step, translation into functional implications, is what makes the assessment useful.
