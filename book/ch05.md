## Introduction

Personality assessment asks a different kind of question from symptom assessment. Symptoms describe what is happening now; personality describes the relatively stable organisation of dispositions, motives, strategies and relational patterns that shape how a person responds across situations and over time. Because that organisation is inferred rather than observed directly, personality assessment depends more heavily on multiple methods and on cautious inference than any other area covered in this book.

The clinical value of the enterprise is considerable. Personality information improves prediction of treatment response and alliance, illuminates the interpersonal cycles that maintain distress, guides the choice between supportive and interpretive approaches, and provides the client with a description of enduring patterns that is often experienced as more respectful than a diagnostic label. The risks are equally considerable: personality language lends itself to reification, to moral judgement disguised as description, and to conclusions that follow the client for years. This chapter develops both the technical and the ethical dimensions of the work. ## 5.1|Purposes of Personality Assessment

Personality assessment is commissioned for several distinct purposes, and the purpose determines the method, the depth and the appropriate form of reporting. Confusing the purposes produces reports that answer a question nobody asked.

### Differential diagnosis and formulation

In diagnostic assessment, personality measures help to distinguish enduring patterns from current states, to identify traits that predispose to particular presentations, and to characterise the personality context in which a symptom disorder occurs. For formulation, the relevant question is usually functional: which enduring strategies maintain the presenting problem, and what would a treatment that addresses them look like?

### Treatment planning and prediction

Personality data inform treatment planning in several ways. They predict alliance and dropout risk, identify the interpersonal behaviours likely to be enacted with the therapist, suggest the level of interpretive work the client can tolerate, and indicate the likely response to structured versus exploratory modalities. These predictions are probabilistic and should be stated as such; the evidence for personality-based treatment matching is stronger for some dimensions — such as trait conscientiousness and adherence, or interpersonal hostility and alliance rupture — than for others.

### Selection, fitness and legal questions

Personality assessment is also used in employment selection, fitness-for-duty evaluations, custody proceedings and forensic determinations. These applications raise the stakes and change the requirements: the assessor must be familiar with the relevant legal standards, must use instruments with evidence of validity for the specific purpose, must evaluate response style rigorously, and must be able to withstand cross-examination. Instruments validated for clinical description are not thereby validated for forensic prediction, and the distinction should be recorded in the report.

## 5.2|Trait and Dimensional Models

The dominant framework for describing individual differences is the trait model, in which people are positioned on continuous dimensions along which they differ in degree rather than in kind. The five-factor model, comprising neuroticism, extraversion, openness to experience, agreeableness and conscientiousness, has accumulated substantial cross-cultural and longitudinal support and provides the reference structure for most contemporary inventories.

### Strengths of the trait approach

Traits are measurable with high reliability, stable over years in adulthood, heritable to a moderate degree, and predictive of important outcomes including occupational performance, relationship stability and mental health. The five-factor structure has been recovered in many languages and cultures, though the adequacy of the model in every cultural setting remains an empirical question rather than a settled fact. For the assessor, the trait model's principal virtue is that it describes without explaining away: it can say that a client is high in neuroticism and low in agreeableness without implying pathology.

### Limits of the trait approach

Traits describe average tendencies, not behaviour in a specific situation. A person high in conscientiousness may be punctilious at work and chaotic at home, and the trait score will not reveal which. Traits also say little about the organisation of personality — how conflict is managed, what happens under stress, how the person experiences themselves and others — which is precisely the information that psychodynamic and interpersonal traditions supply. Finally, extreme trait scores are not diagnoses: high neuroticism is a risk factor, not a disorder.

### Hybrid and severity models

Contemporary models attempt to combine trait description with a measure of severity or impairment. The alternative model for personality disorders in DSM-5-TR specifies both a level of personality functioning, rated on dimensions of self and interpersonal functioning, and a set of maladaptive trait domains; ICD-11 characterises personality disorder primarily by severity with optional trait qualifiers (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; World Health Organization, 2024). Both approaches reflect the evidence that traits alone do not distinguish disorder from variation, and that severity of impairment carries most of the clinical information.

## 5.3|Broad-Band Personality Inventories

Broad-band inventories assess multiple domains within a single administration. They are the workhorses of clinical personality assessment and the instruments most likely to be encountered in doctoral training.

### The Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory

The MMPI family, most recently the MMPI-3 and the MMPI-2-RF, combines clinical scales with extensive validity scales and has an unusually large research base (Ben-Porath & Tellegen, 2008). Its strengths are empirical scale construction, sophisticated validity indices and strong normative data. Its limitations include length, reading level, the interpretive complexity of its higher-order structure, and a normative sample that, while large, is specific to the United States. The instrument is best used as a hypothesis-generating device: its scales identify domains in which further enquiry is warranted, and its validity scales inform the interpretation of everything else.

### The Personality Assessment Inventory

The Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI; Morey, 2007) was constructed with a coherent theoretical model, uses a four-point response format, and includes validity scales, clinical scales and treatment consideration scales. Its treatment scales are particularly useful in counselling psychology, since they address treatment rejection, aggression, suicidal ideation and stress. Its reading level is lower than the MMPI's, which extends its applicability, though not to clients with significant literacy limitations.

### Five-factor inventories

The NEO Personality Inventory-3 (McCrae, Costa, & Martin, 2005) and comparable five-factor instruments provide a normal-range description of personality with excellent psychometric properties. They are often the most useful instruments for counselling psychology, because they describe the person rather than the pathology, and because their facet-level scores connect to specific behaviours. Their limitation is that they were not designed to detect disorder, and their clinical utility depends on the assessor's ability to translate trait descriptions into functional implications. ## 5.4|Narrow-Band Measures

Narrow-band measures assess a single construct or a small cluster of related constructs in depth. They are useful where the broad-band profile has raised a specific question, and they are often better validated for that specific question than the broad instrument that generated it.

### Selecting a narrow-band measure

Selection follows the same criteria as any other instrument: construct definition, normative adequacy, reliability at the score level used, validity for the intended interpretation and evidence of fairness for the client's group. The additional consideration is specificity: a measure of "perfectionism" should have evidence that it distinguishes perfectionism from conscientiousness and from obsessive-compulsive symptoms, or the assessor will be measuring general distress under a more specific name.

### Examples and their uses

Common narrow-band measures in counselling psychology include measures of defence style, emotion regulation, anger, shame, self-esteem, dependency and self-criticism, alexithymia, and interpersonal problems. Each has a research literature connecting it to treatment process; the assessor should know that literature before using the instrument to make a treatment recommendation. A measure of defence style, for example, is most useful when the assessor can say what a particular defence implies for the therapeutic relationship, not merely that the client scores high on it.

### Combining narrow- and broad-band data

The combination is most informative when the narrow-band finding contradicts or qualifies the broad-band profile. A client with a modest elevation on a broad depression scale but an extreme score on a narrow measure of self-criticism may be presenting a picture that the broad instrument smooths over. Discrepancies of this kind are the most productive starting points for formulation.

## 5.5|Interpersonal and Attachment Assessment

Personality is expressed in relationships, and the assessment of interpersonal functioning is often more clinically useful than the assessment of traits. Two traditions dominate: the interpersonal circumplex, which describes behaviour along dimensions of dominance and affiliation, and attachment theory, which describes expectations about the availability and responsiveness of others.

### The interpersonal circumplex

Circumplex measures position a person's characteristic interpersonal behaviour on a circle defined by dominance (agency) and affiliation (communion). The resulting profile describes, for example, a person who is warm and submissive, or cold and dominant, and it identifies the interpersonal problems that accompany each position — being too controlling, too distant, too self-sacrificing or too vindictive. The model's clinical value lies in its account of complementarity: dominant behaviour invites submission, and hostile behaviour invites hostility, which is how interpersonal patterns become self-maintaining.

### Attachment assessment

Attachment is assessed by interview, by self-report questionnaire and by observation of interaction. The Adult Attachment Interview (George, Kaplan, & Main, 1985) classifies attachment states of mind from the coherence of narrative about early relationships; self-report measures such as the Experiences in Close Relationships questionnaire (Brennan, Clark, & Shaver, 1998) assess attachment anxiety and avoidance. The two traditions measure different things — one, the organisation of discourse about attachment; the other, self-reported orientation in close relationships — and they do not always agree. The assessor should know which is being measured and should not treat one as a proxy for the other.

### Clinical use

Interpersonal and attachment data are most useful when they are translated into predictions about the therapeutic relationship and into hypotheses about recurrent relational patterns. "The client describes intense worry about abandonment and responds to perceived criticism with withdrawal; in the assessment sessions this appeared as silence following a clarifying question" is a formulation that can be worked with. A circumplex coordinate alone is not.

## 5.6|Response Style and Impression Management

Self-report personality data are always affected by the respondent's approach to the task. In clinical settings, the two most consequential styles are under-reporting, often driven by social desirability, and over-reporting, which may reflect genuine distress, help-seeking, or deliberate exaggeration.

### Social desirability and defensiveness

Social desirability is normally distributed in the population and is not in itself pathological. At moderate levels it improves the accuracy of some predictions, since people who present themselves favourably also tend to behave favourably. At extreme levels it invalidates self-report, because the respondent is answering a question about who they would like to be. The assessor's response is to seek corroboration and to interpret the profile as a description of the client's self-presentation rather than of the client.

### Over-reporting and its interpretations

Elevated scores on over-reporting indices may indicate deliberate exaggeration, a cry for help, a personality style characterised by dramatic expression, or simply severe distress in a person with limited vocabulary for it. Distinguishing among these requires collateral information, consistency across measures, and attention to the pattern of elevations. The assessor should resist both the naïve reading (the client is exaggerating) and the defensive reading (the client is distressed), and should record the evidence for whichever interpretation the data support.

### Interview-based response style

Response style also operates in interviews. Clients present differently to different interviewers, emphasise different aspects of their history depending on what they believe the interviewer wants, and adjust their accounts in the light of the interviewer's reactions. Structured interviews reduce but do not eliminate this. The interviewer's protection is the same as elsewhere: multiple sources, recorded observations, and explicit attention to discrepancies.

## 5.7|Performance-Based and Projective Approaches

Performance-based measures present ambiguous stimuli and score the responses according to a standardised system. They have a long history in clinical psychology, a contested evidence base, and a continuing role in assessment, particularly where the assessor needs information about implicit processes or where self-report is compromised.

### Evidence

The evidence for performance-based measures is uneven and depends on the specific variable being scored. Mihura, Meyer, Dumitrascu and Bombel (2013) conducted systematic reviews and meta-analyses of Comprehensive System Rorschach variables and found that some variables have good validity while others do not; the valid variables tend to concern perceptual accuracy and thought disturbance rather than the broad personality inferences for which the instrument is best known. This finding supports the use of specific Rorschach indices for specific purposes, and it does not support the interpretive traditions that read personality from response content.

### Appropriate use

Performance-based measures are most defensible when used to answer a specific question for which a specific variable has demonstrated validity, as one source among several, by an assessor trained in the scoring system and aware of its limits. They are least defensible when used as a general personality description, when scored impressionistically, or when their output is granted more authority than the self-report data it contradicts.

### Narrative and collaborative uses

Thematic apperceptive methods retain a place in assessment for eliciting the client's narrative themes, conflicts and representations of relationships, particularly in therapeutic assessment models. Their use in this mode is idiographic: the assessor is not scoring against norms but listening for themes that can be discussed with the client. This is a legitimate clinical activity, provided it is not presented as a standardised finding.

## 5.8|Personality Functioning and Severity

The most important development in personality assessment over the past two decades is the separation of personality variation from personality disorder by introducing a dimension of severity. Severity describes how far a person's personality organisation impairs their capacity to function in the domains of self and relationships; it is what distinguishes a person with difficult traits from a person with a personality disorder.

### Domains of personality functioning

The DSM-5-TR alternative model specifies two broad domains, self and interpersonal, each with two elements. Self functioning comprises identity (the experience of a coherent sense of self and the capacity to regulate self-esteem) and self-direction (the capacity to pursue meaningful goals). Interpersonal functioning comprises empathy (the capacity to understand and appreciate others' experience) and intimacy (the capacity for enduring, mutually satisfying close relationships). ICD-11 uses a similar structure, grading severity from mild personality difficulty through moderate disorder to severe disorder.

### Assessing severity

Severity is assessed by interview, by informant report and by observation of the client's functioning over time. Useful indicators include the stability of the client's self-concept, the coherence of their narrative, their capacity to maintain relationships and employment, the quality of their reflection on their own contributions to difficulties, and the presence of self-harm or repeated crises. Structured instruments, such as the Level of Personality Functioning Scale, support the judgement but do not replace it.

### Why severity matters clinically

Severity predicts treatment intensity, risk, and the likelihood of dropout. A client with mild personality difficulties may benefit from time-limited work in a general service; a client with severe personality disorder requires a longer, more structured approach with explicit attention to alliance and containment. Confusing the two leads to either over-treatment or, more commonly, to under-treatment disguised as a diagnosis.

## 5.9|Personality Assessment in Case Formulation

Personality findings become clinically useful when they are translated into a formulation: an account of how the person's enduring patterns interact with current circumstances to produce and maintain the presenting problem.

### From trait to pattern to process

The translation proceeds in three steps. Traits become patterns when they are described in terms of recurring behaviour across situations. Patterns become processes when they are described in terms of mechanism: what triggers them, what they achieve, and what they cost. A client high in neuroticism and low in agreeableness may show a pattern of anticipating criticism and responding with counter-criticism; the process is a cycle in which anticipatory vigilance produces behaviour that elicits the criticism that confirms the expectation.

### Developmental and relational context

Formulation situates the pattern in its developmental history and its current relational field. The relevant questions are how the pattern developed, what function it served, and what maintains it now. A pattern that developed as a protection in an unpredictable household may persist long after the household has gone, maintained by its own consequences. This account is explanatory, testable and non-judgemental in a way that a trait description is not.

### Writing the formulation

A written formulation of personality should describe the pattern, its origins, its maintaining processes, its costs and its adaptive value, in language the client could read without feeling accused. It should distinguish what is known from what is inferred, and it should identify the point at which intervention is most likely to be effective. Where the formulation cannot be written in such language, it is usually not yet a formulation.

## 5.10|Feedback from Personality Assessment

Feedback is where personality assessment either becomes useful or becomes harmful. Clients are frequently given personality labels without explanation, and the experience of being described in a report they never see, in terms they would not recognise, is a recognised source of harm.

### Principles of feedback

Feedback should be collaborative, specific and behavioural. It should avoid trait labels used as explanations, translate findings into examples the client recognises, invite correction, and connect each finding to something the client can act on. Where the assessment identified a pattern the client had not noticed, the feedback should present it as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a verdict.

### Discussing difficult findings

Some findings are difficult: low empathy, high hostility, marked dependency, or a pattern of self-defeating behaviour. The evidence suggests that these findings can be discussed productively when they are framed as protective or adaptive in origin, described in behavioural terms, connected to the client's own stated goals, and accompanied by explicit acknowledgement of the assessor's limits. Feedback that moralises, or that uses the assessment as an opportunity to say what the assessor has always thought, is harmful and should not be delivered.

### Follow-up

Feedback should be followed by an opportunity to respond, and the client's response should be recorded. Where the client disputes a finding, the dispute is information: it may indicate that the interpretation was wrong, that it was correct but premature, or that it was correct and unwelcome. Distinguishing among these requires the assessor to remain curious rather than defensive.

## 5.11|Cultural Interpretation of Personality Scores

Personality inventories were developed largely in North America and Western Europe, and their item content, normative data and implicit models of healthy functioning carry cultural assumptions. The assessor must therefore consider whether the construct, the items and the norms are appropriate for the client.

### Cultural variation in trait expression

The distribution of traits differs across cultural groups, and the meaning of a given trait level may differ more than the distribution. Assertiveness, emotional restraint, deference to family and directness of self-disclosure are valued differently, and a score that is average in one setting may represent unusual conformity or unusual deviation in another. The five-factor structure has been broadly replicated, but the interpretation of specific facets is more culturally variable than the factor structure suggests.

### Norms, reference groups and comparison

Where norms are available for the client's cultural or linguistic group, they should be used, with attention to their quality. Where they are not, the assessor should interpret scores with explicit caution and should prefer within-person and behavioural comparisons. A useful practice is to ask the client what their own reference group would consider typical, which often supplies context that no norm table contains.

### Avoiding cultural attribution of pathology

There is a persistent risk that culturally patterned behaviour is read as personality pathology: restraint as constriction, deference as dependency, indirectness as evasiveness, or emotional expressiveness as histrionic. The corrective is not to exempt cultural behaviour from assessment but to generate cultural explanations as competing hypotheses and to test them with the client, the family and, where appropriate, a cultural consultant.

## 5.12|Integrating Personality Findings

Integration requires the assessor to combine self-report, interview, informant data, behavioural observation and, where used, performance-based measures into a description that is coherent, evidence-based and useful. The obstacles are predictable: sources disagree, instruments measure overlapping constructs, and the pressure to produce a definitive description is strong.

### A procedure for integration

A workable procedure begins with the questions the assessment was asked to answer, not with the instruments. For each question, the assessor arrays the evidence from each source and notes its direction and quality. Where sources converge, the finding can be stated with confidence. Where they diverge, the assessor examines the conditions under which each was obtained and decides which is more informative for the specific question. The final description should state the pattern, its functional consequences, its probable developmental origins and its implications for treatment.

### Writing without reification

The most important stylistic discipline in personality reporting is to avoid turning a score into an entity. "The client is a narcissistic personality" is a reification; "the client describes a strong need for admiration and reacts to criticism with rage, which has cost him two jobs and a marriage" is a description. The second can be discussed, tested and treated; the first can only be accepted or rejected. Reports should describe behaviour, its function and its consequences, and should use diagnostic terms only where a diagnosis has been formally established and is required by the recipient.

### Limits of the assessment

Finally, the report should state what the assessment could not establish: the stability of the pattern over time where only one assessment was conducted, the possibility of state effects on trait measurement, the absence of informant data where none was available, and the limits of the norms for this client. A personality assessment that acknowledges these limits is more credible than one that presents a fully formed portrait on limited evidence.

:::panel Advanced Case Conference
**The referral.** A psychotherapy service asks for assessment of a 34-year-old woman referred after two failed courses of treatment, the first ending in abrupt termination and the second in a crisis admission. The question is what kind of treatment, if any, is likely to help.

**The evidence.** Interview reveals longstanding difficulty in close relationships, a history of intense but unstable attachments, recurrent self-harm in her twenties and a pattern of idealisation followed by devaluation of therapists. A five-factor inventory shows very high neuroticism, low agreeableness and low conscientiousness. A broad clinical inventory shows elevations across several scales with a validity profile suggesting moderate over-reporting. Self-report attachment measures indicate high anxiety and high avoidance. There is no informant data; the client declined collateral contact.

**The reasoning.** The severity of impairment in self and interpersonal functioning, combined with the longstanding pattern and the repeated treatment ruptures, is consistent with a moderate to severe personality disorder. The trait profile is consistent but non-specific, and the over-reporting suggests that the self-report scores should be interpreted as an indication of distress rather than as precise estimates. The pattern of rapid idealisation and devaluation has direct implications for treatment: an unstructured exploratory therapy is likely to reproduce the rupture, while a structured approach with explicit agreements about contact between sessions, a clear treatment frame and a named clinician responsible for coordination is more likely to hold.

**The decision.** The report describes the pattern and its severity, states the diagnosis provisionally, and recommends a structured, formulation-driven treatment with explicit frame agreements and a named coordinator. It states what would indicate that the treatment is working, and what would indicate that it should be reviewed. It records the absence of collateral data as a limitation and the client's reasons for declining it.

**The teaching point.** The assessment's value lay in what it said about the treatment, not in what it said about the personality. The most consequential finding was a pattern of relationship rupture, and it changed the recommendation.
:::

## Chapter Summary

Personality assessment describes enduring organisation rather than current state, and it requires multiple methods and cautious inference. Trait models provide a reliable descriptive structure but do not by themselves distinguish disorder from variation; severity of impairment in self and interpersonal functioning carries most of the clinical information. Broad-band inventories supply comprehensive description with validity scales, narrow-band measures supply depth, and interpersonal and attachment frameworks supply the relational context in which personality difficulties are enacted. Response style affects every self-report measure and must be interpreted rather than ignored. Performance-based measures are defensible where specific variables have demonstrated validity for specific questions, and not as general personality description. Integration should proceed from the question, describe patterns and processes rather than traits-as-entities, and state the limits of what a single assessment can establish.
