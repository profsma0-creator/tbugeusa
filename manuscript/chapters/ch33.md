## Saul and David: When the Old Guard Fears the Emerging Generation

**PART SIX opens with the most thoroughly documented generational collision in Scripture, and it is documented with an unusual amount of detail, because the narrator of Samuel wants the reader to see the inside of the older man's mind.**

**Two men are anointed in the space of one book. Neither is a usurper, and neither is an apostate, and the younger man will not lift his hand against the older.**

**And the older man will spend the last decade of his reign, and the whole apparatus of a kingdom, trying to kill him.**

**The collision is not caused by ambition, and it is not caused by doctrine, and it is not caused by any failing in the young man at all.** It is caused by something the older man sees and cannot bear, and the text names it in five words.

### The song that ended a peace

*And it came to pass as they came, when David was returned from the slaughter of the Philistine, that the women came out of all cities of Israel, singing and dancing, to meet king Saul, with tabrets, with joy, and with instruments of musick. And the women answered one another as they played, and said, Saul hath slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands. And Saul was very wroth, and the saying displeased him; and he said, They have ascribed unto David ten thousands, and to me they have ascribed but thousands: and what can he have more but the kingdom? And Saul eyed David from that day and forward* (1 Samuel 18:6–9).

**Look carefully at what happened in that scene, because it is the ordinary origin of a generational war and it is almost always misread.**

**The women came out to meet *king Saul*.** The celebration was for the king. **They brought the young man's victory to the monarch's own party and laid it at his feet as part of his honour.**

**And they sang a comparison.**

**And the comparison was not the young man's invention.** David had not claimed the ten thousands, and there is no evidence anywhere in the narrative that he had spoken a word about the numbers. **The evaluation was made by the crowd, in the street, while the king was present, and the crowd belonged to the king.**

**And note the words Saul says to himself: *they have ascribed unto David ten thousands, and to me they have ascribed but thousands: and what can he have more but the kingdom?***

**The logic moves in one step from an inferior statistic to a threatened throne, and the step is irrational and the irrationality is diagnostic.** A comparison in a song is not a claim to a crown. **But a man with a kingdom and a conscience knows what the crowd's preference can eventually amount to, and once he has taken that step in his own mind, no subsequent act of the young man can correct it.**

**And the last sentence is the one to sit with: *And Saul eyed David from that day and forward.***

**The Hebrew is *ayin*, to look with a fixed and hostile attention.** From that day, the young man is no longer a person in the narrative; he is an object under surveillance. **Which means the relationship did not end in a quarrel. It ended in a monitoring.**

**And this is the first lesson of the collision, and it must be stated plainly: the older generation frequently responds not to what the young man does but to the recognition the young man receives.**

**The recognition can be genuine and it can be earned and it can be entirely outside the young man's control.** A congregation notices something. A crowd reports a result. **A single unguarded comparison at a dinner becomes, in the older man's heart, a verdict about his replacement.**

**And the man who is watching for this can see the exact moment.** It is the moment a son's success stops being a reason for the father's rejoicing and becomes a datum in a case against him.

### The diagnosis: what the old guard is actually afraid of

**And the narrator refuses to leave Saul's fear in the realm of psychology, because three times in one chapter he tells us what the fear was *of*.**

*And Saul was afraid of David, because the Lord was with him, and was departed from Saul* (1 Samuel 18:12).

*And David behaved himself wisely in all his ways; and the Lord was with him. Wherefore when Saul saw that he behaved himself very wisely, he was afraid of him* (1 Samuel 18:14–15).

*And Saul was yet the more afraid of David; and Saul became David's enemy continually* (1 Samuel 18:29).

**Read the clauses in the order in which the narrator gives them.**

**The fear is of the presence of God with the young man, stated in verse 12 and restated in verse 14.** Not the young man's army, not the young man's popularity, not the young man's intentions. **The Lord was with him.**

**And the second reason given in verse 15 is even more uncomfortable: *when Saul saw that he behaved himself very wisely.***

**The old king was afraid because the young man was good at his work.**

**And verse 29 tells us the fear intensified: *Saul was yet the more afraid.* And verse 30 tells us why — *David behaved himself more wisely than all the servants of Saul; so that his name was much set by.***

**So the growth curve of the collision is fed by the young man's competence.** Every wise decision, every successful engagement, every additional year of good conduct raised the older man's fear, **which means that the collision could not be resolved by the young man improving. His improvement was the fuel.**

**And there is a further detail that turns the screw:** *Therefore Saul removed him from him, and made him his captain over a thousand; and he went out and came in before the people* (1 Samuel 18:13).

**Saul's first response to the young man was to promote him away from his sight.**

**Which is the characteristic move of an institution that feels the threat and cannot admit it: the young man is given a larger responsibility and less access.** He is honoured publicly and removed relationally. **And every subsequent generation of young men experiences a version of this: an appointment that functions as an exile.**

**And the deepest element of the diagnosis is in verse 12 and it deserves its own paragraph: *because the Lord was with him, and was departed from Saul.***

**The older man knew the presence had gone.** This is the fact he could not state out loud and could not stop knowing.

**In 1 Samuel 15 he had been told face to face, with the mantle rent in Samuel's hand: *The Lord hath rent the kingdom of Israel from thee this day, and hath given it to a neighbour of thine, that is better than thou* (1 Samuel 15:28).**

**So the verdict had been pronounced, and it had been pronounced years before David arrived in the court.** And this changes the whole character of the conflict.

**Saul was not defending a kingdom he possessed. He was resisting a sentence he had already received.**

**And a man in that position does not fight for his position; he fights against the truth.** Which is why such men are so difficult to reason with, and why the ordinary pastoral remedies — reassurance, honour, inclusion, patience — do not work. **You cannot reassure a man about something he has already been told by God.**

**Which gives us the second lesson of the collision: the older man's fear is frequently a symptom of an unresolved verdict rather than a response to the young man at all.** The young man is the place where the older man's own failure has become visible. **And a man will often prefer to destroy the evidence than to submit to the judgement.**

### What David did not do

**And now the part of the story that Scripture gives more space to than any of Saul's attacks, and it is the part that determines the outcome.**

**David did not defend himself, and he did not retaliate, and he did not build a faction.**

**He behaved himself wisely, which in the narrator's usage means a consistent faithfulness in his assigned post.** He served as captain over a thousand; he went out and came in before the people (18:13); he took the commission that was designed as a death sentence and executed it better than it had been designed (18:25–27). **He did not resign in protest and he did not campaign for the throne. And by verse 16, *all Israel and Judah loved David, because he went out and came in before them.***

**And note the specific impossibility of the situation he was in.** The king tried to kill him with a javelin twice in the house (18:11) while he was playing the harp — the task for which he had been brought into the palace in the first place.

**And the last two chapters of the story present him with the throne on a plate, twice.**

**The first is in the cave at En-gedi.** Saul enters alone, at the place where David and his men are hiding, and David's men say the decisive sentence: *Behold the day of which the Lord said unto thee, Behold, I will deliver thine enemy into thine hand* (1 Samuel 24:4). **And David cuts off the skirt of Saul's robe and his heart smites him for even that.**

**And the second is in the camp at Hachilah.** David and Abishai walk into the middle of Saul's sleeping army, and Abishai asks for permission to pin him to the ground with the spear. **And David's refusal is the statement of doctrine on which his entire legitimacy rests:**

*And David said to Abishai, Destroy him not: for who can stretch forth his hand against the Lord's anointed, and be guiltless? David said furthermore, As the Lord liveth, the Lord shall smite him; or his day shall come to die; or he shall descend into battle, and perish. The Lord forbid that I should stretch forth mine hand against the Lord's anointed: but, I pray thee, take thou now the spear that is at his bolster, and the cruse of water, and let us go* (1 Samuel 26:9–11).

**Read the second sentence carefully, because it is the whole ethic of the emerging generation summarised in four options.**

**The Lord shall smite him.** Or his day shall come to die. Or he shall descend into battle, and perish. **Three ways the incumbent may be removed, and every one of them belongs to God.**

**And the fourth is the one David takes: *let us go.***

**He removed the spear and the water cruse — the symbols of Saul's authority and of his life — and then he stood at a distance and displayed them to Abner, and called out to the king, and made his case, and went his way.**

**The young man's method is stated in three words: he took the symbols and left the man.**

**Which is the exact opposite of what every revolutionary instinct in a young man screams at him, and it is the reason David's kingdom lasted and the reason the kingdom of the house of Saul did not have to be destroyed by force.**

**And the reasoning behind it had already been stated to Saul's face, in the cave, in one sentence that deserves to be memorised by every young man in a system where the older man is unjust:**

*The Lord judge between me and thee, and the Lord avenge me of thee: but mine hand shall not be upon thee* (1 Samuel 24:12).

**The Lord judge.** Not the crowd, not the committee, not the assembly, not the elders, not the internet. **And this is not passivity; it is a jurisdiction claim.** David is saying that the case is not within his authority to decide, and that he will not occupy a court that is not his.

**And the practical consequence of that stance was ten years in the wilderness.** He was hunted through the caves of Judah, betrayed by the men of Keilah and the Ziphites, and obliged to live among the Philistines for his own safety. **The Lord's jurisdiction is not a fast method. It is a certain one.**

### The machinery the old guard is willing to build

**And now the account of what the old man spent in order to keep a place he had already been told he would lose, because Scripture records the expense in detail and the detail is the warning.**

**He tried the direct violence first.** *And Saul cast the javelin; for he said, I will smite David even to the wall with it. And David avoided out of his presence twice* (1 Samuel 18:11). **The harpist was nearly killed at the instrument that was meant to soothe the king.**

**And when the direct attack failed, he moved to a commission with a fatal price.** He offered his daughter for a dowry of a hundred Philistine foreskins, and the text says exactly what he was doing: *Saul thought, I will make him fall by the hand of the Philistines* (1 Samuel 18:21, 25). **The promotion and the death sentence were the same document.**

**And then he turned to the informers.** The Ziphites come to him with David's whereabouts and are thanked for it: *Blessed be ye of the Lord; for ye have compassion on me* (1 Samuel 23:21). **Note the religious language attached to the betrayal.** The older man has now recruited the vocabulary of blessing into the service of his fear.

**And then he corrupted his own household.** He offered Michal to David as *a snare* (18:21). He accused his own son of conspiring against him in language of sexual insult (20:30). **And he threw a javelin at Jonathan** (20:33), the same weapon he had thrown at David, because Jonathan had asked one reasonable question: *Wherefore shall he be slain? what hath he done?*

**And then he killed the priests.**

*And the king said to the footmen that stood about him, Turn, and slay the priests of the Lord; because their hand also is with David, and because they knew when he fled, and did not shew it to me. But the servants of the king would not put forth their hand to fall upon the priests of the Lord. And the king said to Doeg, Turn thou, and fall upon the priests. And Doeg the Edomite turned, and he fell upon the priests, and slew on that day fourscore and five persons that did wear a linen ephod. And Nob, the city of the priests, smote he with the edge of the sword, both men and women, children and sucklings, and oxen, and asses, and sheep, with the edge of the sword* (1 Samuel 22:17–18).

**Two things in that passage must be extracted.**

**The first is that the king's own bodyguard refused the order.** *But the servants of the king would not put forth their hand to fall upon the priests of the Lord.* **Even the men who had sworn to obey him could see what the command amounted to, and the guard's refusal is the last moral check the institution had.**

**And the second is that the man who carried it out was *Doeg the Edomite* — not an Israelite.**

**When the house of God's anointed king had no one left in it willing to do what he wanted, he imported a foreigner to do it.** And eighty-five priests and the entire population of Nob, including suckling children and livestock, were killed in a single day, **because an elderly monarch could not bear the presence of God on a young man he had promoted himself.**

**Which is the third lesson of the collision, and it is the hardest: the older man's fear will eventually cost him things that have nothing to do with the young man.** He will spend his priests, his children, his integrity, his guard's respect, and his own posterity on a war that cannot be won, **because the verdict he is fighting was issued by God.**

**And note the terrible economy of the thing.** The slaughter at Nob was the exact opposite of the older man's stated interest. **Saul lost the intercession of the priesthood, the loyalty of his own soldiers, and the survival of his dynasty, in exchange for the continued pursuit of a man who would not touch him.**

### "As long as the son of Jesse liveth"

**And Saul states the logic of the collision himself, in words that are more honest than anything else he says in the book.**

*For as long as the son of Jesse liveth upon the ground, thou shalt not be established, nor thy kingdom. Wherefore now send and fetch him unto me, for he shall surely die* (1 Samuel 20:31).

**He is speaking to Jonathan, his heir.** And the sentence is a complete theory of generational conflict stated in one breath.

**His opposition to David is not based on anything David has done and it is not based on anything David believes.** It is based on the fact that David's existence makes his own house unviable. **He cannot be established while the young man lives.**

**And this is the most common form of the collision, and it is the most difficult to adjudicate, because there is nothing to adjudicate.** The old man is not claiming that the young man is a heretic, or a thief, or a rebel. **He is claiming a structural incompatibility, and he is right about the structure and wrong about the remedy.**

**The structure is real. God had transferred the kingdom, and the existence of the anointed successor did genuinely mean the end of the incumbent's dynasty.** Saul's diagnosis was accurate. **His prescription was the murder of a righteous man.**

**And this is why so many generational conflicts cannot be resolved by mediation and cannot be settled by clarification.** Both parties are describing the same situation and only one of them is willing to accept it.

**And the older man's fight is not for his position; it is against the sentence.** He cannot kill the sentence, so he tries to kill the evidence.

**And the evidence is a person, which means the older man in that condition will eventually be at war with God in the person of God's chosen — and will describe his war as a defence of what God gave him.**

### Jonathan: the heir who chose the emergence

**And into this collision comes the single most remarkable character in the book, and one of the most affecting figures in the whole of Scripture.**

**Jonathan was the crown prince.** He was the legitimate heir, a proven commander, a man whose courage had already decided a battle (1 Samuel 14:1–14), and the direct beneficiary of his father's position. **He had the most to lose from the emergence of David, and by every measure of interest he should have hated him.**

**And the text says:**

*And it came to pass, when he had made an end of speaking unto Saul, that the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul* (1 Samuel 18:1).

**The Hebrew is *qashar*, to be bound together, and the word is used of things that cannot be separated.** They made a covenant, and Jonathan sealed it by giving David the insignia of the heir:

*And Jonathan stripped himself of the robe that was upon him, and gave it to David, and his garments, even to his sword, and to his bow, and to his girdle* (1 Samuel 18:4).

**The robe is the royal garment, and the sword, the bow and the girdle are the instruments of command.** He dressed the young man in what belonged to himself.

**And he never repented of the decision, and he never acted against it, and he maintained it under direct attack from his father.**

*And Jonathan Saul's son arose, and went to David into the wood, and strengthened his hand in God. And he said unto him, Fear not: for the hand of Saul my father shall not find thee; and thou shalt be king over Israel, and I shall be next unto thee; and that also Saul my father knoweth. And they two made a covenant before the Lord: and David abode in the wood, and Jonathan went to his house* (1 Samuel 23:16–18).

**Consider what Jonathan said in that forest.** He promised the young man the throne and assigned himself the second place. **And he said that his father already knew it.**

**Which means the crown prince walked into a wood to tell a fugitive that the succession had been settled and that he was not the successor, and to strengthen him in God.**

**And then he went home to the father who had thrown a javelin at him.**

**And he died with his father on Mount Gilboa, and he died for his father's crown, and his death is recorded in the lament that David wrote.**

*I am distressed for thee, my brother Jonathan: very pleasant hast thou been unto me: thy love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women* (2 Samuel 1:26).

**And David kept the covenant after Jonathan was dead.** In 2 Samuel 9 he searches the house of Saul for anyone remaining, brings Mephibosheth, Jonathan's lame son, to his own table, restores him the lands of Saul, and arranges for him to *eat bread at my table continually* (2 Samuel 9:7–13).

**Note what that means for the collision.** The man David took into the palace as a permanent guest was the grandson and heir of the king who had hunted him for a decade. **The house of the old guard was provided for at the young man's table, because of a covenant made in the wilderness with the old man's son.**

**And that is the answer to a generational war: not the elimination of the other house, but a table for it.**

**And Jonathan is the model for every man in the middle. He was the natural heir, and he chose the emergence.** He did not become the obstacle, he did not use his natural advantage, and he did not wait to be overcome. **He recognised the presence of God on another man, which is the one discernment that Saul lacked, and acted on it at the cost of his inheritance and finally of his life.**

**And it is worth saying that Jonathan's choice is the harder one and the rarer one.** The older man's error — fear leading to suppression — is common. **The intermediate man's error is subtler and more respectable: to defend the line, to invoke propriety and family loyalty, and to become the instrument by which the old guard fights the future.** Jonathan refused, and paid for it, and is remembered two thousand years later as the man who loved the anointed of God more than his own position.

### The long war, and how it actually ended

**And the narrator gives the collision one sentence of summary, and the sentence contains the entire mechanics of the resolution.**

*Now there was long war between the house of Saul and the house of David: but David waxed stronger and stronger, and the house of Saul waxed weaker and weaker* (2 Samuel 3:1).

**Four elements, and each of them is important for anyone living through a generational conflict now.**

**The first is the duration: *long war.*** The Hebrew is *milchamah arokkah*. **It was not a confrontation and it was not a crisis that could be resolved by a meeting.** It was an attrition that lasted years, and the years are not wasted; they are the means by which the stronger house is sorted from the weaker.

**The second is the direction of the two houses: one grows stronger and one grows weaker.** And the growth is described with the verb *halokh*, going, walking, an ongoing progression. **There was no single decisive battle. There was a trajectory.**

**And the third element is the one that must be stated carefully, because it is the point on which impatient young men go wrong: David did not accelerate the process.**

**He had the throne within reach twice and refused it twice.** When Saul died, the throne of Israel was given to a son of Saul, and David was made king of Judah only, and he waited. **When Abner, the old man's general, offered to defect and deliver the whole kingdom to him, David received him and did not use him as a weapon, and the offer collapsed when Abner was murdered and David distanced himself from the murder publicly, with a fast, and turned the murderer over to execution.**

**And when Ishbosheth, Saul's son and surviving heir, was murdered by his own captains, David executed the men who brought him the head.**

*How much more, when wicked men have slain a righteous person in his own house upon his bed? shall I not therefore now require his blood of your hand, and take you away from the earth?* (2 Samuel 4:11).

**Which is the fourth element, and it is the most remarkable of all: the young man made it impossible to think of him as the enemy of the old man's house.**

**He executed the men who helped him, he mourned the old king, and he provided for his posterity.** By the time the elders of Israel came to him at Hebron to anoint him over all the tribes, his rise was not attributable to any act of violence against Saul's line at all — and that fact is a substantial part of his qualification to reign.

**And this is the settlement of the collision, and it is worth stating in the plainest possible language: the old guard cannot be defeated in the short term and does not need to be. It will lose, and the young man's task is to be the kind of man who can be trusted when it does.**

### The psalms that came out of the collision

**And the final thing to be said about this collision is that it produced the largest body of worship literature in world history, and it did so in the caves.**

**Psalm 57 is superscribed *when he fled from Saul in the cave*.**

*Be merciful unto me, O God, be merciful unto me: for my soul trusteth in thee: yea, in the shadow of thy wings will I make my refuge, until these calamities be overpast* (Psalm 57:1).

**And the phrase that deserves attention is *until these calamities be overpast.***

**The young man's theology in the cave is not a theology of victory. It is a theology of shelter with a horizon: the calamities will pass, and meanwhile there is a shadow, and I will sit in it.**

**And Psalm 18, written at the other end of the process, states what the waiting produced:**

*Thou hast delivered me from the strivings of the people; and thou hast made me the head of the heathen: a people whom I have not known shall serve me. As soon as they hear of me, they shall obey me: the strangers shall submit themselves unto me* (Psalm 18:43–44).

**Note the contrast between the two psalms and the two conditions.** In the first, he is hiding under wings. In the second, strangers obey him at the report of his name. **And the same man wrote both, and the transition between them took about twenty years, and it was made of years of refusing to put his hand on the anointed.**

**Which gives the last lesson of this chapter, and it is a comfort for anyone in the young man's position.**

**The years of unjust constraint are not lost time, because they are the years in which the character required for the throne is formed.** A young man who takes the throne by removing the old man inherits a kingdom administered by a man who removes people. **A young man who waits twenty years under harassment in order to keep one doctrine intact is formed into a king and the formation cannot be bought any other way.**

**And the Psalms are the evidence that the waiting was productive.** Every congregation that has ever sung *be merciful unto me, O God* has been singing the fruit of a decade in the caves.

### The two errors

**And the chapter closes with the two errors, one for each generation, and they are not symmetrical.**

**The error of the old guard is to treat the presence of God on a younger man as a threat, and then to defend a position that God has already transferred, in the process spending everyone and everything they have.**

**And the symptom to watch for in yourself is the surveillance that begins on the day recognition arrives.** *Saul eyed David from that day and forward.* If you have started monitoring a younger man's standing, and comparing it with your own, and interpreting his competence as aggression, you are in the position and the process has started.

**And the error of the young is to conclude that the injustice of the incumbent transfers the authority to them.**

**David's doctrine answers that, and it is stated twice, in the cave and in the camp: *who can stretch forth his hand against the Lord's anointed, and be guiltless?* The answer is nobody, and the doctrine holds even when the anointed is behaving like a madman.**

**And the young man who honours that doctrine will often wait longer than he thinks he can bear, and will frequently be right about the old man's failures and wrong about his own timetable.** The Lord shall smite him, or his day shall come to die, or he shall descend into battle, and perish. **And in the meantime the instruction is the same as it was at Hachilah: take the spear, take the cruse, and go.**

### Principles

**First: the collision usually begins over recognition rather than behaviour.** *Saul hath slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands.* The crowd's comparison, not the young man's ambition, is what started the war.

**Second: the old guard's fear is theological.** *Saul was afraid of David, because the Lord was with him, and was departed from Saul.* Diagnose accordingly — reassurance does not cure a man who is resisting a verdict.

**Third: competence makes the collision worse, not better.** *When Saul saw that he behaved himself very wisely, he was afraid of him.* Once a man has begun to read your success as a threat, better work will not reconcile him.

**Fourth: the anointed incumbent is not the young man's to remove.** *Who can stretch forth his hand against the Lord's anointed, and be guiltless?* The Lord shall smite him, or his day shall come to die, or he shall perish in battle. Take the spear and the water, and go.

**Fifth: the fear of the old guard will cost everyone around him.** Eighty-five priests and the city of Nob, a crown prince nearly killed with a javelin, a bodyguard that refused an order, and an Edomite hired to do what an Israelite would not.

**Sixth: the natural heir can choose the emergence.** Jonathan stripped himself of the robe, the sword, the bow and the girdle, strengthened David's hand in God, and told him he would be second. The man in the middle is not obliged to become the obstacle.

**Seventh: the war ends by trajectory, not by a decision.** *David waxed stronger and stronger, and the house of Saul waxed weaker and weaker.* And David's part in it was to refuse to accelerate it, and to refuse to profit from the deaths of the other house.

**And eighth: the years of unjust waiting are the years of formation.** *In the shadow of thy wings will I make my refuge, until these calamities be overpast.* The caves produced the Psalms.

### For the fathers

- **Watch the day your attention becomes surveillance.** *Saul eyed David from that day and forward.* If you have begun to monitor a younger man's standing rather than to serve his formation, the process has started in you and not in him.
- **Ask whether your fear is about his conduct or about God's evident presence on him.** *Because the Lord was with him, and was departed from Saul.* The second cannot be fixed by correcting the first.
- **If you have already been told, stop fighting.** Saul had received the verdict in 1 Samuel 15 and spent the rest of his reign resisting it. Resisting a judgement is more expensive than accepting it.
- **Refuse the machinery.** The javelin, the dangerous commission, the informers, the manipulation of your own children, the imported enforcer. Every one of those is in the account, and every one of them cost the man who used it.
- **Do not promote a man away from you because you fear him.** *Saul removed him from him, and made him his captain over a thousand.* Honour that functions as exile is transparent, and the people can see it.
- **And if your own son chooses the emergence, do not throw the spear at him.** Jonathan asked one honest question and was answered with a weapon. The old guard who attacks his own heir has already lost the argument.

### For the young

- **Expect the recoil to follow the recognition, and because of the recognition.** The song in the street, and not your ambition, is what begins the war. Do not be shocked, and do not answer it.
- **Behave yourself wisely in your post, and stay in it.** *He went out and came in before them.* David did not resign in protest, form a faction, or campaign. He did the assignment, and the nation read his conduct.
- **Never put your hand on the Lord's anointed — even when he is wrong, and even when he is dangerous.** *The Lord shall smite him; or his day shall come to die; or he shall descend into battle, and perish.* Three options belong to God. Yours is: take the spear, take the cruse, and go.
- **Take the symbols and leave the man.** David took the robe's skirt and the spear and the water and called out from a distance. Demonstrate the wrong without doing the wrong.
- **Refuse to profit from the deaths of the other house.** David executed the men who brought him Ishbosheth's head and fasted for Abner. The men who cannot do that inherit a kingdom of murderers.
- **Expect years and not months, and let the years form you.** *In the shadow of thy wings will I make my refuge, until these calamities be overpast.* The psalms you will one day need are being written in the cave.
- **And provide a table for the old man's house when your day comes.** Mephibosheth ate at David's table for the rest of his life.

### Questions for reflection

1. Has your recognition of a younger man's gift produced joy in you or surveillance, and can you name the day it changed?
2. What is your fear actually about — his competence, or the evident departure of the Lord's presence from you?
3. What have you already spent on keeping a position that God has transferred — and who paid the price for it besides you?
4. If you are the man in the middle, whose robe, sword, bow and girdle are you still wearing?
5. What do you intend to do, after your day comes, for the house of the man who opposed you?
