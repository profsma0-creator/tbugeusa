## Eli's Silence and Samuel's Awakening

Chapters two and three of 1 Samuel contain one of the darkest episodes in the historical books, and it ends with an entire priestly line extinguished in a single day and the ark of God carried off by a pagan army. And when the narrators of Scripture come to interpret what happened — the psalmist in Psalm 78, the author of the book itself — they trace it not to Philistine military superiority and not to political failure, but to a father who would not restrain his own sons.

This chapter examines the anatomy of that failure in detail, because Eli is the clearest case study in Scripture of how a spiritually compromised older generation produces a crisis, and of how God goes about recovering the word when the institution set up to carry it has stopped functioning.

### The house of Eli

The background is given in the first chapter of 1 Samuel, and it is a family matter before it is a national one.

Eli is the priest at Shiloh, the sanctuary where the ark of the covenant resided. *And the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, the priests of the Lord, were there* (1 Samuel 1:3).

His tenure is forty years (1 Samuel 4:18). He is, by the end of it, ninety-eight years old and blind (4:15). He has outlived his usefulness by a considerable margin, and the reason the record gives is that he could not see — *his eyes began to wax dim, that he could not see* (3:2). And the physical blindness is the metaphor the narrator uses for everything else.

**And his sons are introduced with a verdict that is passed before any of their crimes are described:**

*Now the sons of Eli were sons of Belial; they knew not the Lord* (1 Samuel 2:12).

Two clauses, and the second explains the first. They were worthless because they did not know God. And that is the fundamental datum of the chapter: **the sons of the high priest, raised at the sanctuary from birth, were unacquainted with God.**

That fact is the argument. If a household with the tabernacle in it, conducted by a priest of forty years' standing, produced two sons who did not know God, then proximity to the things of God is not sufficient for the transmission of the knowledge of God. Something else was required, and it was missing.

### The three offences

The narrator then describes the sons' conduct, and he does so in a deliberate order, from the institutional to the private.

**The first offence is the flesh-hook.**

*And the priests' custom with the people was, that, when any man offered sacrifice, the priest's servant came, while the flesh was in seething, with a fleshhook of three teeth in his hand; and he struck it into the pan, or kettle, or caldron, or pot; all that the fleshhook brought up the priest took for himself. So they did in Shiloh unto all the Israelites that came thither* (1 Samuel 2:13–14).

This is the sin of taking more than the prescribed portion, using the machinery of the office. The law had assigned the priests specific parts of the sacrifice (Leviticus 7:31–34; Deuteronomy 18:3). The flesh-hook allowed them to take whatever came up by chance, which meant the amount was determined by luck rather than by statute.

**It is theft by process.** No rule was broken visibly; the instrument was simply applied in a manner that produced a larger yield. And it was done *unto all the Israelites that came thither*, which means it was systematic and universal.

**The second offence is the raw fat.**

*Also before they burnt the fat, the priest's servant came, and said to the man that sacrificed, Give flesh to roast for the priest; for he will not have sodden flesh of thee, but raw* (1 Samuel 2:15).

The fat was the Lord's portion, to be burned on the altar (Leviticus 3:16). The instruction was that it had to be offered first. And the priests' servants demanded the meat before the fat was burnt, so that the worshipper was prevented from performing the sacrifice at all in its proper order.

And the narrator adds the worshipper's protest, and the reply:

*And if any man said unto him, Let them not fail to burn the fat presently, and then take as much as thy soul desireth; then he would answer him, Nay; but thou shalt give it me now: and if not, I will take it by force* (1 Samuel 2:16).

*I will take it by force.* The Hebrew is *lakachti be-chozqah*, I will seize it with strength. **The priest's servants were using threats against the worshippers.** And verse 17 gives the verdict: *Wherefore the sin of the young men was very great before the Lord: for men abhorred the offering of the Lord.*

**That is the consequence, and it is the one that matters most.** The people did not merely resent the priests; they came to despise the sacrifice itself. The corruption of the ministers had contaminated the worship.

**The third offence is sexual, and it is the most damaging.**

*Now Eli was very old, and heard all that his sons did unto all Israel; and how they lay with the women that assembled at the door of the tabernacle of the congregation* (1 Samuel 2:22).

*The women that assembled at the door of the tabernacle.* These were women who served at the entrance of the sanctuary — the phrasing suggests a recognised order of service. And the priests were using their position to take them.

**And note the first clause: *Now Eli was very old, and heard all that his sons did unto all Israel.***

**Eli knew.** He was not ignorant. The text says explicitly that he heard everything. And he heard it while he was still alive, still in office, and still capable of acting.

### The rebuke that was not a restraint

And now we arrive at the heart of the chapter, which is the distinction between a rebuke and a restraint.

Eli's response to the report is recorded in full:

*And he said unto them, Why do ye such things? for I hear of your evil dealings by all this people. Nay, my sons; for it is no good report that I hear: ye make the Lord's people to transgress. If one man sin against another, the judge shall judge him: but if a man sin against the Lord, who shall intreat for him?* (1 Samuel 2:23–25).

Take the speech apart, because it is in many respects a good speech.

**He asks the right question.** *Why do ye such things?* — the very question David never asked Adonijah. Eli asked it.

**He names the evidence.** *For I hear of your evil dealings by all this people.* He does not pretend the matter is a private one.

**He identifies the consequence.** *Ye make the Lord's people to transgress.* He tells them that the damage is not confined to themselves.

**And he gives a piece of theology of the highest order.** *If one man sin against another, the judge shall judge him: but if a man sin against the Lord, who shall intreat for him?* That sentence is one of the sharpest statements of the gravity of sin in the Old Testament: an offence against a fellow human being can be arbitrated, but an offence against God has no human mediator.

**By any measure, Eli delivered a serious rebuke. And it accomplished nothing, because it was never accompanied by a consequence.**

The text tells us the outcome immediately: *Notwithstanding they hearkened not unto the voice of their father, because the Lord would slay them.*

And the divine verdict, delivered by the man of God who came to Eli, names the failure precisely:

*Wherefore the Lord God of Israel saith, I said indeed that thy house, and the house of thy father, should walk before me for ever: but now the Lord saith, Be it far from me; for them that honour me I will honour, and they that despise me shall be lightly esteemed* (1 Samuel 2:30).

And the charge that precedes it:

*Wherefore kick ye at my sacrifice and at mine offering, which I have commanded in my habitation; and honourest thy sons above me, to make yourselves fat with the chiefest of all the offerings of Israel my people?* (1 Samuel 2:29).

**Honourest thy sons above me.** That is the diagnosis, and it is not a diagnosis of silence. Eli spoke. He rebuked. He was theologically correct.

**What he did was prefer his sons to God.** And the preference expressed itself as a refusal to remove them from office.

Look at what restraint would have required. Eli was the high priest. Hophni and Phinehas were priests under his authority. He could have dismissed them from the priesthood, barred them from the sanctuary, brought them before the elders of Israel, or invoked the law of Deuteronomy 21:18–21 concerning a stubborn and rebellious son. He had the authority and the evidence and the witnesses.

And the reason he did not, the text says, is that he honoured them above God. The motive may have been paternal affection, family pride, dread of scandal, or fear of his own old age. What it was not was an inability to act.

**And the narrator's summary of the matter, given by God Himself, is a sentence that has been the standing warning of this book:**

*For I have told him that I will judge his house for ever for the iniquity which he knoweth; because his sons made themselves vile, and he restrained them not* (1 Samuel 3:13).

*He restrained them not.* The Hebrew is *velo kihah bam* — and he did not restrain them, did not rebuke them with effect, did not check them. Some manuscripts and the Septuagint read *he did not restrain them*, and the sense is the same: **the failure was in the hand, not the mouth.**

### The sign and the sentence

And the sentence passed on the house of Eli is the severest given to any family in Scripture, and it was given twice, in advance, so that there could be no dispute about its cause.

**The first pronouncement, through the man of God:**

*Behold, the days come, that I will cut off thine arm, and the arm of thy father's house, that there shall not be an old man in thine house. And thou shalt see an enemy in my habitation, in all the wealth which God shall give Israel: and there shall not be an old man in thine house for ever. And the man of thine, whom I shall not cut off from mine altar, shall be to consume thine eyes, and to grieve thine heart: and all the increase of thine house shall die in the flower of their age. And this shall be a sign unto thee, that shall come upon thy two sons, on Hophni and Phinehas; in one day they shall die both of them* (1 Samuel 2:31–34).

**The second, through the child Samuel, on the night of his first prophetic word:**

*In that day I will perform against Eli all things which I have spoken concerning his house: when I begin, I will also make an end ... because his sons made themselves vile, and he restrained them not* (1 Samuel 3:12–13).

And then a detail that shows how completely the failure had reached into the household: *Therefore I swear unto the house of Eli, that the iniquity of Eli's house shall not be purged with sacrifice nor offering for ever* (1 Samuel 3:14).

**No offering.** The institution Eli presided over could not repair the situation. The sacrificial system, administered by the very man who had failed, had no remedy for his failure. This is a limit placed on the machinery of religion itself, and it is stated in the strongest terms in the Bible.

### The fulfilment

And the narrative fulfils the prediction with a precision that makes the earlier passages read like a specification.

Israel went to war against the Philistines and was defeated. And the elders of Israel asked the wrong question:

*Wherefore hath the Lord smitten us to day before the Philistines? Let us fetch the ark of the covenant of the Lord out of Shiloh unto us, that, when it cometh among us, it may save us out of the hand of our enemies* (1 Samuel 4:3).

**They treated the ark as a mechanism.** The presence of the object, in the absence of the covenant, would deliver them. And they were wrong, and the ark was captured, and Hophni and Phinehas were killed, both in one day (4:11), exactly as announced.

And Eli, seated by the gate of Shiloh, blind and ninety-eight, heard the report. He asked what had happened, and he was told about the battle, the casualties, and the ark. And the record says:

*And it came to pass, when he made mention of the ark of God, that he fell from off the seat backward by the side of the gate, and his neck brake, and he died: for he was an old man, and heavy. And he had judged Israel forty years* (1 Samuel 4:18).

**It was the mention of the ark, not of his sons, that killed him.** The text is explicit: the messenger told him about the flight, the slaughter, the deaths of his two sons, and the capture of the ark; and it was at the last item that he fell.

Which tells us something about Eli that is easy to miss, and which we should hold alongside the verdict. **He loved the house of God more than he loved his own household, and he was still unable to act against his sons.** That is a recognisable condition, and it is worth naming: the man whose deepest loyalty is to the institution, and whose private loyalty to his family defeats it.

His body was carried away, and Phinehas' wife, in labour, gave birth to a son and named him before she died:

*And she named the child I-chabod, saying, The glory is departed from Israel: because the ark of God was taken, and because of her father in law and her husband* (1 Samuel 4:21).

*Ichabod*, no glory. She named the child after the disaster, and her reason joins the national catastrophe to the family failure without distinguishing them: *because the ark of God was taken, and because of her father in law and her husband.*

**The ark was taken because of her father-in-law and her husband. That is the Old Testament's own reading of the event, in the words of a dying woman.**

### The psalm that interprets it

And the interpretation is taken up, long afterwards, by the psalmist, in the great historical psalm that recounts God's dealings with Israel from Egypt to David.

*When God heard this, he was wroth, and greatly abhorred Israel: so that he forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh, the tent which he placed among men; and delivered his strength into captivity, and his glory into the enemy's hand. He gave his people over also unto the sword; and was wroth with his inheritance. The fire consumed their young men; and their maidens were not given to marriage. Their priests fell by the sword; and their widows made no lamentation* (Psalm 78:59–64).

Note the sequence, and note what it says about the fate of an institution.

**He forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh.** Shiloh was the place where God had set His name for the period of the judges — the central sanctuary for four hundred years. And God abandoned it.

**He delivered his strength into captivity, and his glory into the enemy's hand.** The ark — the symbol of divine presence — was captured by Philistines.

**The fire consumed their young men.** The whole generation of fighting men.

**And: their priests fell by the sword; and their widows made no lamentation.** The priests were killed and there was not even the customary mourning, because the disaster was so complete that the rites could not be performed.

**This is what happened to a functioning religious institution with a four-hundred-year pedigree, and the reason given in Scripture is the failure of one man to restrain his own sons.**

There is no more sobering text in the Bible for an older generation. And it must be stated plainly, because the temptation is to distance oneself from it: **the institution is not preserved by its own antiquity, its own liturgies, its own buildings, or its own sacrifices. It is preserved by the obedience of the men who lead their own households.**

### And Samuel

And here the chapter turns, and we must be careful about what it turns to.

**God did not repair the house of Eli. He raised up a child outside it.**

The boy Samuel was given to the sanctuary by Hannah, and he grew up under the man whose sons were worthless. And in 1 Samuel 3, in the same building, on the same night, with the same lamp burning and the same old priest asleep, God spoke to the child.

We have treated that chapter at length earlier in this book. What matters here is the shape of the outcome.

**Samuel was not Eli's son, and he inherited Eli's office.** The words *I will raise me up a faithful priest, that shall do according to that which is in mine heart and in my mind: and I will build him a sure house* (1 Samuel 2:35) were fulfilled in a man from a different family, from the hill country of Ephraim, whose father was not a priest and whose mother had been barren.

**The word of God continued, and it continued through a person who had no claim on the office whatsoever.**

And the mechanism of his awakening was Eli's own teaching. The blind old man who could not restrain his sons gave the child the words to answer the voice of God: *Speak, Lord; for thy servant heareth* (1 Samuel 3:9).

**That is the whole enigma of Eli, and it must be held together.** The man who destroyed his own household produced, in the same house, the founder of the prophetic order of Israel. He was useless as a father and decisive as a teacher. He could not stop his sons and he could not hear God — and he could tell a boy what to say.

**God's purposes were not defeated by Eli's failure. Eli's household was.**

### Principles

**First: proximity to the things of God is not the same as knowledge of God.** Hophni and Phinehas were raised in the tabernacle and *knew not the Lord*. A Christian household, a Christian school, a Christian congregation, and a Christian vocabulary are all forms of proximity, and none of them is acquaintance.

**Second: the failure of the older generation is often a failure of restraint rather than of speech.** Eli rebuked his sons and did not remove them. *He restrained them not.* The most common form of father-failure among serious religious men is not silence but the absence of consequence.

**Third: the failure is usually a matter of misplaced honour.** *Thou honourest thy sons above me.* The question is not whether a man loves his children. It is which loyalty prevails when the two are in conflict, and the conflict is the test.

**Fourth: the corruption of the ministers contaminates the worship.** *Men abhorred the offering of the Lord.* The consequence of a compromised leadership is not merely that the leaders are compromised; it is that the people stop valuing the thing the leaders administer. This is the reason institutional corruption is so much more damaging than personal sin.

**Fifth: the institution will not save you and does not need you.** Shiloh was forsaken. The house of Eli ended. And the ark was carried off, returned, later lodged in a tent at Jerusalem, and finally placed in a temple built by a man who was not a priest, in a city that was not Shiloh, through a line that was not Eli's. **God had other arrangements.**

**Sixth: a man who fails his own household may still be the instrument that raises up his successor.** Eli taught Samuel. The man who could not restrain his sons taught the child who replaced them. This is not a consolation for the failure; it is a reminder that God's freedom to work is not confined to the worthy, and that even a compromised old man can hand over the skill he has.

**And seventh: the recovery of the word of God began with a child. The lamp had not gone out, the ark was still there, the priest was still on duty, and the word did not come to the priest. It came to a boy who was not old enough to know what he was hearing.**

**That is the pattern this whole book describes, and the house of Eli is the reason it exists.**

### The twenty years

And there is one further piece of the story that must be told, because it is the part that concerns the generation that comes after a collapse.

Shiloh was abandoned. Eli was dead. His sons were dead. And the ark of God had been captured by the Philistines — and then returned, because the Philistines could not keep it: their god Dagon fell on his face before it (1 Samuel 5:3–4), and the cities that housed it were struck with disease, and they sent it back on a cart drawn by two milch kine.

And it came to rest not in Shiloh, not in Jerusalem, but in a private house in the hill country.

*And the men of Kirjath-jearim came, and fetched up the ark of the Lord, and brought it into the house of Abinadab in the hill, and sanctified Eleazar his son to keep the ark of the Lord* (1 Samuel 7:1).

And then the sentence that describes what a whole generation lived through:

*And it came to pass, while the ark abode in Kirjath-jearim, that the time was long; for it was twenty years: and all the house of Israel lamented after the Lord* (1 Samuel 7:2).

**Twenty years.** The ark of the covenant, the throne of God among His people, sat in a private house in a hill town for two decades. There was no tabernacle, no high priest functioning centrally, no day of atonement at which the mercy seat was approached. A generation grew up for whom the ark was a rumour, a story, a piece of furniture in Eleazar's house.

**And the text says that during those twenty years, all the house of Israel lamented after the Lord.** The absence produced longing. The generation that had lost the presence of God wanted it back.

**This is the situation of a large part of the church in every era, and it is the situation of many individual believers.** They were born after the collapse. They did not cause it. They inherited a diminished version of the thing, and they are told by their elders that the glory is departed and that this is simply how things are.

And the recovery began with a call, and the call was addressed to the whole house:

*If ye do return unto the Lord with all your hearts, then put away the strange gods and Ashtaroth from among you, and prepare your hearts unto the Lord, and serve him only: and he will deliver you out of the hand of the Philistines* (1 Samuel 7:3).

**Three commands: put away, prepare, serve.** And a promise.

### The first attempt fails

And then the restoration, and David's attempt to bring the ark up to Jerusalem is one of the most instructive episodes in Scripture about the danger that faces a young generation that inherits a diminished version of a thing and tries to restore it.

David's first attempt is in 1 Chronicles 13. It is made with enthusiasm and consultation, and it is done in the wrong way:

*And they carried the ark of God in a new cart out of the house of Abinadab* (1 Chronicles 13:7).

A new cart. It was the Philistines' method — the cart on which the ark had come back from Philistia (1 Samuel 6:7–8). The Philistines had no law governing the transport of the ark, and a cart was a perfectly reasonable solution for them. **But Israel had a law: the Levites were to carry it on their shoulders by the staves** (Numbers 4:15; 7:9).

And the consequence was catastrophic:

*And when they came unto the threshingfloor of Chidon, Uzza put forth his hand to hold the ark; for the oxen stumbled. And the anger of the Lord was kindled against Uzza, and he smote him, because he put his hand to the ark: and there he died before God* (1 Chronicles 13:9–10).

And David's reaction is honest and instructive:

*And David was afraid of God that day, saying, How shall I bring the ark of God home to me?* (1 Chronicles 13:12).

**The man who killed a lion and a bear, who faced Goliath, and who had conquered every enemy from Moab to Zobah, was afraid — and his fear was of the God whose instructions he had not consulted.**

And the ark sat in the house of Obed-edom for three months (1 Chronicles 13:14), which is the same pause, in smaller form, that had lasted twenty years.

**Then David did it properly.** And his account of what had gone wrong is the sentence this chapter needs:

*Then David said, None ought to carry the ark of God but the Levites: for them hath the Lord chosen to carry the ark of God, and to minister unto him for ever ... For because ye did it not at the first, the Lord our God made a breach upon us, for that we sought him not after the due order* (1 Chronicles 15:2, 13).

**Not after the due order.** The Hebrew is *kammishpat* — according to the judgment, the ordinance, the prescribed manner. The first attempt had good intentions, national support, the correct destination, and the wrong method, because nobody had gone back to the instructions.

And the second attempt is described with a precision that shows what had changed:

*So the priests and the Levites sanctified themselves to bring up the ark of the Lord God of Israel. And the children of the Levites bare the ark of God upon their shoulders with the staves thereon, as Moses commanded according to the word of the Lord* (1 Chronicles 15:14–15).

*As Moses commanded according to the word of the Lord.* Note the chain: the instruction had been given to Moses, written down, preserved through the period of the judges, and recovered by a generation that went looking for it.

**This is a pattern the young must understand.** A generation that wants to recover what its predecessors lost will do it twice, or it will not do it. The first attempt is made with energy, the wrong method, and the assumption that sincerity is sufficient. And when the breach comes, the choice is either to abandon the project out of fear or to go back to the book and find the instructions.

**David went back to the book.**

And the lesson is the one this whole book turns on. **The generation that restores a lost thing is the generation that learns to read the instructions again.** It is not the generation that improvises with the best available method, and it is not the generation that inherits the method from an institution that has already lost the substance.

### For the fathers

- **Examine your rebukes for whether they have consequences attached.** A rebuke with no consequence has been heard by your children as a formality. Ask honestly whether the last correction you gave changed anything in the structure of the house.
- **Identify the son or daughter you honour above God**, which may or may not be the one you are most worried about. The test is not affection but whether there is a case in which you would not act.
- **Do the thing that costs the relationship.** Eli's reluctance was entirely understandable and it destroyed his line. There is a point at which not acting is the crueller act.
- **Notice what you are skilled at, and hand it over.** Eli could not hear God and he could teach a child to listen. Whatever you are still able to give, give it before the lamp goes out.
- **Ask who in the house is rising.** Samuel was in the building, unrecognised, while the sons were in the office. Look for the child, the apprentice, the servant who is doing the work.

### For the young

- **Do not assume that the environment you were raised in gave you knowledge of God.** Hophni and Phinehas had the ark at the door. Proximity is not acquaintance, and the first generation to mistake one for the other is usually the one that loses the other.
- **If you grew up in a compromised house, the failure does not transfer to you.** Samuel was not Eli's son, and he grew up under those conditions and heard the voice of God in the same building.
- **Receive what the compromised old man can still give.** Samuel received the five words from Eli and they changed the history of Israel. Do not refuse a gift because the giver is unworthy.
- **And do not repeat the pattern.** If you know what Eli failed to do, the knowledge is itself an obligation. The son of a silent father is the man who must learn to speak.

### Questions for reflection

1. Where have you substituted a rebuke for a restraint?
2. Who has been left in a position they should have been removed from, in your household or your institution, because the removal was too costly?
3. Samuel was in the building. Who is in your building doing the work while the recognised office-holders do something else?
4. What did you inherit from a compromised older man that was genuinely valuable, and have you refused it because of your judgment on him?
5. If your household were to be described in one sentence at the end, what sentence would the narrator write?
