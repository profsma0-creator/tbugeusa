## Daniel and the Young Captives Who Outlived Babylon's Wisdom

Of all the narratives in the Old Testament, the book of Daniel is the one that most directly addresses the situation of a young person living inside a hostile institution — and its answer is neither assimilation nor withdrawal. It is something much more precise, and it is worth examining carefully, because it is the model for every believer who will spend a career inside a system that does not share his convictions.

The book opens with a deportation and a programme. And the programme is the thing to notice, because it is exactly the programme that a modern institution runs on talented young people.

### The programme

*And the king spake unto Ashpenaz the master of his eunuchs, that he should bring certain of the children of Israel, and of the king's seed, and of the princes; children in whom was no blemish, but well favoured, and skilful in all wisdom, and cunning in knowledge, and understanding science, and such as had ability in them to stand in the king's palace, and whom they might teach the learning and the tongue of the Chaldeans* (Daniel 1:3–4).

Set out the criteria. Nebuchadnezzar's recruitment programme selected for:
- **lineage** — the royal family and the nobility;
- **physical condition** — no blemish, well favoured;
- **intellectual capacity** — skilful in wisdom, cunning in knowledge;
- **aptitude for learning** — understanding science;
- **social competence** — ability to stand in a king's presence;
- **and teachability in the imperial curriculum** — the learning and the tongue of the Chaldeans.

That is a talent identification process as sophisticated as anything in a modern corporation or a modern seminary. And the purpose is unambiguous: *whom they might teach* the language and literature of Babylon. The empire does not merely conquer its enemies; it absorbs their best young people and re-educates them into its own service.

And then the provision: *And the king appointed them a daily provision of the king's meat, and of the wine which he drank: so nourishing them three years, that at the end thereof they should stand before the king* (Daniel 1:5).

Three years of provision, of education, of access — and at the end, standing before the king. The path is clear, the price is invisible, and the whole thing is presented as a gift.

Now, note the structural parallels between this and the situation of any young believer entering a great institution today. The selection is by ability. The education is three years or more. The provision is generous. The access is real — you will meet people you could not otherwise meet. And there is always an implicit condition, and the condition is not usually stated.

In Babylon, the implicit condition turned out to be identity.

### The three things the empire wanted

*Unto whom the prince of the eunuchs gave names: for he gave unto Daniel the name of Belteshazzar; and to Hananiah, of Shadrach; and to Mishael, of Meshach; and to Azariah, of Abednego* (Daniel 1:7).

The name is the identity. And the new names are not a bureaucratic convenience. Each is a declaration:
- **Daniel** means *God is my judge*. **Belteshazzar** means, on the most probable reading, *Beltis protect the king* — or *the lord's prince*. A theophoric name for a Babylonian deity substituted for a theophoric name for Yahweh.
- **Hananiah** means *the Lord is gracious*. **Shadrach** is probably *command of Aku* — a moon god.
- **Mishael** means *who is what God is?* **Meshach** is contested, but appears to invoke a Babylonian deity.
- **Azariah** means *the Lord has helped*. **Abednego** means *servant of Nego* — a form of Nebo.

Four Hebrew names, each containing the name of the God of Israel, replaced with four Babylonian names containing the names of Babylonian gods. The empire is not merely training these young men; it is attempting to re-establish their identities.

And three things were on offer, in sequence: **a new name, a new education, and a new diet**. And the diet is the one where Daniel drew the line.

### The refusal and its form

*But Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the portion of the king's meat, nor with the wine which he drank: therefore he requested of the prince of the eunuchs that he might not defile himself* (Daniel 1:8).

This is one of the most carefully composed verses in the book, and every word in it is doing work.

**He purposed in his heart.** The resolution was made internally, before any conversation, before any pressure, before any opportunity arose. Daniel decided in advance. This is the mechanism by which a person maintains integrity in an institution — not repeated crisis management, but prior commitment.

**That he would not defile himself.** Daniel's category is *defilement*, not *discomfort* or *preference*. He is not objecting to Babylonian food on cultural grounds, or because it is unfamiliar, or because he prefers his mother's cooking. The issue is Levitical purity — the diet would have included prohibited animals, and the meat would have been offered to idols at the beginning of the meal, which is the standard practice of a pagan court.

**Therefore he requested.** Not demanded. Not refused. Not staged a protest. Daniel *requested*. He approached the official who had authority over him, in the correct order, with a respectful petition.

**That he might not defile himself.** Note what Daniel does and does not ask for. He does not ask to be exempted from the programme, or transferred, or excused from service, or returned to Jerusalem. He asks for one thing: that he be permitted to obey God while remaining under Babylon's authority.

This is the whole shape of Daniel's life, and it explains why the book is so useful to a young person in a hostile institution. **Daniel distinguishes between what he can accept and what he cannot, and he restricts his resistance to the second category.**

Look at what he accepts. He accepts the Babylonian name — he is called Belteshazzar for the rest of his life, and the narrative calls him by it in official contexts. He accepts the Babylonian education, three years of the learning and the tongue of the Chaldeans. He accepts the position in the imperial administration. He accepts the service of a foreign king.

He accepts all of it, and he refuses meat and wine.

This is a very precise line, and it is not the line that either assimilation or withdrawal would draw. Assimilation would accept the diet. Withdrawal would refuse the education. Daniel does neither, and the consequence is that he spends seventy years in the highest reaches of Mesopotamian government, serving four kings, and dies in Babylon still praying toward Jerusalem.

### The mechanism of the favour

And now the practical detail, which is often skimmed and is central.

*Now God had brought Daniel into favour and tender love with the prince of the eunuchs* (Daniel 1:9).

Before Daniel makes his request, God has prepared the official. The favour precedes the petition. And the same thing happens again with the steward whom Ashpenaz appoints to manage the matter (1:11–14) and again with Nebuchadnezzar, and again with Darius, and again with Cyrus.

Which means that the first thing a young believer needs in a hostile institution is not an argument but *favour* — and favour is God's gift, obtained by character and prayer rather than by manoeuvre. Daniel's integrity in the *interior* precedes his usefulness in the *exterior*.

And then the second mechanism, which is the test.

*Prove thy servants, I beseech thee, ten days; and let them give us pulse to eat, and water to drink. Then let our countenances be looked upon before thee ... And the prince of the eunuchs consented to them in this matter, and proved them ten days* (Daniel 1:12–14).

This is a proposal of an *empirical test*. Daniel does not ask to be believed; he asks to be tested. Ten days, a controlled diet, a visible comparison. And the test is accepted because Daniel has made it easy for the official to say yes: he has not asked for a permanent exemption, a formal ruling, or an institutional change. He has asked for a ten-day experiment.

This is one of the most practical lessons in the book. **A young man who wants to change an institution should begin by proposing a test rather than demanding a policy.**

### The outcome and the paradox

And the outcome is stated in a verse that repays close reading.

*As for these four children, God gave them knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom: and Daniel had understanding in all visions and dreams* (Daniel 1:17).

Note the attribution. It is not that the four outperformed their classmates in Babylon's curriculum. It is that *God gave them* the knowledge and skill. The education was Babylon's; the gift was God's. The learning and the tongue of the Chaldeans were absorbed — a genuinely pagan curriculum — and the ability to master it was a divine gift.

And then: *And in all matters of wisdom and understanding, that the king enquired of them, he found them ten times better than all the magicians and astrologers that were in all his realm* (Daniel 1:20).

*Ten times better.* Babylon's own assessment instrument, applied to Babylon's own candidates, produced a verdict in favour of the young men who had refused the king's meat.

And so the paradox is complete. The empire's programme of assimilation produced the very witnesses that would eventually expose its gods as nothing. Four young men went through the full Babylonian curriculum and emerged, three years later, as the men God used to interpret the king's dreams, to survive a furnace, to read the writing on the wall, and to outlast the empire itself.

### The dream and the institution's failure

We must now turn to Chapter 2, because it is the pivot of the book and it makes an argument about generational revelation that is quite explicit.

Nebuchadnezzar has a dream that troubles him, and he does something entirely unreasonable and entirely revealing: he demands that his wise men tell him both the dream and its interpretation.

*The king answered and said to the Chaldeans, The thing is gone from me: if ye will not make known unto me the dream, with the interpretation thereof, ye shall be cut in pieces, and your houses shall be made a dunghill* (Daniel 2:5).

The institution's response is instructive. The Chaldeans protest that this is unreasonable — *There is not a man upon the earth that can shew the king's matter* (2:10) — and then they say something that turns out to be a theological statement:

*And it is a rare thing that the king requireth, and there is none other that can shew it before the king, except the gods, whose dwelling is not with flesh* (Daniel 2:11).

*Whose dwelling is not with flesh.* The empire's professional religious establishment has just declared that revelation is unavailable. They have the training, the apparatus, the guild, the library, and they can say with confidence that no god discloses his secrets to human beings. And the king, in a rage, orders the execution of every wise man in Babylon — including, incidentally, Daniel and his friends, who are not even consulted.

The institution, in other words, has failed on its own terms, and it is about to destroy itself to conceal the failure.

### The young men and the night

And now Daniel's response. And it is a four-part sequence that should be memorised by anyone who hopes to receive revelation in a crisis.

**First, he asks for time.** *Then Daniel answered with counsel and wisdom to Arioch the captain of the king's guard ... and said, Why is the decree so hasty from the king? ... Then Arioch made the thing known to Daniel. Then Daniel went in, and desired of the king that he would give him time, and that he would shew the king the interpretation* (Daniel 2:14–16).

He does not panic, he does not protest the injustice, and he does not immediately promise a solution. He asks for a delay.

**Second, he gathers a company.** *Then Daniel went to his house, and made the thing known to Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, his companions* (Daniel 2:17).

This is important and easy to miss. Daniel does not pray alone. He has maintained a small group of men who share his convictions and his God, and he brings the crisis to them. The companionship has been preserved through three years of a hostile educational programme.

**Third, they pray for the specific thing they need.** *That they would desire mercies of the God of heaven concerning this secret* (Daniel 2:18).

Note the specificity. They do not pray for deliverance, or for the king's favour, or for the reversal of the decree. They pray for *this secret* — for the content of the revelation.

**Fourth, the revelation comes at night.** *Then was the secret revealed unto Daniel in a night vision. Then Daniel blessed the God of heaven* (Daniel 2:19).

At night. Outside working hours. Not delivered to the institution, or to the guild, or to the king. Given to a young exile who had refused the king's meat three years earlier and who was now praying with three friends for a specific piece of information.

### Daniel's doxology and its content

And what follows is one of the great prayers of the Old Testament, and its content is precisely relevant to the subject of this book.

*Daniel answered and said, Blessed be the name of God for ever and ever: for wisdom and might are his: and he changeth the times and the seasons: he removeth kings, and setteth up kings: he giveth wisdom unto the wise, and knowledge to them that know understanding: he revealeth the deep and secret things: he knoweth what is in the darkness, and the light dwelleth with him* (Daniel 2:20–22).

Set out the claims:
- **He changeth the times and the seasons.** The timetable of history is His.
- **He removeth kings, and setteth up kings.** Empires are His instruments.
- **He giveth wisdom unto the wise.** Even the natural wisdom of the Babylonians was His gift.
- **He revealeth the deep and secret things.** This is the clause that answers the Chaldeans' despair. They had said that no god discloses secrets to flesh. Daniel says that this God does, and He has just done it.
- **He knoweth what is in the darkness, and the light dwelleth with him.** The God who reveals is the God who sees.

The doxology is a statement about *revelation*, made by a young man at the moment he received revelation, and it is not accidental that the first thing he does is attribute it entirely to God. There is no triumphalism in Daniel 2. There is thanksgiving.

### The interpretation and the generations

We should note, finally, what the dream actually contained, because it is a prophecy of generations.

The image is of a single statue with four parts: a head of gold, a chest of silver, a belly of bronze, and legs of iron, with feet of iron mingled with clay. Nebuchadnezzar is the head of gold. The three subsequent metals are three subsequent empires. And then:

*And in the days of these kings shall the God of heaven set up a kingdom, which shall never be destroyed: and the kingdom shall not be left to other people, but it shall break in pieces and consume all these kingdoms, and it shall stand for ever* (Daniel 2:44).

Four empires, one after another, and then a kingdom that is not the fifth in the sequence but is rather the destruction of the sequence. *Not be left to other people.* The kingdom is not transmitted on the pattern of the empires — it is not left to another people, or granted to a successor, or passed on. It endures.

This is the answer to a question that must have troubled the exiles deeply. Babylon had outlived Israel. Babylon had destroyed the temple, taken the ark, ended the Davidic monarchy, and deported the leadership. The generation that was taken captive had every reason to believe that the covenant had failed and that the gods of the empire had won.

And what Daniel receives in the night is a timeline showing that the empires are temporary and the kingdom is not. Which means the exile is not the end of the covenant. It is one season inside a longer story that the empires do not control.

That is what revelation given to a young man in a hostile institution looks like. It is not information about his personal future. It is a framework for understanding the empire, delivered at the moment the empire's own experts had declared revelation impossible.

### The principles

Let us draw the principles from Daniel, because they are unusually practical.

**First, participation in a hostile institution is not the same as compromise with it.** Daniel accepted the education, the language, the name and the position, and refused the meat. He did not withdraw into a ghetto, and he did not assimilate into Babylon. He found the precise line and held it for seventy years.

**Second, the line must be drawn before the pressure arrives.** *Daniel purposed in his heart.* The resolution preceded the test by years, and it is why the resolution held.

**Third, requests work better than demands.** Daniel petitioned the official, proposed a test, and offered a way for his supervisor to say yes without losing face.

**Fourth, favour precedes effectiveness.** God brought Daniel into favour before Daniel made his argument. Integrity of character, maintained in private, is the mechanism by which God opens institutional doors.

**Fifth, revelation comes to those who ask for it specifically, in a company.** Daniel gathered three friends, and the four of them prayed for a specific thing, and it was given.

**Sixth, and last: Babylon's own assessment eventually favoured the young men who had refused its table.** *Ten times better.* This is not a general promise that faith produces career success, and it would be dishonest to present it as one. But it is a recorded fact about a specific instance, and it means that integrity is not always the loser in the institutional calculation.

And there is one thing more, which belongs to the argument of this book. When the four young men were taken to Babylon, the generation that remained in Jerusalem had lost its temple, its king and its land. But the word of God did not die in Jerusalem; it went to Babylon, in the persons of four teenagers, and it came back seventy years later in the form of a prophet, a scribe, a governor and a wall.

The thing that survived the destruction of the nation was the thing that was carried by the young people who were dragged out of it.
