## Seed, Soil and Season

Every institution runs on a clock, and the clock is almost never the one Scripture uses for the work of God. The institutional clock measures quarters and years, programmes and campaigns, targets and reviews. It produces a rhythm of initiation, measurement and abandonment, and the rhythm is defensible on its own terms.

And it is incompatible with the way seed works.

Scripture describes the transmission of the word of God through the whole agricultural cycle, from sowing to harvest, and it does so persistently across every genre it contains. **The reason is not that the biblical world was agrarian, and ours is not.** It is that **the kingdom of God operates on agricultural time, and always has**, and the parables are not illustrations borrowed from farming; they are descriptions of the operating system.

This chapter sets out the laws of the seed and applies them to the work of one generation in another.

### The seed that grows while nobody watches

The parable that states the mechanism most precisely is one of the shortest in the Gospels, and it appears only in Mark.

*And he said, So is the kingdom of God, as if a man should cast seed into the ground; and should sleep, and rise night and day, and the seed should spring and grow up, he knoweth not how. For the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself; first the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear. But when the fruit is brought forth, immediately he putteth in the sickle, because the harvest is come* (Mark 4:26–29).

**Note first what the man does.** He casts seed into the ground. **And then he sleeps.**

That is the whole of his labour described in the parable: one act of sowing, followed by ordinary life — *sleep, and rise night and day.* The text does not describe watering, weeding, fertilising, or checking. It describes a man getting on with his days.

**And note the mechanism: *the seed should spring and grow up, he knoweth not how.***

The Greek is *hos ouk oiden autos* — as he does not know. **The growth is not merely invisible; it is unintelligible.** The man cannot explain what is happening in the soil, and he does not need to, and his inability to explain it is not a defect in his farming. It is the nature of seed.

**And the earth brings forth fruit *automate* — of herself.** That is the Greek word, and it is the origin of the English *automatic*. **The soil has an internal principle of production that the sower does not supply and cannot accelerate.**

**The point of the parable is that the interval between sowing and harvest is not the sower's business.** He is responsible for the sowing. The growing is done by a process he neither controls nor understands, and the harvest arrives at a time he did not set.

**Which is exactly the opposite of how a programme works.** A programme is designed, resourced, scheduled, monitored and evaluated, and its success or failure is determined by whether the design was adequate. **A seed has no design. It has a nature.** The man who sowed it can ruin the crop by sowing in the wrong place or at the wrong season, and he cannot produce a crop by any amount of management.

### The four stages cannot be skipped

And then the sequence, which is the part of the parable that speaks most directly to the impatience of a young generation:

*First the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear* (Mark 4:28).

**Three stages, in a fixed order, and no stage can be omitted or compressed.**

**The blade** — the first green shoot, the evidence that something is alive. It looks like nothing. It is indistinguishable from grass and it feeds no one.

**The ear** — the stalk with the head forming, the grain not yet filled. The shape is there and the substance is not. **A man who harvested at the ear stage would get chaff.**

**And the full corn in the ear** — the grain filled out, hard, ready. This is the only stage at which the crop is useful, and it is the last.

**Now apply the sequence to a young person.** The blade is the first evidence of life: the desire, the hunger, the early gift, the willingness. **The ear is the period when the shape of the ministry is visible and the substance is not: the young man who preaches acceptably and has no depth, the young woman who leads well and has not been tested.**

**And the full corn is character that has been through enough seasons to hold weight.**

**The error of the impatient older generation is to put the ear in the barn.** The error of the impatient young one is to demand to be harvested while still a blade.

**And the parable's last sentence is a warning to the harvester as well as an encouragement to the sower:** *But when the fruit is brought forth, immediately he putteth in the sickle, because the harvest is come.*

**The harvest has a window.** A crop that is left standing too long rots in the field. **The same generation that must not reap too early must not fail to reap when the grain is ready** — and this is the counterpart error to everything said above about patience.

**The sower must wait, and the reaper must not.** Both are the same discipline of reading the season.

### Three agents and one harvest

And the apostle Paul gives the doctrine its clearest formulation, in a passage about the factions at Corinth and therefore a passage about a succession dispute:

*I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase. So then neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth; but God that giveth the increase. Now he that planteth and he that watereth are one: and every man shall receive his own reward according to his own labour* (1 Corinthians 3:6–8).

**Three agents, and the verse does not rank them.**

**Paul planted.** He founded the church at Corinth, preached there for eighteen months, and left.

**Apollos watered.** He arrived later, *an eloquent man, and mighty in the scriptures* (Acts 18:24), and he taught and built.

**And God gave the increase.** The growth that neither of them produced.

**Now note the two statements Paul makes about this arrangement, because they are in tension and the tension is the doctrine.**

**First: *neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth.*** The Greek is *ouden eisin* — they are nothing, they are no one. **Neither the founder nor the successor is the cause of the result.**

**And second: *he that planteth and he that watereth are one.*** The Greek is *hen eisin* — they are one thing, one entity, a single unit. **Despite the temporal separation, the two men are one worker.**

**And then: *every man shall receive his own reward according to his own labour*.** The Greek is *ton idion misthon kata ton idion kopon* — his own wages according to his own toil. **The unity does not abolish individual accounting.**

**So the two men are one in the work and separately accountable for their parts of it.**

**That is the constitutional arrangement of generational ministry, and it resolves a problem that plagues every institution.** The founder is not diminished by the successor's contribution, the successor is not a mere caretaker of the founder's achievement, and neither is anything in himself. **They are one work; they are two labours; and the increase belongs to God.**

**And note that the founder said this.** Paul, writing about a church he had planted, described himself as *nothing* and said that he and the man who came after him were *one*. **He is the model of a founder who hands over without needing to remain the cause.**

### The seed that must die

And there is a law of the seed in Scripture that has no analogue in any institution, and it is stated by the Lord in the last week of His life.

*Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit* (John 12:24).

**The law: fruitfulness requires the death of the seed.**

**And the mechanism is the one thing a seed cannot avoid.** A wheat grain kept in a jar remains a single grain indefinitely. It is safe, intact, and alone. And a grain planted and buried disappears entirely as a grain, and comes up as a stalk with thirty grains on it.

**There is no intermediate option.** The seed can be preserved or it can be fruitful. **It cannot be both, and the choice is made by whether it is buried.**

**And this is the law that governs every transmission of life from one generation to another, and it is the reason for the resistance.** A man who hands over his knowledge, his standing and his position is a seed in the soil; he disappears, and something else appears in his place. **A man who keeps all three intact is a seed in a jar: safe, and alone, and useless.**

**The Lord applied the law immediately to His own death — *now is my soul troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour: but for this cause came I unto this hour* (John 12:27) — and then extended it to His disciples:**

*He that loveth his life shall lose it; and he that hateth his life in this world shall keep it unto life eternal. If any man serve me, let him follow me; and where I am, there shall also my servant be* (John 12:25–26).

**Follow me — into the ground.**

### The guarantee

And there is a corresponding promise attached to the sowing, and it is one of the strongest statements of assurance in Scripture.

*For as the rain cometh down, and the snow from heaven, and returneth not thither, but watereth the earth, and maketh it bring forth and bud, that it may give seed to the sower, and bread to the eater: so shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please, and it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it* (Isaiah 55:10–11).

**The comparison is between rain and the word, and the point of the comparison is that rain does not come back.** It falls, and it soaks in, and it runs off, and it evaporates, and it never returns to the sky in the form in which it came down. **It is spent on the ground.**

**And the word is the same. *It shall not return unto me void.***

The Hebrew is *velo-yashuv elai reqam* — and it shall not return to me empty, or empty-handed. The word *reqam* means with nothing, with empty hands, without effect.

**And then the two positive clauses, which are remarkably specific:**

***It shall accomplish that which I please*** — the Hebrew is *ki im-'asah eth-'asher chafatsti*, and shall do that which I delight in. **The word accomplishes God's pleasure, not the hearer's preference.**

***And it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it*** — the Hebrew is *vehitsliach asher-shelachtiv*, and it shall prosper in that for which I sent it. **The prosperity is indexed to the sending, not to the reception.**

**Which means the promise does not guarantee that the word will be received. It guarantees that the word will do what it was sent to do.** And what it was sent to do may be the hardening of a hearer as much as the salvation of one — Isaiah's own commission was to make a people's hearts fat and their ears heavy (Isaiah 6:10), and the word accomplished that.

**The practical consequence for a generation is this: you cannot tell from the reception whether the sowing worked.** A sermon that empties a congregation and a sermon that fills one may both be the word of God doing what it was sent to do.

### The patience of the husbandman

And James gives the doctrine its pastoral form, and it is addressed to believers under persecution who were expecting an imminent return:

*Be patient therefore, brethren, unto the coming of the Lord. Behold, the husbandman waiteth for the precious fruit of the earth, and hath long patience for it, until he receive the early and latter rain* (James 5:7).

**The Greek for *patient* is *makrothymesate*, and the word means long-tempered — the capacity to endure a slow process without becoming angry or giving up.** It is not passive resignation. It is the deliberate extension of the temper over time.

**And the model is the farmer, who *waiteth for the precious fruit*.** The Greek is *ekdechetai* — he receives it out of, he waits to receive from. **The farmer is described as waiting for a crop that he cannot produce and cannot hurry.**

**And note the phrase *the early and latter rain*.** In Palestine, the early rain came in the autumn and made sowing possible; the latter rain came in the spring and brought the grain to fullness. **Two rains, months apart, both necessary, neither in the farmer's control.**

**Which is a picture of the two generations.** The early rain and the latter rain are not the same rain, neither is sufficient alone, and the harvest requires both. **And the farmer who has received the early rain has to trust that the latter will come, on the evidence of a promise, during a dry season.**

*Be patient therefore, brethren, unto the coming of the Lord.*

### The faintness is the danger

And immediately after, the same principle stated as a warning.

*And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not* (Galatians 6:9).

**Three clauses, and each is doing work.**

**Do not be weary in well doing.** The Greek is *me enkakomen* — do not lose heart, do not give in to evil, do not become exhausted in the doing of good. **The verb describes a specific kind of tiredness: the tiredness of doing good without result.**

**For in due season we shall reap.** The Greek is *kairo idio* — in its own season, in its own appointed time. **The reaping is scheduled, and the schedule is not disclosed.**

**And the condition: *if we faint not*.** The Greek is *me ekluomenoi* — not being loosed, not relaxing, not unstringing. **The word describes a bowstring going slack.**

**The single threat to the harvest named in that verse is not drought, disease, or enemy action. It is the farmer giving up before the season arrives.**

**And that is the threat to a generation.** Not that the older men will be wicked, or the young ones rebellious, or the institution corrupt. **That the people in the middle of the interval will conclude the interval is the outcome and stop sowing.**

And the last of the seed texts is the most freeing, because it removes the burden of prediction.

*Cast thy bread upon the waters: for thou shalt find it after many days. Give a portion to seven, and also to eight; for thou knowest not what evil shall be upon the earth ... He that observeth the wind shall not sow; and he that regardeth the clouds shall not reap ... In the morning sow thy seed, and in the evening withhold not thine hand: for thou knowest not whether shall prosper, either this or that, or whether they both shall be alike good* (Ecclesiastes 11:1–6).

**Take the instructions in order.**

**Cast thy bread upon the waters.** The image is of bread thrown onto the surface of a river or the sea — apparently wasted, definitely unrecoverable. **The instruction is to give away what you need, in a direction that offers no return.**

**For thou shalt find it after many days.** The return is promised and the interval is indefinite. *Many days* in Hebrew idiom means a long time, possibly beyond the giver's lifetime.

**Give a portion to seven, and also to eight.** The instruction is to diversify, and the numbers are deliberately open-ended: seven, and then more than seven. **The reason given is *thou knowest not what evil shall be upon the earth*.**

**He that observeth the wind shall not sow; and he that regardeth the clouds shall not reap.** The perfect rebuke to the man who will not act until conditions are favourable. **The Hebrew verbs describe a man watching the sky, calculating the risk, and never getting to the field.**

**And then the most freeing sentence in the chapter: *for thou knowest not whether shall prosper, either this or that, or whether they both shall be alike good.***

**The Preacher's argument is that the results are not knowable, and therefore the calculation cannot be completed, and therefore the sowing must be done anyway.** He does not say that the outcomes are unimportant. He says they are unavailable, and that a man who will only sow when he can predict the harvest will not sow.

**And the verse before it establishes the general principle: *As thou knowest not what is the way of the spirit, nor how the bones do grow in the womb of her that is with child: even so thou knowest not the works of God who maketh all* (Ecclesiastes 11:5).**

**The two things the Preacher selects as examples of human ignorance are the growth of a foetus and the movement of the wind.** Both are instances of life being formed by a process no one can observe. **And the works of God in history are of the same kind.**

### The ground that must be broken

But the Bible does not permit the seed imagery to be used as an excuse for passivity about conditions, and two prophets make this explicit.

*For thus saith the Lord to the men of Judah and Jerusalem, Break up your fallow ground, and sow not among thorns* (Jeremiah 4:3).

*Sow to yourselves in righteousness, reap in mercy; break up your fallow ground: for it is time to seek the Lord, till he come and rain righteousness upon you* (Hosea 10:12).

**Break up your fallow ground.** The Hebrew is *niruw lakh nir* — break up for yourselves a breaking. **A *nir* is ground that has been ploughed at some time in the past and has since been left.**

**And the command is that it must be broken again before anything can be sown.** Fallow ground, by definition, is land that is not producing. It is not barren; it is idle, and hard on the surface, and full of the roots of whatever grew there when it was last left alone.

**And the second command is *sow not among thorns*.** The reference is to the parable of the sower, where the seed that fell among thorns was choked — and the interpretation given is *the care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches* (Matthew 13:22).

**The practical implication for a generation is that the ground is not neutral.** A young person growing up in a household, a congregation or an institution in which the soil has not been broken will receive the seed and produce nothing, and the failure will be attributed to the seed.

**Which is why the previous generation's work includes ploughing.** No one praises a ploughman; the field looks worse after he has been through it than before. **And the seed cannot go in until he has done his work.**

### Sowing in tears

And there is one further text, and it is a psalm of return from exile, and it gives the emotional shape of the interval.

*They that sow in tears shall reap in joy. He that goeth forth and weepeth, bearing precious seed, shall doubtless come again with rejoicing, bringing his sheaves with him* (Psalm 126:5–6).

**The psalm is about the return from Babylon, and this line is about the return to the land.** The exiles came back to ruined fields and had to sow in a year when there would be no harvest. **The sowing was done in tears because the sowing was the act of a people who had nothing to eat and were putting their last grain in the ground.**

**And the promise is doubled: *shall doubtless come again with rejoicing*, and *bringing his sheaves with him*.** The Hebrew of verse 6 is *bo-yavo verinnah* — coming, he shall come with singing — the same emphatic construction as Habakkuk 2:3.

**Note what the weeping sower is carrying: *precious seed*.** The Hebrew is *meshekh hazzara* — literally the drawing out, or the draught, of the seed: the seed taken out of the store that was meant for food. **The sowing was done with the grain the family needed to eat.**

**That is the definition of faith in the agricultural metaphor, and it is what a generation looks like when it invests in a harvest it will not eat from.** It takes the food, and it puts it in the ground, and it weeps, and it does not eat.

**And the promise is not that the weeping ends at the harvest. It is that the harvest comes and there is singing.**

### The five wrong clocks

And now the contrast, because the failure this chapter describes is not theoretical. Here are the five models of time that institutions substitute for the agricultural one, and each one is defensible in its own domain.

**The first is the fiscal period.** The quarter, the financial year, the budget cycle. It exists because money must be accounted for, and it is necessary, and it is entirely indifferent to whether anything is growing in the ground. **A programme that is evaluated on the fiscal year will be judged before the blade appears.**

**The second is the programme.** A defined initiative with a beginning, a middle and an end, a budget and a report. Programmes are useful for building things. **They cannot grow them, because growth does not have a completion date.**

**The third is the campaign.** A concentrated effort with a specific objective and a deadline. The campaign model produces real results and it produces them by concentrating resources. **And a campaign mentality, applied to the formation of a person, produces a three-week training course for a twenty-year calling.**

**The fourth is the personality.** The clock of a leader's tenure. Everything is measured by whether it happened while he was in charge, and the natural consequence is that nothing begins that will not mature before he leaves. **Which guarantees that nothing significant begins.**

**And the fifth is the metric.** Whatever can be counted gets counted, and what gets counted becomes the objective. **And there is no metric for a seed.**

**None of these five is wicked, and all five are in use in every institution that exists.** The failure is not in using them. **It is in using them to evaluate the one work that runs on the other clock.**

**A generation that wants to transmit what it has must be able to say: this will not appear in any report, it will not finish in my lifetime, and I cannot tell you whether it will work.**

### Principles

**First: the sowing is the whole of the labour; the growth is not yours.** *The seed should spring and grow up, he knoweth not how.* A generation is responsible for what it puts in the ground and for nothing else.

**Second: the stages are sequential and none may be skipped.** *First the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn.* Putting a young man into a mature assignment before the ear has formed guarantees chaff.

**Third: the harvest has a window, and a crop left standing rots.** The same generation that must not reap early must not fail to reap when the grain is ready.

**Fourth: the founder must consider himself nothing and be at one with his successor.** *Neither is he that planteth any thing ... and he that planteth and he that watereth are one.*

**Fifth: fruitfulness requires the seed's death, and there is no third option between the jar and the field.** A generation that keeps its knowledge, standing and position intact remains alone.

**Sixth: the word's success is measured by its sending, not by its reception.** *It shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it.* A generation that judges its ministry by the response will stop sowing.

**Seventh: the only threat named in Galatians 6:9 is the sower's faintness.** Not the weather, not the soil, not the enemy. **The bowstring going slack before the season.**

**And eighth: you will not be able to calculate the outcome, and the calculation is not required of you.** *Thou knowest not whether shall prosper, either this or that.* **Cast the bread on the water and go home and sleep, and rise night and day, and let the earth do her work.**

### One soweth, and another reapeth

And the clearest statement in Scripture that the sower and the reaper are different people is given by the Lord to His disciples in Samaria, and it is a command to reap a field they had not planted.

*Say not ye, There are yet four months, and then cometh harvest? behold, I say unto you, Lift up your eyes, and look on the fields; for they are white already to harvest. And he that reapeth receiveth wages, and gathereth fruit unto life eternal: that both he that soweth and he that reapeth may rejoice together. And herein is that saying true, One soweth, and another reapeth. I sent you to reap that whereon ye bestowed no labour: other men laboured, and ye are entered into their labours* (John 4:35–38).

**Note first the correction of the calendar.** *Say not ye, There are yet four months, and then cometh harvest?* The disciples were applying the ordinary agricultural expectation — sowing now, reaping in four months — and the Lord tells them to look up, because the field is already ripe.

**The schedule they were working from was correct for wheat and wrong for that field.**

**And then the doctrine of the two workers, stated three times in four verses:**

**One soweth, and another reapeth.** The proverb is quoted as a settled saying.

**I sent you to reap that whereon ye bestowed no labour.** The disciples are explicitly commissioned to harvest work that is not theirs.

**Other men laboured, and ye are entered into their labours.**

**And then the sentence that resolves the whole tension, which is the reason this passage belongs in this chapter: *that both he that soweth and he that reapeth may rejoice together* (John 4:36).**

**The sower and the reaper rejoice together.** Not the reaper thanking the sower, and not the sower being honoured by the reaper. **Together** — *homou*, at the same time, in the same place, in the same joy.

**Which means the reward of the sower is not that the reaper acknowledges him. It is that the sower is present at the harvest and shares the joy of it.**

**And Scripture makes the same arrangement a standing feature of Israel's life in the land.** Twice, in Deuteronomy and in Joshua, the people are told that what they are about to enjoy was built by other men:

*And it shall be, when the Lord thy God shall have brought thee into the land which he sware unto thy fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give thee great and goodly cities, which thou buildedst not, and houses full of all good things, which thou filledst not, and wells digged, which thou diggedst not, vineyards and olive trees, which thou plantedst not; when thou shalt have eaten and be full* (Deuteronomy 6:10–11).

*And I have given you a land for which ye did not labour, and cities which ye built not, and ye dwell in them; of the vineyards and oliveyards which ye planted not do ye eat* (Joshua 24:13).

**Cities you did not build. Houses you did not fill. Wells you did not dig. Vineyards you did not plant.** And the command attached to the enjoyment of all of it is the passage that contains the Shema: *then beware lest thou forget the Lord* (Deuteronomy 6:12).

**The danger of entering into another man's labours is forgetting whose labours they were — and, more fundamentally, forgetting the God who gave the land in the first place.**

**And the counterpart is stated by Micah as a judgement: *Thou shalt sow, but thou shalt not reap* (Micah 6:15).**

**A generation that sows and does not reap is not being punished by absence from the harvest; it is being punished by exclusion from the joy of it.** The two are different things, and the distinction is the whole of this chapter.

### For the fathers

- **Distinguish the programme from the seed.** Ask of every initiative: can this be evaluated this year? If it can, it is a programme, and it is not the formation of a person, and both may be legitimate — but they are not the same.
- **Break up the fallow ground before you sow into it.** The ground your successor will inherit needs ploughing first, and ploughing looks like damage.
- **Sow in the morning and in the evening.** *Withhold not thine hand.* The scattergun approach of Ecclesiastes 11 is commanded, because you cannot tell which will take.
- **Identify the ear and hold the sickle.** Do not harvest the blade; and do not leave the ripe grain in the field because you are still deciding.
- **Plan for a harvest you will not eat from.** *Cast thy bread upon the waters, for thou shalt find it after many days.* The interval is indefinite and the instruction is unconditional.
- **Watch your own faintness, not the weather.** Galatians 6:9 names one threat to the harvest, and it is internal. Know the signs of your own weariness.

### For the young

- **You are, most likely, a blade or an ear, and neither is the finished thing.** *First the blade, then the ear.* Being harvested early is not a promotion; it is a loss of the crop.
- **Do not judge your value by the size of the visible result.** The blade looks like grass. The ear looks like promise. Only the full corn feeds anyone, and it comes last.
- **Receive from the one who sowed and honour the one who watered.** *They are one.* The founder and the builder of a work are not rivals, and the New Testament refuses to rank them.
- **Expect that some of what you were given was not for you.** The prophets searched their own prophecies and were told *not unto themselves, but unto us.* You are the *us*, and the material is now yours.
- **And expect to be the seed.** *Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die.* There is no path to fruitfulness that is not a burial, and the burial is not a metaphor for something mild.

### Questions for reflection

1. What have you sown that you may not live to see harvested?
2. Which of the five wrong clocks — fiscal period, programme, campaign, personality, metric — is governing how you evaluate your work?
3. Where have you harvested a blade, or left a field standing too long?
4. What is the fallow ground in the next generation that needs breaking before anything can go in?
5. What would you do differently if you knew that nothing you did would be measured by a response?
