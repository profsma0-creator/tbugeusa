## Elijah's Crisis and Elisha's Emergence

**There is a sequence in the life of Elijah that every older man in Christian work should read once a year, and it occupies two consecutive chapters that could not be more different.**

**In the first, a single prophet stands against four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal on a mountain, calls down fire from heaven, and hears the whole nation confess that the Lord is God.**

**In the second, within about forty-eight hours, the same prophet is hiding in the wilderness, asking God to kill him, and reporting that he is the only believer left in the country.**

**And in that second chapter, while the man is at his lowest point, God names his successor.**

**Which tells us something that has never been widely believed: the emergence of the next generation is frequently set in motion by the collapse of the present one, and the successor is often named on the worst day of the predecessor's life.**

### The prophet who ran from a woman's threat

**And the account of the collapse is given without any attempt to protect the prophet's reputation.**

*And Ahab told Jezebel all that Elijah had done, and withal how he had slain all the prophets with the sword. Then Jezebel sent a messenger unto Elijah, saying, So let the gods do to me, and more also, if I make not thy life as the life of one of them by to morrow about this time. And when he saw that, he arose, and went for his life, and came to Beer-sheba, which belongeth to Judah, and left his servant there* (1 Kings 19:1–3).

**Consider the proportions of this.**

**The day before, he had stood in front of a king, a nation, and a hostile priesthood, and had initiated a contest whose terms were that the losing god's prophets would die.** And the fire fell, and the prophets of Baal were executed by the brook Kishon, and the nation fell on its face.

**And the day after, a single threat from a queen sent him running for his life.**

**Note where he ran: Beer-sheba, which *belongeth to Judah* — out of the northern kingdom altogether, beyond the reach of Ahab and Jezebel's jurisdiction.** He left the country. And he *left his servant there*, which is the detail that tells us he had decided to be alone.

**And then the further journey, and the request.**

*But he himself went a day's journey into the wilderness, and came and sat down under a juniper tree: and he requested for himself that he might die; and said, It is enough; now, O Lord, take away my life; for I am not better than my fathers* (1 Kings 19:4).

**The Hebrew for *juniper tree* is *rothem*, a desert broom, a shrub with almost no shade in a country that will kill a man in a day.** He sat under the smallest possible covering in the middle of open country, one day beyond the last settlement, **and asked God to end his life.**

**And the reason he gives for the request is the phrase this book is about, and it is easy to read past.**

***For I am not better than my fathers.***

**He had just done something unprecedented.** No prophet in the history of Israel had called fire down from heaven in a public contest and turned a nation in an afternoon. **And his assessment of his own life was a comparison with the generations before him, and the verdict was that he had not surpassed them.**

**Which is the specific affliction of a man who has carried an exceptional load, and it is well documented in the accounts of men who have done great work.** The depression that follows a peak is not caused by failure. **It is caused by the discovery that the peak did not change the underlying situation.**

**Elijah had expected the fire to settle something. And after the fire, the queen was still the queen, the king was still the king, and the covenant was still broken.**

**And notice that he measured himself against his fathers, which is the one comparison the whole arrangement of Israel's religion was meant to prevent.** Each generation was to be judged by its own obedience to the covenant and not by the achievements of its predecessors. **Elijah had swapped the standard and could not survive the comparison.**

**And note the state of the man precisely, because it affects everything about how God dealt with him.** He was exhausted, he was frightened, he was alone, he was sleeping in the open, and he had just asked to die. **Whatever else is said about him in this chapter, he was not in a condition to be reasoned with about strategy.**

**And God's first treatment was not a rebuke and not a lecture. It was sleep and food.**

*And as he lay and slept under a juniper tree, behold, then an angel touched him, and said unto him, Arise and eat. And he looked, and, behold, there was a cake baken on the coals, and a cruse of water at his head. And he did eat and drink, and laid him down again. And the angel of the Lord came again the second time, and touched him, and said, Arise and eat; because the journey is too great for thee* (1 Kings 19:5–7).

**Two meals and two sleeps before any conversation.** And the reason given for the second meal is not that the prophet was depressed but that *the journey is too great for thee* — the task ahead exceeded the resources of the man, **and the remedy was the restoration of the body.**

**Then forty days and nights to Horeb, the mountain where Moses had received the law**, and there in a cave the conversation that changes the subject from the present generation to the next.

*And he came thither unto a cave, and lodged there; and, behold, the word of the Lord came to him, and he said unto him, What doest thou here, Elijah?* (1 Kings 19:9).

**The question is asked twice in the chapter, and the man gives the same answer twice, word for word, and the repetition is deliberate.** And the answer is the complaint of every man who has carried a work alone and can see no one behind him.

### The arithmetic of "I only"

*And he said, I have been very jealous for the Lord God of hosts: for the children of Israel have forsaken thy covenant, thrown down thine altars, and slain thy prophets with the sword; and I, even I only, am left; and they seek my life, to take it away* (1 Kings 19:10).

**Note the structure of the complaint.** Three facts about the nation and one about himself.

**The nation has forsaken the covenant, thrown down the altars, and killed the prophets.** Every one of those statements is true, and the first two are the language of the covenant lawsuit that the prophets were trained to make.

**And then the conclusion: *I, even I only, am left.***

**The Hebrew is *va'ivvater ani levaddi* — and I am left, I alone.** And he says it twice, in verse 10 and again in verse 14, and the second time is after the wind and the earthquake and the fire and the still small voice. **So the repetition is not a slip; the man's perception did not change with the revelation, and God corrected it with a number.**

**And there were the seven thousand.**

*Yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel, all the knees which have not bowed unto Baal, and every mouth which hath not kissed him* (1 Kings 19:18).

**The Hebrew for *I have left* is *hisharti*, from the same root that Elijah used of himself — he said *I am left*, *nivtarti*, and God says *I have left*, *hisharti*.** The word is the same and the subject is different. **Elijah thought he had survived; God had reserved.**

**And Paul reads the church's own situation through this passage, and he draws the doctrinal conclusion:**

*Wot ye not what the scripture saith of Elias? how he maketh intercession to God against Israel, saying, Lord, they have killed thy prophets, and digged down thine altars; and I am left alone, and they seek my life. But what saith the answer of God unto him? I have reserved to myself seven thousand men, who have not bowed the knee to the image of Baal* (Romans 11:2–4).

**Note what Paul calls Elijah's speech: *he maketh intercession to God against Israel.***

**A complaint that describes the nation's condition and the prophet's isolation is not merely a lament. It is a form of intercession, and the direction is against the people.** And note that Paul's name for the remedy is *reserved to myself* — the remnant is God's act and not the prophet's achievement. **The lone man did not know about them because they had not been his to count.**

**And there is a further detail that makes the size of the error visible: the seven thousand were not a secret cell of trained workers. They were knees that had not bowed and mouths that had not kissed Baal.** Ordinary people with no profile, no meetings, and no prophet's attention. **They were the invisible infrastructure of a nation's faithfulness, and the man at the centre of the story had never seen one of them.**

**Which is the first lesson of this chapter, and it is addressed to every older man who is convinced he is the last one standing.**

**The feeling of aloneness is real and the arithmetic of aloneness is usually false.** And the correction is not encouragement; it is data — *I have reserved to myself seven thousand.* A man in that condition needs a number more than he needs a compliment.

### The voice that was not in the wind

**And then God gives the prophet a demonstration, and it is a demonstration of what He is not.**

*And he said, Go forth, and stand upon the mount before the Lord. And, behold, the Lord passed by, and a great and strong wind rent the mountains, and brake in pieces the rocks before the Lord; but the Lord was not in the wind: and after the wind an earthquake; but the Lord was not in the earthquake: and after the earthquake a fire; but the Lord was not in the fire: and after the fire a still small voice* (1 Kings 19:11–12).

**Wind that tears mountains, an earthquake, and fire.** Three of the most spectacular phenomena in Scripture, and the text tells us plainly that God was not in them.

**And note the parallel between the third of them and the event of the previous chapter.** Elijah had just called down fire at Carmel, and the fire had consumed the sacrifice, the wood, the stones, the dust, and the water in the trench. **And now, at Horeb, God shows him a fire and says: not in this.**

**Which is an astonishing thing to say to the man whose greatest triumph was a public fire.** And it is said to him in the desert on the second day of his collapse, and the meaning is not that Carmel was wrong. **It is that the spectacles are not the channel by which the next stage of the work will be done.**

**The Hebrew for *a still small voice* is *qol demamah daqqah* — the sound of a thin silence, a voice of gentle stillness.** And what follows it is a conversation, a question, and a set of instructions.

**And this is the second lesson of the chapter, and it belongs to the formation of the young. The instruments that impress a crowd are not the instruments that build a successor.** The wind, the earthquake and the fire got the nation's attention; **the thin silence got a man named, anointed, and sent.**

**And the demonstration has a further function, which is to tell the exhausted prophet what the rest of his ministry is going to be like.** He had spent his career on the mountain; he would spend his last years in the quiet, doing three anointings and training a son.

**And note what he did when he heard the voice: *he wrapped his face in his mantle, and went out, and stood in the entering in of the cave* (1 Kings 19:13).**

**He covered his face with the garment that would later be given away.** The mantle is already in the narrative, before the transition, and it is the object that will carry the succession. He hid behind the thing he would give away.

### The three anointings, and the successor named "in thy room"

**And God's answer to the complaint is not a defence of His own faithfulness and not a debate about the seven thousand. It is an assignment that includes the man's own replacement.**

*And the Lord said unto him, Go, return on thy way to the wilderness of Damascus: and when thou comest, anoint Hazael to be king over Syria: and Jehu the son of Nimshi shalt thou anoint to be king over Israel: and Elisha the son of Shaphat of Abel-meholah shalt thou anoint to be prophet in thy room* (1 Kings 19:15–16).

**Three names, three spheres, and one phrase that must be extracted.**

**Hazael over Syria is the judgement from outside.** **Jehu over Israel is the purging of the house of Ahab from within.** **And Elisha is the prophet *in thy room.***

**The Hebrew is *tachteicha*, in your place, in your stead, under you.** The word is used of a substitute, a replacement, the one who stands where another stood.

**So the answer to *I am left alone* is a three-part programme of which the third part is: you are not indispensable, and your replacement is already determined.**

**And it is worth pausing over the sequence of the exchange.** Elijah complained that the nation had killed the prophets, and God told him to anoint a prophet. **Elijah said the altars had been thrown down, and God gave him an assignment involving two kings and a successor. Elijah said he was the only one left, and God said the succession was already arranged.**

**And the ordering is immense for our subject: the successor was named on the day of the predecessor's collapse, and named by God, and named before the predecessor had recovered.**

**Which is the third lesson of this chapter, and it is addressed to the older man who believes that his work cannot continue without him. The provision of a successor does not wait for your readiness. It may predate your strength.**

**And note that the appointment was made and the anointing was not performed for a long time.** Elijah went and cast his mantle on Elisha (19:19), which is the calling, **but he did not anoint him, and the public title of the young man for the entire remainder of Elijah's ministry was domestic.**

### "Which poured water on the hands of Elijah"

**And here the account of the apprenticeship, because it is one of the most striking silences in the Old Testament.**

*So he departed thence, and found Elisha the son of Shaphat, who was plowing with twelve yoke of oxen before him, and he with the twelfth: and Elijah passed by him, and cast his mantle upon him. And he left the oxen, and ran after Elijah, and said, Let me, I pray thee, kiss my father and my mother, and then I will follow thee. And he said unto him, Go back again: for what have I done to thee? And he returned back from him, and took a yoke of oxen, and slew them, and boiled their flesh with the instruments of the oxen, and gave unto the people, and they did eat. Then he arose, and went after Elijah, and ministered unto him* (1 Kings 19:19–21).

**The last three words are the whole of the apprenticeship: *and ministered unto him.***

**And the record then says nothing about Elisha for years.**

**Between 1 Kings 19:21 and 2 Kings 2:1, Elisha appears exactly once, and his title is the one that a servant would have.**

*But Jehoshaphat said, Is there not here a prophet of the Lord, that we may enquire of the Lord by him? And one of the king of Israel's servants answered and said, Here is Elisha the son of Shaphat, which poured water on the hands of Elijah* (2 Kings 3:11).

**Which poured water on the hands of Elijah.**

**This is the description given to two kings by a court official, and it is the résumé of the greatest successor in the history of Israel.** He was the man who handed the prophet a basin and a towel before meals. **And that is what he was known for at the age of perhaps forty, after a decade of service.**

**And there is no complaint about it anywhere in the record, and no attempt by Elisha to establish his own profile, and no evidence that he performed a single miracle before Elijah was taken.**

**Which is the fourth lesson, and it is the hardest one in the chapter for a young man to receive. The length of the apprenticeship is not a measure of the mentor's delay. It is the mechanism by which the successor is made.**

**A decade of pouring water on an old man's hands is how the double portion was earned, and there is no shorter route to the position Elisha received.** He knew the prophet's habits, his prayers, his routes, his moods, his enemies, and his God **by being in the room with a basin in his hand when nobody was watching.**

**And the contrast with a cohort that has grown up with a man in a palace is worth stating.** Elisha did not share Elijah's privileges; he shared his service. **And the man who has shared nothing but the results of another man's ministry will not be able to carry it when the results stop.**

**And the last element of Elijah's career is the provision he made.**

**He kept offering the young man a way out — *Tarry here, I pray thee* — three times, at Gilgal, at Bethel, and at Jericho (2 Kings 2:2, 4, 6), and the young man refused all three.** And at the end, the question the older man asked was the only one that mattered:

*Ask what I shall do for thee, before I be taken away from thee* (2 Kings 2:9).

**Which is what an older man does when he has accepted that he is leaving: he stops protecting his position and starts giving things away.**

**And the thing that Elisha asked for was not a role and not a title. It was the firstborn's inheritance, and the condition attached to it was that he should watch his master go.**

**So the crisis of Elijah ended in the emergence of Elisha, and the emergence was purchased with a decade of obscurity and a request for a double portion, and it was all set in motion on the day a tired man sat under a desert shrub and asked to die.**

### What the exhausted man must hear

**And the chapter should say something directly to the man who is presently in Elijah's condition, because the narrative is unusually detailed about God's treatment of him and none of the details are incidental.**

**The first thing God did was feed him and let him sleep, twice, before saying anything.** And the second meal came with an explanation: *the journey is too great for thee.* **Which means that the state of the body is part of the diagnosis, and a man who is trying to make vocational decisions in a state of exhaustion is not thinking; he is guessing.**

**The second thing God did was ask a question, twice, and let the man answer in full both times.** He did not correct the exaggeration on the first hearing. **He asked, listened, and asked again, and the second asking came after a demonstration of His presence.** A man in that condition needs to be heard, and God heard him.

**The third thing God did was correct the facts, and He corrected them with a number.** *I have left me seven thousand.* **Which is a different kind of help from sympathy. It is the restoration of the man's picture of the country.**

**The fourth thing God did was give him a job that contained his own replacement.** Two kings to anoint and a prophet to appoint. **Which means the cure for a man who thinks he is the last one standing is not rest alone. It is an assignment that requires him to build the generation that will follow him.**

**And the fifth thing God did was tell him to eat again and go back the way he came.** *Go, return on thy way to the wilderness of Damascus* (19:15). **The prophet who had fled to the extreme south of the country was sent back north, through the territory he had run from, to do the work that remained.**

**And the sixth and final thing to be learned from the passage is what God did not do: He did not rebuke him for fleeing, and He did not accept his resignation.** There is no word of censure in the chapter for the flight, and there is no agreement with the request to die.

**Which is a template for how an older generation should treat a burnt-out older generation.** Feed him, hear him, correct his arithmetic, give him a successor, and send him back to the road. **And above all, do not accept the resignation offered in the wilderness as the man's final word. Elijah's last years were the most productive of his life, and they were spent training a farmer he found at a plough.**

### The two errors at the point of transition

**And the crisis and the emergence produce two opposite errors, and one belongs to each party.**

**The error of the exhausted older man is to conclude that the state of the work equals the state of his heart.** Elijah's sentence — *I, even I only, am left* — was an accurate description of his feelings and a false description of the country. **A man who has collapsed will frequently interpret his own condition as information about the future of the work, and he will therefore delay the transfer or dramatise it.**

**And the error of the emerging young man is to be impatient with a mentor in that state.** Elisha's response to a decade of domestic obscurity was to remain, and to keep pouring water on the man's hands, and to refuse the three offers to be sent home. **A young man who has decided that his mentor is finished before God has finished with him will frequently inherit nothing, because the inheritance is passed to the man who stayed.**

**And the right posture is stated in the imagery of the whole episode.** The older man wrapped his face in his mantle in the cave; the mantle was the thing that would be passed on; **and the young man's entire duty was to stay close enough to be near it when it fell.**

*And he took the mantle of Elijah that fell from him, and smote the waters, and said, Where is the Lord God of Elijah?* (2 Kings 2:14).

**The mantle fell, and it fell on the man who was standing there.**

### Principles

**First: collapse can follow triumph within a day, and the peak is often the trigger.** Carmel, and then a queen's threat, and then a desert shrub and a request to die. *For I am not better than my fathers* is the sound of a man who expected the fire to settle everything.

**Second: God's first treatment of an exhausted leader is not counsel but sleep and food.** *Arise and eat; because the journey is too great for thee.* Twice, before any conversation.

**Third: the feeling of aloneness is real and the arithmetic of aloneness is usually false.** *I, even I only, am left* — and *I have left me seven thousand in Israel*, unnoticed by the man at the centre.

**Fourth: God is not in the wind, the earthquake or the fire, and the successor is raised in the thin silence.** *Qol demamah daqqah.* The instruments that impress a crowd are not the instruments that build a man.

**Fifth: the successor is named on the predecessor's worst day.** *Elisha the son of Shaphat of Abel-meholah shalt thou anoint to be prophet in thy room* — appointed before the predecessor had recovered, and provided for by God in advance.

**Sixth: the answer to a man who thinks he is the last one standing is an assignment that contains his own replacement.** Two kings to anoint, one prophet to appoint, and a road to walk back up.

**Seventh: the apprenticeship is served in the ordinary tasks, and it is long.** *Which poured water on the hands of Elijah* — the title of the greatest successor in Israel for a decade of domestic service.

**And eighth: the older man who accepts his departure begins to give things away.** *Ask what I shall do for thee, before I be taken away from thee.* And three times he offered a way out, and the young man refused all three, which is why the mantle fell on him.

### For the fathers

- **Treat the body before the heart.** Twice the angel said *arise and eat* before anything was discussed. Do not make vocational decisions in a state of exhaustion, and do not let the men around you make them either.
- **Correct your arithmetic before you act on your feelings.** *I, even I only, am left* was true as a feeling and false as a fact. Ask for the number.
- **Expect the next stage to be quieter than the last.** The wind, the earthquake and the fire were not the instruments; the thin silence was. Ministry that is built entirely on spectacle has no mechanism for forming a man.
- **Name your successor, in writing, and do it before you feel ready.** God named Elisha on the worst day of Elijah's life. Your readiness is not a required input.
- **Give the young man a job with a basin in it.** Elisha's decade of service was not a delay. It was the training, and it produced a man who could ask for the double portion and know what he was asking for.
- **And when you accept that you are leaving, start giving things away.** *Ask what I shall do for thee, before I be taken away from thee.*

### For the young

- **Do not read your mentor's exhaustion as God's verdict on him.** Elijah asked to die in the wilderness, and his last years were his most productive. You are watching a chapter, not the whole book.
- **Serve in the ordinary tasks, and stay for as long as it takes.** *Which poured water on the hands of Elijah* is the résumé of a man who received a double portion. The inheritance goes to the one who is in the room.
- **Refuse the honoured way out.** Three times Elijah said *tarry here*, and three times Elisha went on. The offers of early comfort are the ordinary test of a successor.
- **Be near the mantle, and watch him go.** *If thou see me when I am taken from thee, it shall be so unto thee.* The one requirement was presence at the end.
- **Learn his God and not only his methods.** The question at the Jordan was *where is the Lord God of Elijah*, and it could not be answered by imitating a style.
- **And if you find no seven thousand, look again, and count carefully.** Elijah's sense of being the last man standing was measured against a nation he had not audited.

### Questions for reflection

1. What has your greatest success left you feeling, and what comparison is driving it?
2. Whose exhaustion have you interpreted as a verdict on their ministry?
3. Who is pouring water on your hands, and what are you doing to prepare him for the day he will not be able to ask you anything?
4. When did you last refuse an easier assignment in order to stay close to the man who is forming you?
5. What have you done with the mantle you were handed — and did you stay close enough to see it fall?
