## The Ancient School of Seeing

There is a parenthetical remark in the first book of Samuel, dropped into the narrative as an aside, which tells us more about the pre-monarchic religion of Israel than any formal description could.

*Beforetime in Israel, when a man went to enquire of God, thus he spake, Come, and let us go to the seer: for he that is now called a Prophet was beforetime called a Seer* (1 Samuel 9:9).

The narrator is explaining a change in vocabulary, as a man might explain that a road once called the Old North Road is now called the A1. And in doing so he preserves a fragment of institutional memory: Israel once had, and evidently valued, a class of persons whose defining characteristic was not speech but *sight*.

This chapter is about those persons — the seers, the prophets, the dreamers and the interpreters — and about the deliberate, patient process by which God forms them. It is also about the fact that this formation is *generational*, because the school of seeing has never had a syllabus, and the only way to learn it is to sit at the feet of someone who sees.

### The vocabulary of perception

Scripture uses at least five distinct terms for those who receive revelation, and the distinctions are worth marking because they describe different modes of reception rather than different ranks of office.

**The prophet** (*nabi*) is the most familiar. The word appears to derive from a root meaning to bubble up or to pour forth, and it has an ecstatic background in the ancient world, but in Israel the office is domesticated by its content: a prophet is one who speaks the words God puts in his mouth. This is how Moses defines the office in Deuteronomy: *I will raise them up a Prophet from among their brethren, like unto thee, and will put my words in his mouth; and he shall speak unto them all that I shall command him* (Deuteronomy 18:18). The prophet is defined by a mouth.

**The seer** (*ro'eh*) is defined by an eye. He perceives things that are not accessible to ordinary observation — where lost animals are, what has happened in the spirit world, what a king should do. Samuel functions as a *ro'eh* in 1 Samuel 9 when he tells Saul about the asses before he tells him about the throne.

**The man of God** (*ish ha'elohim*) is defined by his relation, and the phrase emphasises the authority that comes from that relation. It is used of Moses, of Samuel, of Elijah and Elisha, and of the unnamed prophet who denounces Jeroboam's altar (1 Kings 13:1).

**The watchman** (*tsopheh*) is a metaphor drawn from city walls, and it is used by the prophets themselves. The watchman does not make the news; he sees what is coming and warns. *Son of man, I have made thee a watchman unto the house of Israel* (Ezekiel 3:17). The metaphor carries the whole burden of prophetic accountability.

**The dreamer** (*ba'al hachalom*) is defined by the passivity of the mode. He receives while he is not in control, which is precisely the point, and which explains both the frequency of dreams as a channel of revelation and the suspicion with which they are regarded.

Five terms, then. A mouth, an eye, a relation, a warning and a sleeping recipient. And the important thing to observe is that none of these is a *qualification* in the sense that a modern institution uses the word. Nobody certifies a seer.

### Why God speaks in more than one mode

The variety itself is instructive, and Numbers 12 gives us a formal account of it from God's own mouth, spoken to Aaron and Miriam when they had challenged Moses' authority.

*And he said, Hear now my words: If there be a prophet among you, I the Lord will make myself known unto him in a vision, and will speak unto him in a dream. My servant Moses is not so, who is faithful in all mine house. With him will I speak mouth to mouth, even apparently, and not in dark speeches; and the similitude of the Lord shall he behold* (Numbers 12:6–8).

This is remarkable. God does not rebuke Aaron and Miriam for their expectation of revelation, only for their presumption about its distribution. He begins by conceding that dreams and visions are His normal mode: *if there be a prophet among you*, that is how I will come to him. And then He distinguishes Moses not because Moses alone receives revelation, but because Moses receives it with a different intimacy: mouth to mouth, without the veil of a dream.

The right conclusion to draw from this passage is *not* that ordinary revelation is inferior. It is that God deliberately distributes His self-disclosure across a range of modes. And this distribution has a consequence that bears directly on our subject: it means that no single generation can claim to contain the whole of the knowledge of God.

Amos states the principle as a positive doctrine: *Surely the Lord God will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets* (Amos 3:7). The word translated *secret* is *sod*, which denotes a council, a counsel, a confidential consultation. God does not act without convening a council, and the prophets are the ones He admits to it. But no individual prophet is present at every council. Jeremiah sees some things; Ezekiel sees others; Daniel is given a timeline nobody else receives; Zechariah sees eight night visions with details that appear nowhere else in Scripture.

The knowledge of God is therefore **cumulative across generations**, because the councils span generations. This is a structural fact about revelation, not an accident about the amount of information available.

### The school of the prophets

Sometime during the prophetic era, Israel developed institutions for the formation of those who would carry revelation. They appear in the text as *the sons of the prophets*, and they are sufficiently organised that we can make out some details.

Elijah is approached, near the end of his life, in this way: *And the sons of the prophets that were at Bethel came forth to Elisha, and said unto him, Knowest thou that the Lord will take away thy master from thy head to day?* (2 Kings 2:3). The same scene is repeated at Jericho (2:5) and at the Jordan (2:7), and at the Jordan fifty men stand to watch at a distance (2:7). Fifty men are also sent to search for Elijah's body (2:17).

We are therefore looking at communities: groups of men, resident in towns, apparently under the direction of a master. Records survive of their provisioning arrangements. When Elisha increases the widow's oil, he does so within a community whose financial difficulties are a matter of general concern (2 Kings 4:1). When he multiplies the loaves, a man comes bringing *twenty loaves of barley ... in his husk*, and Elisha says, *Give unto the people, that they may eat* (2 Kings 4:42). When the sons of the prophets build a larger dwelling, they go with Elisha to the Jordan, cut wood, and lose a borrowed axe-head in the water — which Elisha retrieves (2 Kings 6:1–7). And when the Syrian army besieges Samaria and food becomes desperately scarce, the sons of the prophets are among those with whom Elisha sits (2 Kings 4:38).

This is not mysticism in the sense of solitary illumination. It is a **school**: a community of formation, with buildings, with a leader, with provisions, with practical problems, and with the ordinary intergenerational friction that such arrangements produce. The sons of the prophets at Jericho are apparently attempting to modernise their accommodation and propose to Elisha that they should go and dwell there, and Elisha assents and goes with them. When one of them proposes that a particular piece of land is pleasant, he is answered by a colleague who owns the difficulty (2 Kings 6:2).

Now consider what this institutionalisation achieves, and why it matters for this book.

**First, it makes transmission deliberate rather than accidental.** A man with a gift can train a son. A school can train fifty students, generation after generation, with continuity across the death of any individual master.

**Second, it preserves a body of method.** The sons of the prophets knew how to perform music for prophecy (1 Samuel 10:5; 1 Chronicles 25), how to keep records, how to discern the quality of a word. None of this is written down as a curriculum, but the institution itself is the curriculum.

**Third, it embeds the prophetic calling in a community rather than an individual.** And this has an important consequence for our subject: when the prophetic schools failed, the office did not disappear; it merely detached itself from accountability and became a freelance profession, which is what happens in the book of Amos, and what a prophet writes a whole book about.

**Fourth — and this is the point most relevant to generational revelation — schools outlive individuals.** Elijah's personal impact ends at the Jordan. The sons of the prophets, and their descendants, are still functioning in the days of Elisha, and the schools continue to appear in the record for centuries, as late as the world of the prophets, and in the period of the return, when Zechariah the prophet is himself described as the son of a line of priests and prophets.

### The formation of a seer

Set out the sequence by which Scripture describes the making of a prophet, and a pattern emerges that is markedly different from how we ordinarily speak of calling.

#### 1. The initiative is God's

Amos is emphatic. When Amaziah the priest of Bethel tells him to go back to Judah and prophesy there for money, Amos answers with a sentence that dismantles the professional model of prophecy in a single line: *I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: and the Lord took me as I followed the flock, and the Lord said unto me, Go, prophesy unto my people Israel* (Amos 7:14–15).

The sentence has a deliberate structure. Amos denies the two routes by which a man became a prophet: personal decision (*I was no prophet*) and inheritance (*neither was I a prophet's son*). And then he supplies the actual mechanism — the Lord took me and the Lord said.

Jeremiah receives the same doctrine with an even more startling implication: *Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee, and I ordained thee a prophet unto the nations* (Jeremiah 1:5). Before he was born. Before the argument about his qualifications had even a subject.

#### 2. The candidate is separated

Elijah is withdrawn from the public record for years — first by the brook Cherith, where ravens feed him, and then by a widow in Zarephath (1 Kings 17:2–24). Paul, after his conversion, goes into Arabia (Galatians 1:17), and three years elapse before he appears in Jerusalem. John the Baptist is *in the wilderness* until the day of his showing (Luke 1:80).

This is a point at which modern practice diverges sharply from biblical pattern. We put a young man with evident gift immediately into public ministry, and then wonder why his gift is accompanied by an unformed character. Scripture removes him first.

#### 3. The Scripture comes before the vision

And here is the step most often omitted from accounts of prophetic formation. The word in the mouth of the prophet is the word of the book.

Look at the commissioning of Joshua, whose office was military and administrative rather than prophetic, and notice what he is given: not a sign but a document. *This book of the law shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night, that thou mayest observe to do according to all that is written therein: for then thou shalt make thy way prosperous, and then thou shalt have good success* (Joshua 1:8).

Look at the strange and disturbing account of Ezekiel's calling, in which he is handed a scroll and told to eat it (Ezekiel 2:9–3:3), and the scroll has writing on both sides, *and there was written therein lamentations, and mourning, and woe*. And Ezekiel eats it, and it is in his mouth *as honey for sweetness*. The message must be internalised before it is delivered; the prophet is a man who has first digested the book.

Look at John, in the revelation given at Patmos, told to take the little book from the angel's hand and *eat it up; and it shall make thy belly bitter, but it shall be in thy mouth sweet as honey* (Revelation 10:9). And he takes it and eats it, and the result is exactly what he was told.

The pattern is consistent. The prophet is not a man with unusually vivid impressions. He is a man so saturated in the written word that when God speaks fresh, he is able to recognise and interpret what he hears.

#### 4. The word is sent, and the sender takes responsibility for it

Here is the most comforting feature of the whole arrangement. In Jeremiah's commissioning, the prophet objects on the grounds of youth, and God answers not with a reassurance about Jeremiah's ability but with a transfer of responsibility: *But the Lord said unto me, Say not, I am a child: for thou shalt go to all that I shall send thee, and whatsoever I command thee thou shalt speak. Be not afraid of their faces: for I am with thee to deliver thee, saith the Lord* (Jeremiah 1:7–8).

*Be not afraid of their faces.* The fear of faces is the prophet's characteristic weakness — the temptation to adjust the message by the expression of the listener. And God deals with it not by strengthening Jeremiah's nerve but by promising His own presence, and by adding a specific guarantee: *for I am with thee to deliver thee*.

Then, three verses later, the mechanism is described in physical terms. God puts forth His hand and touches Jeremiah's mouth and says, *Behold, I have put my words in thy mouth* (Jeremiah 1:9). The prophet does not generate content. He carries it.

#### 5. The prophet is sustained through contradiction

Every prophet in Scripture is opposed, and the opposition is not incidental to the office — it is constitutive of it. A prophet whose word is always welcomed is not prophesying.

Elijah opposes a king and is called the troubler of Israel (1 Kings 18:17). Micaiah is asked to agree with four hundred prophets and refuses (1 Kings 22:13–14), and is then beaten and imprisoned. Jeremiah is beaten, put in stocks, thrown into a cistern, and left to die in the mud. Amos is accused of conspiracy. Hosea's whole domestic life becomes a sign.

The prophet's suffering is not a divine oversight. It is the price of a word that contradicts the consensus of the people to whom it is sent.

#### 6. The word is finally authenticated by fulfilment

And here is the criterion God Himself gives for distinguishing a true prophet from a false one: *When a prophet speaketh in the name of the Lord, if the thing follow not, nor come to pass, that is the thing which the Lord hath not spoken, but the prophet hath spoken it presumptuously* (Deuteronomy 18:22).

Note the patience this requires. A prophet's authenticity may not be evident until decades after his death. Jeremiah's word about the seventy years of exile was vindicated long after Jeremiah was dead. Which means that the authentication of prophetic ministry is itself a *generational* matter: one generation speaks, and a later generation discovers whether the word was from God.

### The transfer of the prophetic office

We have been describing the formation of individuals. But the question this book is pursuing is how the office is carried across a generation. And here Scripture gives one of its clearest pictures.

When Elijah is taken up, Elisha receives the mantle — but the actual event of transfer is stranger and more interesting than the word *mantle* suggests. Elisha had already been called, had already followed, had already served for years. He had already refused to be separated from his master three times: at Gilgal, at Bethel, at Jericho, and again at the Jordan (2 Kings 2:1–6). When the two of them crossed the Jordan, Elijah asked what he should do for Elisha before he was taken, and Elisha answered with a request that reveals exactly what he understood the office to consist of.

*And Elisha said, I pray thee, let a double portion of thy spirit be upon me* (2 Kings 2:9).

The phrase translated *double portion* is a technical legal term: the inheritance share of the firstborn son. Elisha is not asking for twice as much as Elijah, which would be a bizarre request. He is asking to be recognised as the *heir* — and he asks it in legal language.

And Elijah's answer is instructive: *Thou hast asked a hard thing: nevertheless, if thou see me when I am taken from thee, it shall be so unto thee; but if not, it shall not be so* (2 Kings 2:10). The condition is personal, not administrative. The gift depends on whether Elisha is present at the moment of departure — which is to say, on a relationship sustained to the very end.

This is the pattern for the transfer of any spiritual office. What travels is not the office; it is the *inheritance*, and the inheritance is conferred in relationship, and the relationship must last.

### When the school fails

We must not idealise the schools, because Scripture does not.

By the time of Jeremiah, the prophets have become a profession. Jeremiah complains, *For from the least of them even unto the greatest of them every one is given to covetousness; and from the prophet even unto the priest every one dealeth falsely* (Jeremiah 6:13). He describes the guild of prophets who speak peace when there is no peace, and who say *no evil shall come upon you* (Jeremiah 23:17), because that is what the client wants to hear.

The mechanism of the failure is worth understanding, because it is a mechanism that recurs in every generation of the church. The prophets had become dependent on their audiences. A court prophet lives on the king's favour. A popular prophet lives on the crowd's attention. And once the prophet's income depends on the reception of his word, his word will begin to suit his income.

Against this, God sets the criterion in Deuteronomy: *the prophet which shall presume to speak a word in my name, which I have not commanded him to speak ... even that prophet shall die* (Deuteronomy 18:20). And after Elijah's triumph on Carmel he goes into the wilderness and asks to die, and reports to God his own assessment of the situation: *I, even I only, am left; and they seek my life, to take it away* (1 Kings 19:10).

God's response is the reason this chapter belongs in this book. He corrects Elijah's arithmetic — *yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel, all the knees which have not bowed unto Baal* (1 Kings 19:18) — and then He gives Elijah an assignment with three parts, of which the third is the one that matters here: *and Elisha the son of Shaphat of Abel-meholah shalt thou anoint to be prophet in thy room* (1 Kings 19:16).

To be *prophet in thy room*. That is what a school is for.

### The seer and the boy

And so we come back to the sentence with which the chapter opened, and to the seer through whom, in the providence of God, the office became permanent in Israel.

Samuel is the pivot of the whole prophetic institution, and he is a strange choice for it. He is not raised in a prophetic school; he is raised in a corrupt shrine by a father who could not restrain his own sons. What he receives in that shrine is not information but a *capacity*: the ability to recognise God's voice and answer it. And the record of his growth is a sentence about transmission: *And the Lord appeared again in Shiloh: for the Lord revealed himself to Samuel in Shiloh by the word of the Lord. And the word of Samuel came to all Israel* (1 Samuel 3:21–4:1).

*The Lord revealed himself to Samuel.* Nowhere does the text say that Eli taught him to prophesy. Eli taught him to say, *Speak, Lord; for thy servant heareth* (1 Samuel 3:9). And that, in the end, is what the school of seeing is: a place where an older generation teaches a younger one how to answer the voice of God, and then steps back to let the answer come.

That is a lower ambition and a greater one than it sounds. A father cannot give his son a revelation. He can teach him to listen for one.
