There is a man in the New Testament who is never mentioned in the book of Acts, who appears in only thirteen verses of the letters that bear his name, and whom Paul trusted with three of the most difficult assignments recorded in the apostolic church. He is not the subject of a single miracle, a single sermon, or a single controversy of his own. He exists in the record almost entirely as the man Paul sent.

His name is Titus. And the reason this chapter is in this book is that Titus is the model of the second-generation leader — the one who does not found the work but finishes it, who does not receive the title but does the job, and whose whole theology, according to Paul, is summed up in a phrase from 2 Corinthians: *walked we not in the same steps?*

### The unmentioned man

Start with his absence from Acts.

Luke's history covers the apostolic age in considerable detail. It names Barnabas, Silas, Timothy, Apollos, Aquila and Priscilla, Philip, Stephen, Agabus, James, and dozens of others. It records the Jerusalem council in full, including the speeches.

Titus is not in it. He was present at the Jerusalem council — Paul says so in Galatians 2:1, *I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, and took Titus with me also* — and Luke's account of that same council, in Acts 15, does not mention him.

**A man who was in the room is absent from the minutes.** This happens in every institution. The people who do the work that holds the thing together are often invisible in the record.

And what did this invisible man do?

**He was Paul's envoy to Corinth in the worst crisis of that church's history.** That is 2 Corinthians 2, 7 and 8.

**He was left in Crete to organise the churches of the island.** *For this cause left I thee in Crete, that thou shouldest set in order the things that are wanting, and ordain elders in every city, as I had appointed thee* (Titus 1:5).

**He was sent to Dalmatia.** *Titus unto Dalmatia* (2 Timothy 4:10) — Paul's last list of those who had left him, and Titus is on it, doing a job.

**And he was the one who carried money.** 2 Corinthians 8 records that Titus was one of a three-man delegation appointed to carry the collection for the poor saints in Jerusalem.

Three hard assignments, and one of them was the handling of a significant sum of other people's money.

**The New Testament does not record one thing Titus said.** In thirteen verses bearing his name, all of them instructions *to* him, there is not a single speech, argument, or sermon. He is a perfect blank of a man, and his reputation is entirely a matter of what he did.

### The Corinthian crisis

We must now look at the assignment that reveals him, because 2 Corinthians preserves more about Titus than any other part of Scripture.

The church at Corinth had gone into open revolt against Paul. There had been a person in the congregation who insulted him publicly (the offender of 2 Corinthians 2:5–8), the church had tolerated it, and Paul had written them a letter of such severity that he afterwards regretted having to write it.

*For out of much affliction and anguish of heart I wrote unto you with many tears; not that ye should be grieved, but that ye might know the love which I have more abundantly unto you* (2 Corinthians 2:4).

That is the letter to which 2 Corinthians is the follow-up — the *severe letter*, now lost. And Paul, having sent it, was in an agony of uncertainty about what it had done. And then he writes this:

*Furthermore, when I came to Troas to preach Christ's gospel, and a door was opened unto me of the Lord, I had no rest in my spirit, because I found not Titus my brother: but taking my leave of them, I went from thence into Macedonia* (2 Corinthians 2:12–13).

*I had no rest in my spirit, because I found not Titus my brother.* Paul left an open door for the gospel — *a door was opened unto me of the Lord* — because he could not bear the wait for the young man who was carrying the news.

And then the arrival:

*Nevertheless God, that comforteth those that are cast down, comforted us by the coming of Titus; and not by his coming only, but by the consolation wherewith he was comforted in you, when he told us your earnest desire, your mourning, your fervent mind toward me; so that I rejoiced the more* (2 Corinthians 7:6–7).

Set out the structure of what Paul says.

**God is *the God that comforteth those that are cast down*.** The Greek is *ho parakalon tous tapeinous* — the one who encourages the lowly. Paul was at the bottom.

**He comforted us *by the coming of Titus*.** The mechanism of divine comfort was the arrival of a person.

**And not by the coming only**, but by the report. *The consolation wherewith he was comforted in you.* Titus had been received warmly by the Corinthians, and his own spirit had been ministered to by their response, and he carried that back. **Paul was comforted by the fact that his young man had been comforted.**

That mutual texture is the whole substance of 2 Corinthians 7:13–15:

*Therefore we were comforted in your comfort: yea, and exceedingly the more joyed we for the joy of Titus, because his spirit was refreshed by you all. For if I have boasted any thing to him of you, I am not ashamed; but as we spake all things to you in truth, even so our boasting, which I made before Titus, is found a truth. And his inward affection is more abundant toward you, whilst he remembereth the obedience of you all, how with fear and trembling ye received him.*

**Paul had boasted about the Corinthians to Titus before Titus arrived.** *Our boasting, which I made before Titus, is found a truth.* He had described the Corinthians to the young man in advance — and the Corinthians had lived up to the description.

Note what this reveals about how Paul briefed a younger man on a congregation. **He did not send Titus in expecting to be disappointed.** He spoke of the churches in his absence as he spoke of them in their presence. This is the older man's integrity in briefing the younger: the description given in private matches the description given in public.

And note the effect on Titus: *his inward affection is more abundant toward you*. The Greek is *ta splanchna autou perissoteros eis hymas estin* — his bowels, his deep affections, are more abundantly toward you. Titus came back from a difficult assignment loving the people more than when he went.

**That is the mark of a real minister.** Pride comes back from a hard assignment with a report. Affection comes back with love.

### The man who went unbidden

And then the detail that most clearly reveals his character, and that states the principle of this chapter.

Paul is organising the collection for the Jerusalem poor, and he writes this:

*But thanks be to God, which put the same earnest care into the heart of Titus for you. For indeed he accepted the exhortation; but being more forward, of his own accord he went unto you* (2 Corinthians 8:16–17).

*He accepted the exhortation* — Paul asked him, and he agreed. *But being more forward* — the Greek is *spoudaioteros hyparchon*, being more eager, more diligent. *Of his own accord he went unto you* — *authairetos exelthen pros hymas*. The word *authairetos* means self-chosen, voluntary, of one's own impulse.

**Titus did what he was asked, and then more than he was asked, without being asked.** He was not merely compliant; he was eager.

This is the single most important character quality in a second-generation leader, and Paul names it as the reason he thanks God. **Not competence. Not loyalty. Eagerness that exceeds instruction.**

Institutions are full of people who will do precisely what they are told and not one step further. They are reliable and they are useless for anything that has not yet been planned. The people who carry a work forward are those who have owned the work's purposes so thoroughly that they act on them unprompted.

And note Paul's concluding formula in the same passage:

*I desired Titus, and with him I sent a brother. Did Titus make a gain of you? walked we not in the same spirit? walked we not in the same steps?* (2 Corinthians 12:18).

**Walked we not in the same steps?** The Greek is *tois autois ichnesin* — the same footprints, the same tracks. The word *ichnos* means the print left by a foot.

And that is a definition of succession in four words. **Not the same office; the same footprints.** Not the same title; the same gait. The successor is identified by where the prints fall, not by the name on the door.

Paul says: I sent Titus, and you cannot tell the difference between his conduct and mine. The same spirit and the same steps.

### The three-man rule

And there is one further detail in the 2 Corinthians 8 account that deserves separate treatment, because it is a permanent administrative principle given in Scripture.

*And we have sent with him the brother, whose praise is in the gospel throughout all the churches; and not that only, but who was also chosen of the churches to travel with us with this grace, which is administered by us to the glory of the same Lord, and declaration of your ready mind: avoiding this, that no man should blame us in this abundance which is administered by us: providing for honest things, not only in the sight of the Lord, but also in the sight of men* (2 Corinthians 8:18–21).

*Avoiding this, that no man should blame us ... providing for honest things, not only in the sight of the Lord, but also in the sight of men.*

Paul, the apostle, refuses to handle a large sum of money alone. He arranges for a delegation: Titus, an unnamed brother praised throughout all the churches, and a third who was chosen by the churches themselves. And the reason is stated in the text: so that nobody can accuse him.

**And note that the principle is applied to the man with the best possible reputation.** Paul had no personal exposure. He was the founder of most of these churches and had taken no money from Corinth (2 Corinthians 11:7–9). And he still would not administer the collection alone.

This chapter is about the transmission of responsibility from one generation to the next, and this text belongs in it because it is one of the clearest statements in Scripture about how to hand responsibility to the young. **You hand it over in a company, with witnesses, and with the accountability built into the structure.**

And the delegation was not purely symbolic. Titus was accompanied by men who were chosen by the churches themselves, so the young man's accounting was to the church, not merely to Paul. **The older man built a structure in which the younger man would be accountable to someone other than himself.**

That is a crucial principle. A young leader who is accountable only to his mentor is not accountable. He is dependent. And accountability that runs only one way, to one person, is the arrangement under which abuse flourishes.

### Crete and the unfinished work

And now the assignment that gives this chapter its theme, and the verse that states the theology of the second generation.

*For this cause left I thee in Crete, that thou shouldest set in order the things that are wanting, and ordain elders in every city, as I had appointed thee* (Titus 1:5).

*Set in order the things that are wanting.* The Greek is *ta leiponta epidiorthose* — to further straighten out the things that are left over, the things that are lacking. The verb *epidiorthoo* occurs nowhere else in the New Testament, and it is built on *orthos*, straight. It means to straighten up further, to put right what remains.

**Titus was left behind to do the second half of the work.** Paul had evangelised Crete. Paul had planted. Paul had moved on. And what remained was the unglamorous part: appoint elders in every city, correct the false teaching, order the households, and establish the structures that would outlast the founder's visit.

Look at the quality of the congregation Paul left him, because this was not a pleasant assignment. In the same chapter Paul writes:

*For there are many unruly and vain talkers and deceivers, specially they of the circumcision: whose mouths must be stopped; who subvert whole houses, teaching things which they ought not, for filthy lucre's sake. One of themselves, even a prophet of their own, said, The Cretians are alway liars, evil beasts, slow bellies. This witness is true. Wherefore rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith* (Titus 1:10–13).

That is Paul's assessment of the people of Crete, quoting one of their own poets — the line is from Epimenides, a Cretan writer of the sixth century BC, and Paul treats it as accurate. *Alway liars, evil beasts, slow bellies.* And his instruction to Titus is *rebuke them sharply*.

**The young man was left alone on an island full of false teachers, with a population proverbially difficult, and told to appoint elders in every city.**

And to him Paul writes the shortest and most direct of all his letters, with no personal requests and no closing affections — just doctrine, order and instruction. Titus did not need the encouragement that Timothy needed. He needed the specification.

**The two young men were different, and Paul treated them differently.** To Timothy, whose letters run heavy with *son*, *be not ashamed*, *stir up the gift*, Paul gave affection. To Titus, whose letter runs heavy with *speak*, *exhort*, *rebuke*, *set in order*, Paul gave orders. He did not apply one template to the young.

### The curriculum of Titus 2

And now we must examine the passage that makes Titus, rather than Timothy, the model of spiritual reproduction — because Titus 2 is the most detailed programme of intergenerational transmission in the New Testament.

*But speak thou the things which become sound doctrine: that the aged men be sober, grave, temperate, sound in faith, in charity, in patience. The aged women likewise, that they be in behaviour as becometh holiness, not false accusers, not given to much wine, teachers of good things; that they may teach the young women to be sober, to love their husbands, to love their children, to be discreet, chaste, keepers at home, good, obedient to their own husbands, that the word of God be not blasphemed. Young men likewise exhort to be sober minded. In all things shewing thyself a pattern of good works: in doctrine shewing uncorruptness, gravity, sincerity, sound speech, that cannot be condemned; that he that is of the contrary part may be ashamed, having no evil thing to say of you* (Titus 2:1–8).

The passage has a structure, and the structure is the argument.

| Addressed | Who is to teach | Method |
|---|---|---|
| aged men | (implicitly, Titus) | direct instruction |
| aged women | (implicitly, Titus) | direct instruction |
| young women | **the aged women** | *that they may teach* |
| young men | Titus | direct instruction |
| Titus himself | — | *shewing thyself a pattern* |

**One category in this passage is assigned to peers within the generation, and it is the young women.** The aged women are *to teach the young women* — the Greek verb is *sophronizo*, to make sober-minded, to bring to soundness of mind. And the list of things they are to teach is domestic: to love their husbands, to love their children, to be discreet, chaste, keepers at home, good, obedient to their own husbands.

The reason given for the whole arrangement is the last clause: *that the word of God be not blasphemed.* Not *that the church may grow*, not *that the families may flourish*. The purpose is that outsiders should have nothing to say against the Scripture. This is a public-relations argument, and it is a theological one: **the conduct of the younger generation determines what the world says about the word of God.**

And then the last line of the section, which is addressed not to a group but to Titus himself: *In all things shewing thyself a pattern of good works.*

The Greek is *peri panta seauton parechomenos typon kalon ergon* — in all things presenting yourself as a type, a pattern, of good works. **The verse requires the young leader to become the pattern.** Not to commission a curriculum, or write a manual, or appoint a committee. To *be* the model, visibly, in his own person.

And note that Titus is a young man. He is administering a programme in which the aged men and aged women are instructed to behave in certain ways, and *he* is instructed to be, in his own conduct, the pattern that validates the instruction.

**That is what the young leader's authority actually is.** Not his office — he has one, and Paul tells him to use it in verse 15 — but his example.

### "Let no man despise thee"

And now the closing line of the chapter, and it is the same instruction Timothy received, in a different form.

*These things speak, and exhort, and rebuke with all authority. Let no man despise thee* (Titus 2:15).

*With all authority.* The Greek is *meta pases epitages* — with all command, with full authority. The noun *epitage* denotes a formal command issued by one who has the right to issue it. Paul tells the young man on Crete to exercise the full weight of his authority in speaking, exhorting and rebuking.

And then: *Let no man despise thee.* The Greek is *medeis sou periphroneito* — let no one despise, or look down upon, or think around you. The verb *periphroneo* literally means to think around something, that is, to pass over it, to consider it not worth attention.

**The same construction that appears in 1 Timothy 4:12, and in the same grammatical form — a third-person imperative.** *Let him not despise.* It is not an instruction to Titus about his feelings. It is an instruction to the congregation about their behaviour. Paul is telling the church on Crete: do not treat this young man as beneath you.

But he says it *to Titus*, which means Titus is to read it as a charge to make sure there is no ground for it. And in 1 Timothy, Paul supplies the means explicitly: *but be thou an example... in word, in conversation, in charity, in spirit, in faith, in purity.*

**Authority is commanded, and it is also earned.** Paul tells the young man to speak with full authority, and tells the congregation not to despise him, and tells him to be the pattern that makes the command reasonable.

### What Titus teaches about the second generation

Let me now set out the chapter's principles, because this narrative supplies the clearest theology of second-generation ministry in Scripture.

**First: the second generation finishes what the first generation began.** Paul evangelised Crete and left. Titus *set in order the things that are wanting*. The Greek word for what Titus was left to complete literally means *the things left over*. Founders begin; successors complete. And the completing is not less significant than the beginning.

**Second: the successor's legitimacy comes from walking in the same steps, not from holding the same office.** *Walked we not in the same steps?* The test is conduct, not title.

**Third: eagerness beyond instruction is the distinguishing quality.** *He accepted the exhortation; but being more forward, of his own accord he went unto you.* The successor must own the purposes of the work, not merely the tasks assigned to him.

**Fourth: the young leader must be given real responsibility, and it must include accountability to someone other than his mentor.** Paul would not administer money alone, and he would not let the young man do it either. He built a three-man structure, chosen partly by the churches themselves.

**Fifth: the older man must not treat all young leaders alike.** Timothy needed affection and reminders; Titus needed directions and specifications. Paul gave each what he needed. A single template for the young is evidence of a failure to know them.

**Sixth: the young leader's authority is his pattern of good works.** *Shewing thyself a pattern.* The Greek *typos* is a stamp or a die — the implement that makes an impression in another material. The young man is to be the die from which the congregation is struck.

**Seventh, and most important for this book: the content of the transmission is character, not information.** Look at the lists in Titus 2. Nothing is transmitted that is a doctrine in the abstract. The aged men transmit soberness, gravity, temperance, faith, charity and patience. The aged women transmit behaviour, holiness, and a refusal to slander. The young women receive instruction in loving their husbands and their children. The young man receives an exhortation to be sober-minded.

**Every item is a quality of life, and the whole programme is a structured system by which one generation's character is reproduced in another.**

That is spiritual reproduction, and it is a far more demanding thing than teaching. You cannot transmit soberness to a young woman by giving a lecture on it. You transmit it by being sober in her presence for a decade.

### The last mention

And we should end with the detail that closes the record.

In Paul's final letter, in the list of those who are absent, among the departures that clearly grieve him — *Demas hath forsaken me, having loved this present world* — there is this:

*Titus unto Dalmatia* (2 Timothy 4:10).

By the time of that letter, the young man who had walked in the same steps as the apostle was in the Balkans, on assignment, opening a new region, and not in Rome to see the old man one last time.

It is not recorded as a failure. It is recorded as a fact. **The apprentice had become an apostle in his own right, and was doing the work somewhere else, and Paul lists him among the absentees with the same neutral brevity he gives Crescens in Gaul.**

That is what success looks like at the end. The successor is not at the bedside. He is in Dalmatia, doing the thing he was trained to do, with the same spirit and the same steps, and the old man writes his name in a letter without complaint.

**A man who has reproduced himself in a younger man has given up the right to be the centre of the younger man's story.** Paul understood this, and recorded Titus' absence in the same paragraph as Demas' betrayal, and did not confuse the two.
