## Preface

Every book has a moment when it stops being an idea and becomes a debt. This one began at a graveside in Oyo State, in the middle of a long afternoon, when a man I had known all my life was lowered into the ground and I found myself standing among his grandchildren, who did not know what he had done.

They were not indifferent. They were uninformed. Between the man and the boys there had been a silence of perhaps thirty years, maintained by good manners on both sides, and in that silence a whole library had gone out of the world unread. He had built a church out of nothing. He had walked eleven miles each way to a school he was not permitted to attend, and sat outside the window until the teacher relented. He had buried three children and kept singing. None of it was written down, because in his generation a man was not supposed to write about himself, and none of it was spoken, because in that same generation a man was not supposed to boast.

So the grandchildren inherited a name, a piece of land, and a set of expectations they could not explain. They inherited the *results* of a life without inheriting the life. They were, in the precise biblical sense, a generation that arose which knew not.

I came home from that burial with a question that would not leave me, and it was not the question I expected. The expected question is *why do the young forget?* The question that actually presented itself was sharper and less flattering to my own generation of elders: **why did we not tell them?**

That question is the origin of this book. And the more I pursued it in Scripture, the more I discovered that it is not a marginal question at all. It is one of the structural questions of the Bible. Again and again the narrative turns on whether one generation will succeed in handing over to the next what it has itself received, and again and again the crisis of the people of God is described not as a failure of worship or of morality in the first instance, but as a failure of transmission. The book of Judges does not say that the generation after Joshua worshipped other gods because they were wicked. It says they did so because *there arose another generation after them, which knew not the Lord, nor yet the works which he had done for Israel*. They did not know. Somebody had known. The knowledge did not travel.

### What this book is

This is a work of biblical theology, written for the church rather than for the academy, though I have tried to be honest enough for both. Its subject is **generational revelation**: the pattern by which God entrusts His word, His works, His warnings and His promises to a succession of custodians, and the conditions under which that word survives from one custodian to the next or perishes.

The argument of the book can be stated in a sentence, and the whole of it is an unfolding of that sentence. **God does not necessarily choose the young because the old have failed, nor does He preserve the old merely because they are old; Scripture presents a deeper architecture in which fathers preserve, elders interpret, mentors prepare, sons and daughters receive, and a new generation carries divine purposes into territories its predecessors could not enter.**

That sentence refuses two popular readings of the Bible, and the refusal is deliberate.

The first reading is the one that makes the young the heroes and the old the obstacle. It reads the Bible as a story of fresh movements of God breaking out against stale institutions, and it finds in every generation gap a divine verdict. This reading produces a certain kind of church: energetic, suspicious of authority, endlessly innovative, and — usually within a single generation — rootless. It has a text for its text, and the text is true as far as it goes: *out of the mouth of babes and sucklings hast thou ordained strength*. But it has torn that text out of a book whose recurring anxiety is the disappearance of memory.

The second reading makes the old the heroes and the young the danger. It reads the Bible as a story of received truth under constant assault from novelty, and it finds in every innovation a defection. This reading also produces a certain kind of church: stable, reverent, ordered, and — usually within a single generation — sterile. It too has a text: *ask thy father, and he will shew thee*. But it has also torn that text out of a book whose recurring anxiety is that the fathers keep what they will not say.

Scripture will not support either. It will not support them because in Scripture the two failures are frequently the same failure seen from opposite ends. A father who will not speak and a son who will not listen produce, between them, exactly one thing: a lost generation. Judges 2 does not apportion the blame. It simply records the outcome.

### What this book is not

It is not a book about young people, in the contemporary sense of that phrase. It does not argue that youth is a spiritual gift, that age is a spiritual liability, that traditions should be discarded, or that the church needs to become younger. In fact it argues the reverse at several points, and Part IX is devoted to the dangers of youthful presumption in some detail.

It is not a book about succession in the institutional sense either. There are chapters in it on the deliberate preparation of successors, because Scripture has a great deal to say about that, and I hope they will be useful to denominations, congregations and institutions facing a leadership transition. But institutional succession is one application of the book's subject, not its subject. The book's subject is the survival of revelation, which is a larger thing than the survival of an organisation, and sometimes has been better served by its failure than by its success.

It is not, finally, a book of inspirational reading. It is an argument. It moves from a statement of the pattern, through a survey of the biblical material, to a description of the mechanisms, and finally to an account of the dangers and the practice. It can be read devotionally — I hope it will be — but it cannot be read *only* devotionally without losing its point, because its point is a structure rather than a sentiment.

### How the argument proceeds

The book has eleven parts, and they are meant to be read in order.

**Part I** establishes the pattern. It argues that Scripture consistently presents revelation as *custodial* rather than merely propositional: God does not simply give truth to individuals, He gives it to a chain of individuals, and He holds each link responsible for the link that follows. Psalm 78 supplies the governing image.

**Part II** examines the mechanics of revelation and the people through whom it comes — the prophets, the seers, the dreamers, the interpreters — and asks why God so often places a revelation in a generation that cannot yet use it. Joseph, Samuel, David, Elisha and Daniel are treated here as case studies, and the pattern that emerges is uncomfortable: the youthful carrier of a revelation is frequently, and sometimes necessarily, in conflict with the generation that raised him.

**Part III** turns to the question of the title. Why the young? The answer given is not sentimental. God chooses the young not because they are better but because the alternative — choosing the established — has specific liabilities of its own, and because a purpose that must be *built* requires something the mature possess less of than the young: time, and the willingness to work without precedent.

**Part IV** is addressed to fathers, elders and mentors, and it is the hardest section of the book to read as an elder. It argues that silence is a sin, that a generation can possess wisdom and forfeit it by refusing to speak, and that the chief biblical cause of generational loss is not youthful rebellion but elderly reticence.

**Part V** describes the hidden architecture. Revelation has a timeline. Seeds have seasons. Words move from persons to households to peoples to generations. Revelation is transferable through certain channels and not through others. This is the most technical part of the book and the one on which the rest depends.

**Part VI** is about collision. Not every generation gap is a failure; some are the mechanism by which God does something new. But some are catastrophes, and Scripture describes both with equal candour. Saul and David, Rehoboam and the elders, Elijah and Elisha, and the quiet tragedy of John Mark are examined here.

**Part VII** turns to Christ, and argues that the pattern of the whole book is concentrated and perfected in Him. He is the Son who received, and He is the Master who prepared. He did not write a book; He made a generation.

**Part VIII** examines the receiving side. What does it look like, in practice, when a generation inherits an unfinished assignment and carries it further than its predecessors could? Joshua, the builders after the exile, Josiah, and the church at Pentecost are the witnesses.

**Part IX** states the dangers on both sides, because a book that has argued earnestly for youthful reception must equally insist on the testedness of revelation, the discipline of obedience, and the priority of character over gift.

**Part X** is practical. It describes the structures — mentorship, household instruction, succession planning, dialogue across generations — by which a generational covenant is built rather than merely wished for.

**Part XI** closes the argument where it began, with the promise of Joel and Malachi: that God will pour out His Spirit on sons and daughters, that He will turn the hearts of the fathers to the children and the children to the fathers, and that He will never run out of carriers for what He has said.

### Three notes on method

**On Scripture.** Quotations are from the Authorized Version, whose public-domain text is quoted directly and verified against the standard 1769 text. Where the meaning of a passage turns on a Hebrew or Greek word, the word is given in transliteration and its sense explained; readers who have no languages should be able to follow every argument, and readers who have them should be able to check every claim.

**On quotations and sources.** Where I have used the words of another writer, the source is named in the text. Where an idea is drawn from the common inheritance of the church — Augustine on the two cities, Luther on the external word, the Reformation's insistence that the church is *creatura verbi*, the creature of the word — I have said so in general terms rather than footnoting each echo, because these are shared possessions and to cite them individually would imply a copyright that does not exist. The Bibliography records the works I have most leaned upon.

**On the illustrations and diagrams.** The seventy-one figures in this book are not decoration. Each one states an argument that the surrounding prose could only have made at greater length; several of them are, in effect, the load-bearing summary of their chapters. They are drawn in a single visual language so that a reader who learns to read one can read the rest. Their sources, where they are not the author's own construction, are given beneath each figure.

### An invitation

I have written this book against my own instincts as a son of a generation that kept too much. I have also written it against some of the instincts of my contemporaries who are discovering that the young they elevated are now beyond their influence. Both errors have a single remedy, and the remedy is the one the book describes: the fathers must speak, the children must listen, and both must give themselves to something that neither will live to see finished.

That is what the generations are for.

## Introduction — When Heaven Changes the Hands That Carry the Future

There is a peculiar fact about the opening chapters of the Gospel of Matthew to which readers have grown so accustomed that it no longer startles. Matthew begins his account with a genealogy, and the genealogy contains a decisive break. For fourteen generations the pattern holds, and then it is interrupted by a grammatical event: *now the birth of Jesus Christ was on this wise*. The generations have been counted, and at a particular point in the count the counting stops and something happens that was never going to be contained by counting.

Why does that matter? It matters because the genealogy is itself an argument. Matthew is not simply demonstrating the legality of Jesus' descent. He is establishing that God has been at work *across* the generations — patiently, over centuries, through the ordinary mechanism of fathers and sons — and then that God has, at the appointed moment, done something that no generation could have produced. The genealogy makes the Incarnation look like an answer to a long correspondence.

That is the shape of generational revelation. It is not a doctrine about young people. It is a doctrine about how God accomplishes purpose in time.

### The mechanism heaven prefers

Consider, for a moment, the alternatives available to God.

He could have made every generation self-sufficient. He could have granted to each one of them an immediate, unmediated, overwhelming disclosure of His will, so that no generation ever depended upon its predecessors for the knowledge of God. He could have made the truth self-authenticating in every age, arriving fresh in each person's soul.

He did not do that. He chose a slower and more fragile mechanism, and the choice is deliberate and repeatedly affirmed.

He chose to speak to Abraham, and then to command Abraham to *command his children and his household after him* (Genesis 18:19). He chose to reveal His law at Sinai, and then to instruct fathers — not prophets, not priests, not kings, but fathers — to rehearse it to their sons *when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up* (Deuteronomy 6:7). He chose to give Israel a psalm which is nothing but a commission to transmit, and commands the present generation not to hide from the next what God has done (Psalm 78:4).

To Paul, at the end of the apostolic age, He gave a single sentence that is the entire mechanism in miniature: *the things that thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also* (2 Timothy 2:2). Five persons, one sentence: Paul, the many witnesses, Timothy, the faithful men, and those whom they will teach. The word of God in the hands of men, handed onward, generation after generation, with no terminus in the sentence at all.

Why would God do it this way? The question deserves an answer rather than a shrug.

**First, because the mechanism is a condition of love.** A truth that arrives unmediated arrives without a relationship. A truth that arrives through a father, a mother, an elder, a mentor, arrives wrapped in a person, and cannot easily be separated from that person thereafter. It is possible — and common — to receive a doctrine from a book and to love it. It is not possible to receive a testimony from a dying man and to feel neutral about it.

**Second, because the mechanism makes the future a shared project.** If each generation were self-sufficient, the church would be a series of unconnected events. Because revelation travels, the church becomes a body with a memory and a hope, and the work of one generation becomes the inheritance of another. This is why the New Testament can speak of a man *who planteth* and a man *who watereth* and both rejoicing together (John 4:36), and why Hebrews can describe the saints of the old covenant as a cloud of witnesses surrounding us (Hebrews 12:1). We are not the first to run this race, and we will not be the last.

**Third — and this is the point that will occupy most of this book — because the mechanism creates room for those whom the present age has no use for.** When revelation must be carried across a boundary, the carrier of the next stage is frequently someone whom the current stage would never have selected. God entrusts an unbuilt future to the people who have no share in the built present. This is not sentimentality about youth. It is a structural feature of a design in which purpose outlives persons.

### The hands change

Look at the transitions in Scripture and one notices how often the hands change in a moment, and how rarely the change is comfortable.

Moses led Israel out of Egypt, through the sea, to the edge of the inheritance, and was told at the end that he would not cross. He laid his hands on Joshua, before all the congregation, and *gave him a charge, as the Lord commanded* (Numbers 27:23). One generation saw the land; another entered it.

Eli had worn the priestly office for forty years, and in his last years *the word of the Lord was precious, and there was no open vision* (1 Samuel 3:1). The word came next to a boy lying in the same building, who did not recognise the voice and had to be taught to say *Speak; for thy servant heareth*. Eli had to ask a child what God had said.

Saul was anointed, empowered, elevated, and then abandoned — and the replacement was a shepherd so young that when he was brought before the prophet, nobody in his family thought to mention him (1 Samuel 16:11). The old king kept a throne for years after the new king had been anointed, and in those years the whole machinery of the kingdom turned to destroying the future.

David, who saw the pattern for the temple, who gathered the material, who organised the singers, was forbidden to build, and told that *Solomon thy son, he shall build my house* (1 Chronicles 28:6). The eye saw; the hand had not yet been born.

Elijah called fire down on Carmel and then ran for his life from a woman, and in the desert asked to die, and God answered him not with rest but with a successor — a man with twelve yoke of oxen in a field, who left them and *arose, and went after Elijah* (1 Kings 19:21).

In each case the future is handed to someone who was not the incumbent, and in each case the handover is not a mere administrative event. It is a rupture, and often a bitter one.

### Why rupture is the exception rather than the rule

A mistaken reading of this book would conclude that God's normal pattern is to discard the old for the young, and that this is therefore to be sought, engineered, even hastened.

The scriptural record forbids that conclusion for a simple reason: the ruptures are the exceptions precisely because the mechanism is supposed to work quietly.

Read the psalmists. Psalm 71, written by an old man, asks God not to forsake him *in the time of old age*, and states the reason plainly: *until I have shewed thy strength unto this generation, and thy power to every one that is to come*. The psalmist's prayer is not for a quiet retirement; it is for enough life to finish the handover. An old man praying for the strength to teach is the mechanism working normally.

Read the apostles. Paul's last letter contains no sense that his generation has become obsolete. It contains an agony about whether the deposit will survive the winter. *Hold fast the form of sound words, which thou hast heard of me* (2 Timothy 1:13). *That good thing which was committed unto thee keep by the Holy Ghost* (2 Timothy 1:14). The mechanism is not in crisis because Paul is old. It is in crisis because Paul is about to be dead, and death is the one thing transmission cannot be deferred past.

Read Moses' last speeches. The entire book of Deuteronomy is a man who knows he has forty days to live, telling a nation what it must not forget. It is the single most sustained act of generational instruction in the Bible, and it exists because a man understood that his death was going to be, for the next generation, an information event of catastrophic proportions.

So: the quiet mechanism is the design; rupture is what happens when the quiet mechanism has failed. And the point of this book is to make the quiet mechanism work.

### The two hands and the one who holds both

There is a deeper reason why the succession of generations matters, and it is a reason that belongs to the doctrine of God before it belongs to the doctrine of the church.

When God introduces Himself to Moses at the burning bush, He gives a name with a history in it: *I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob* (Exodus 3:6). This is not merely a formality. It is a description of how God has presented Himself *for centuries* — not as the God of a moment, but as the God of a family line, known across four generations by different men with different characters, each of whom met Him and each of whom died.

Christ turns this statement into an argument for the resurrection. *I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. God is not the God of the dead, but of the living* (Matthew 22:32). If God is still calling Himself *the God of Abraham* centuries after Abraham's death, then Abraham is not dead. The grammar of generational identity proves the reality of life beyond the grave.

This is worth pausing on. The Bible's own proof of resurrection — at least, one of the two proofs Christ Himself offers — is built on a name that spans generations. The doctrine of God is, in this precise respect, a doctrine with a timeline. God identifies Himself by a lineage, and the lineage is not of the dead.

Which means that when we speak of generational revelation, we are not merely describing a pragmatic arrangement for the transmission of information. We are describing something closer to the character of God: a God who binds Himself to families across time, who keeps His word to a thousand generations (Deuteronomy 7:9), and whose faithfulness is *unto all generations* (Psalm 100:5).

And it means that the failure of transmission is not a merely administrative tragedy. If God is the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob — if His self-description is a lineage — then a generation that loses the knowledge of God has not simply lost data. It has to that extent lost its place in the family whose God He is.

### The question of the book

Which brings us to the question in the title.

*Why God left it to the young.* The answer this book will give is not that the old were found wanting, though often they were; nor that the young deserved it, though often they did not; nor that God is partial to freshness, which He is not, since He is the same yesterday, today and forever.

The answer is that God has left the future to the young because a future is precisely the thing the young possess and the old are departing. Purpose in God's economy is by design *unfinished* — unfinished in every generation, and delivered unfinished to the next, so that no generation may claim the whole of what God is doing.

Moses did not enter. David did not build. Elijah did not finish. Paul did not see the full harvest. It is not because they failed. It is because the work is such that only a succession can do it, and the succession is such that only God can hold it.

That is the architecture. This book describes it.
