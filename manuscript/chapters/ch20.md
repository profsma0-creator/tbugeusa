## The Sin of Silent Fathers

There is a sentence in Scripture that describes a father's failure in eight words, and it has no parallel in ancient literature for its bluntness: *and his father had not displeased him at any time in saying, Why hast thou done so?* (1 Kings 1:6).

The father is David. The son is Adonijah. And the failure is not cruelty, neglect, absence, or violence. It is the failure to say a sentence.

This chapter is about that sentence, and about the four categories of speech a father owes his children before he dies.

### The command to speak

The Bible does not present the father's role as primarily economic, protective, or disciplinary. It presents it as **verbal**. And the central text is in Deuteronomy, in the middle of the Shema.

*And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: and thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up* (Deuteronomy 6:6–7).

Examine the verbs, because every one of them is a verb of speech.

**Shall be in thine heart.** The first requirement is interior. The father must himself possess the words before he can transmit them. A man who does not hold the word of God in his own affections cannot hand it to anyone; he can only lend a vocabulary.

**Thou shalt teach them diligently.** The Hebrew verb is *shinnantam*, and it is a form of *shanah*, which means to repeat, to do a second time, to sharpen. The intensive form used here means something like *to repeat again and again*, or, on the reading that some have taken from the cognate Arabic, *to sharpen* — the image of the words being ground into the child's mind the way a blade is ground on a stone.

Either way, the mechanism is repetition, and the repetition is the father's work.

**And shalt talk of them.** The Hebrew is *vedibbarta bam* — and you shall speak of them. Not *read them to them*, not *have them memorised*, not *send them to be instructed*. **Talk.** The verb is the ordinary word for conversation.

**At four moments: sitting in the house, walking by the way, lying down, rising up.** These are not four scheduled lessons. They are the four positions of an ordinary life. Sitting is leisure; walking is travel; lying down is the end of the day; rising is its beginning. Together they cover everything.

**The verse does not command a family Bible hour. It commands a household in which the subject is always available.** And that requires the father to be present, at home, in the leisure, in the travel, at the end of the day, and at the start of it — which is precisely what a silent father is not.

And then the instruction continues into the physical fabric of the house:

*And thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thine hand, and they shall be as frontlets between thine eyes. And thou shalt write them upon the posts of thy house, and on thy gates* (Deuteronomy 6:8–9).

The mezuzah and the tefillin are later institutional forms of these commands, and whatever one makes of them, the underlying instruction is clear: **the word was to be so integrated into the daily environment that it could not be escaped.** On the hand, between the eyes, on the door of the house, on the gate of the city.

That is what an Israelite household was supposed to look like. And that is the standard against which a generation's fathers are judged.

### The New Testament command and its two words

The New Testament states the same duty, and it states it with a precision that repays attention.

*And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord* (Ephesians 6:4).

Two prohibitions and two commands, and the balance of the verse is important.

**Do not provoke.** The Greek is *me parorgizete* — do not exasperate, do not irritate to anger, do not stir into fury. And the Colossian parallel supplies the reason: *Fathers, provoke not your children to anger, lest they be discouraged* (Colossians 3:21). The Greek word translated *discouraged* is *athymosin* — from *thymos*, the spirit or the passion. It means to lose heart, to have the spirit knocked out of one.

**This is the same failure as absence, and the Bible treats them as two sides of one coin.** A father who says nothing leaves a child without direction. A father who criticises without encouragement leaves a child without spirit. Both produce sons and daughters who do not flourish, and the second is frequently mistaken for conscientious fathering.

**And then the two positive words:** *bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord.*

**Nurture** is *paideia*. It denotes the whole process of training a child: instruction, correction, discipline, the formation of habits. It is the word the author of Hebrews uses of God's dealings with His own sons (Hebrews 12:5–8). It is not a soft word; it includes chastening.

**Admonition** is *nouthesia*. It denotes the placing of something in someone's mind by word — counsel, warning, reproof spoken to a person. The word is didactic and verbal.

**Nurture is the formation of the whole person; admonition is specifically the use of the spoken word.** And the command is to bring children up in both.

Which means that a father who provides structure, discipline and provision — the *paideia* — while never putting anything into his children's minds by speech has obeyed half the verse.

### The four silences

And now the anatomy of the failure. A silent father is not silent about everything. He is usually talkative about work, money, sport, politics, the weather, and other people's faults. His silence is not the absence of speech; it is the absence of speech about four specific things.

**The first silence is silence about God.** The father never says what he believes, never speaks of God's dealings with him, never tells his children what he has learned in prayer, never names the providences of his own life. His faith, if it exists, is private to the point of invisibility inside his own house.

And this is the silence that Abraham's example is set against. God's own testimony about Abraham is a statement about speech:

*For I know him, that he will command his children and his household after him, and they shall keep the way of the Lord, to do justice and judgment; that the Lord may bring upon Abraham that which he hath spoken of him* (Genesis 18:19).

The Hebrew verb is *yetsavveh* — he will command, he will give orders to, he will charge. God's confidence in Abraham is expressed in terms of what Abraham will *say* to his household. And the consequence clause is staggering: *that the Lord may bring upon Abraham that which he hath spoken of him.* **The fulfilment of the promises to Abraham is attached in this verse to whether Abraham instructs his children.**

**The second silence is silence about sin.** The father does not name what is wrong in his children's conduct, or in his own. He lets the character of his household drift, on the reasoning that they will grow out of it, or that the matter is small, or that a confrontation would damage the relationship.

*And his father had not displeased him at any time in saying, Why hast thou done so?* That is the mechanism precisely. Adonijah's conduct was never named. Every small thing was passed over. And the accumulation of unnamed things produced a man who believed he was fit to be king without being asked.

**The third silence is silence about himself.** The father does not tell his children who he is: what he has failed at, what he fears, what he hoped for and did not get, what he believes about them, what he wants for them. He appears in the household as a function rather than a person — the provider, the disciplinarian, the one who is tired.

And the cost is that his children inherit a man they never knew, and are left to construct a version of him from the parts they observed.

**The fourth silence is silence about the future.** The father does not say what he sees coming, or what he intends to leave, or what he expects of them after he is gone. There is no charge, no blessing, no will, no letter, no plan.

And the biblical pattern is entirely the opposite: Jacob charges his sons before he dies (Genesis 49). Moses writes the law and deposits it with the priests and gives a song (Deuteronomy 31). Joshua assembles the tribes and demands a decision (Joshua 24). Samuel gives an account of his stewardship (1 Samuel 12). David hands over the plans of the temple and the treasury and the unfinished business (1 Chronicles 28–29; 1 Kings 2). Peter writes two letters so the church will have his words after his death.

**Every one of these men spoke before he died.** And the sons and daughters of the men who did not have spent their lives reconstructing what was never said.

### The Eli pattern

And now we come to the mechanism by which a father's silence destroys a household, and the clearest case in Scripture is not David but Eli.

*Now the sons of Eli were sons of Belial; they knew not the Lord* (1 Samuel 2:12).

The Hebrew *beliyya'al* means worthlessness, and it is used of people of no account, unredeemable scoundrels. And the second clause is the diagnosis: *they knew not the Lord.* Not *they did not serve the Lord*, or *they disobeyed the Lord*. They did not know Him.

They were priests. They had grown up in the tabernacle. They had access to everything. And they did not know God.

And the father's record is not total silence. Eli did speak:

*And he said unto them, Why do ye such things? for I hear of your evil dealings by all this people. Nay, my sons; for it is no good report that I hear: ye make the Lord's people to transgress. If one man sin against another, the judge shall judge him: but if a man sin against the Lord, who shall intreat for him? Notwithstanding they hearkened not unto the voice of their father, because the Lord would slay them* (1 Samuel 2:23–25).

**Eli rebuked his sons.** The rebuke is theologically accurate — the line about sinning against God and having no one to intreat for him is one of the finest statements of the problem in the Old Testament. **And it had no effect, because it was a rebuke and not a restraint.**

And the divine diagnosis, delivered through the man of God who came to Eli, is unsparing:

*Wherefore kick ye at my sacrifice and at mine offering, which I have commanded in my habitation; and honourest thy sons above me, to make yourselves fat with the chiefest of all the offerings of Israel my people?* (1 Samuel 2:29).

*And honourest thy sons above me.* The charge is not that Eli did not speak. The charge is that when the moment arrived, Eli's sons mattered more to him than God did.

**And the sentence, pronounced twice and executed once, is the most terrible in the historical books.**

*And this shall be a sign unto thee, that shall come upon thy two sons, on Hophni and Phinehas; in one day they shall die both of them* (1 Samuel 2:34).

*And I will raise me up a faithful priest, that shall do according to that which is in mine heart and in my mind: and I will build him a sure house; and he shall walk before mine anointed for ever* (1 Samuel 2:35).

**The house of Eli ended and the house of Zadok began.** And the words *them that honour me I will honour, and they that despise me shall be lightly esteemed* (1 Samuel 2:30) are the law of the entire transaction.

*Then came the fulfilment.* Hophni and Phinehas went into battle carrying the ark, and both were killed in one day and the ark was captured (1 Samuel 4:11). Eli, aged ninety-eight, seated by the gate, was told, and:

*And it came to pass, when he made mention of the ark of God, that he fell from off the seat backward by the side of the gate, and his neck brake, and he died: for he was an old man, and heavy. And he had judged Israel forty years* (1 Samuel 4:18).

And the child born that day was named *Ichabod*, and the name means *no glory*.

*And she named the child I-chabod, saying, The glory is departed from Israel: because the ark of God was taken, and because of her father in law and her husband* (1 Samuel 4:21).

**And the psalm that reflects on the whole disaster states it as a judgment on the place and the priesthood, and the reason is traceable to the silence of one man.**

*So that he forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh, the tent which he placed among men; and delivered his strength into captivity, and his glory into the enemy's hand* (Psalm 78:60–61).

### The distinction that matters

But we must be careful here, because the failure of a father does not produce a simple outcome, and the case of Eli proves it.

**Eli's silence destroyed Eli's line and did not destroy God's purposes.** The same tabernacle was presided over by the same failed priest in 1 Samuel 3 when the word of the Lord came to a child who was not his son. And the prophet who replaced the failed house came out of that house.

**God raised up a successor inside the institution that the failure had corrupted.** The lamp in the tabernacle was still lit (1 Samuel 3:3). The ark was still there. And the boy who learned to answer the voice was trained in the household of the man who could no longer hear it.

**That is a mercy to the children, and it is not a mercy to the father.** The sons who fail to restrain their own children may still be used to raise someone else's. And the record is honest about both things.

### Five causes of father-silence

And now, because the sin is easier to condemn than to avoid, we must account for why fathers go silent. There are five causes, and they are cumulative.

**The first is absence.** A father who is not in the room cannot speak in the room. And in a work culture that is measured by hours, the ordinary father of an ordinary household has less than an hour a day with his children in their waking hours, much of it spent on logistics. **The four positions of Deuteronomy 6:7 — sitting, walking, lying down, rising up — are simply not available to a man who is not there for any of them.**

**The second is exhaustion.** Speech about important things requires energy. A man who has spent everything he has on his work arrives home with nothing left but the capacity to be present in body. And so the conversations that matter never happen, not because he decided against them, but because he had nothing left at the hour they would have occurred.

**The third is the fear of conflict.** Every one of the four silences is a decision to avoid an uncomfortable conversation. And the fear is rational: naming a child's sin produces a reaction, and the reaction is painful. **The father's problem is that he is weighing two costs and pricing the second one at zero.** The cost of speaking is a bad evening. The cost of silence is a son who thinks he is fit to be king.

**The fourth is misplaced grace.** *I do not want to be the kind of father my father was. I do not want to impose. I want them to be free.* There is a genuine virtue in this and it is frequently a mask. The father who will not say the hard thing because he does not want to be harsh is often repeating his own father's failure with better manners.

**And the fifth, which is the deepest: a man who was never fathered does not know how to father.** This is where the wounds of one generation produce the wounds of the next, and it is the reason this whole book exists. The father who was never told *I see this in you* does not know to say it. The father whose own father never sat him down and named his faults does not know the format. He is not withholding; he is unequipped.

And the Scripture that speaks to this is the last verse of the Old Testament:

*And he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children to their fathers, lest I come and smite the earth with a curse* (Malachi 4:6).

**The turning is the remedy, and it is a turning of *heart*, which is to say of disposition, before it is a turning of behaviour.** The curse at the end of the Old Testament is a curse on households in which the generations have stopped facing each other. And the remedy is not a programme; it is a father who turns round.

### What the New Testament requires of an elder

And this brings the matter into the church, because the New Testament makes a father's speech a qualification for leadership.

*One that ruleth well his own house, having his children in subjection with all gravity; (for if a man know not how to rule his own house, how shall he take care of the church of God?)* (1 Timothy 3:4–5).

The Greek verb for *rule* is *proistemi* — to stand before, to preside over, to lead. And the test is a comparison: **the household is a smaller version of the assembly, and a man who cannot lead the one cannot lead the other.**

And in the same letter Paul states the requirement again for elders: *if any be blameless, the husband of one wife, having faithful children not accused of riot or unruly* (Titus 1:6).

**Note what this means, and it is uncomfortable.** A man whose children are out of control is not, on the New Testament's own terms, qualified to lead a church — not because he is a bad man, and not because the children's conduct is entirely his fault, but because the same skill is required in both settings and its absence is observable.

**The household is where a man's capacity to speak truth to people who are subject to him is tested in the least dangerous conditions.** A father who cannot say *Why hast thou done so?* to a son he feeds will not say it to an elder he does not.

### The remedy

And now the constructive part. What does a silent father do?

**He begins by writing.** This is the single most useful practical recommendation in this chapter, and it is drawn directly from Scripture. Peter, knowing his death was near, wrote (2 Peter 1:12–15). David prepared the temple plans *in writing* (1 Chronicles 28:19). **A man who cannot speak can write, and a written word does not require the same courage in the moment, does not get interrupted, and survives.**

**He says the four things, one at a time.** Not a comprehensive confrontation, which will not happen. Four conversations across a year: what he believes about God; what he sees that concerns him; what he thinks of them; what he expects after he is gone.

**He asks the question David never asked.** *Why hast thou done so?* The question is not an accusation. It is an invitation to explain, and the explanation is often the whole point.

**He accepts that the reaction will be unpleasant and does it anyway.** *He that spareth his rod hateth his son: but he that loveth him chasteneth him betimes* (Proverbs 13:24). The proverb is about the rod, and the principle extends to every form of costly correction. *Betimes* is the older English for *early* — the correction is to be given at the beginning, before the pattern is set.

**And he gives the blessing.** This is the element most often omitted, and it is the one a child remembers. Jacob blessed his sons. Moses blessed the tribes. David blessed Solomon publicly. **A father who has corrected his child and never once told him what he sees in him has left him with an accusation and no benediction.**

### The weight of a father's word

We must pause on a feature of the biblical world that modern readers consistently underestimate, because it explains why the silence of a father is treated in Scripture as a catastrophe rather than a disappointment.

**In the patriarchal narratives, a father's spoken word had the force of a legal act and a prophetic utterance at the same time.** A blessing pronounced was not an expression of goodwill; it was a transfer. A curse pronounced was not an insult; it was a sentence.

The clearest instance is Genesis 27, and it is worth reading closely because it is entirely about the mechanics of what a father's word does.

Isaac, old and blind, asks Esau to hunt and prepare a meal so that *my soul may bless thee before I die* (27:4). Rebekah overhears, and Jacob deceives his father and receives the blessing. Esau returns and discovers what has happened, and his cry is one of the most terrible in Scripture:

*And he said, Is not he rightly named Jacob? for he hath supplanted me these two times: he took away my birthright; and, behold, now he hath taken away my blessing. And he said, Hast thou not reserved a blessing for me?* (Genesis 27:36).

And Isaac's answer establishes the principle that the whole chapter runs on:

*And Isaac answered and said unto Esau, Behold, I have made him thy lord, and all his brethren have I given to him for servants; and with corn and wine have I sustained him: and what shall I do now unto thee, my son?* (Genesis 27:37).

*I have made him thy lord.* And when Esau weeps and pleads, Isaac cannot reverse it. *And Isaac his father answered and said unto him, Behold, thy dwelling shall be the fatness of the earth, and of the dew of heaven from above* (27:39) — a blessing given, but a lesser one, because **the word had already gone out and could not be recalled.**

Notice what this means. In the world of the patriarchs:
- **A father's word was effective.** It did something.
- **It was once-for-all.** It could not be taken back.
- **And it was given at the end, deliberately, in a formal act.**

Which is why the father's silence is not a matter of a missed opportunity for encouragement. **In the biblical frame, an unblessed son has not merely been left without a compliment. He has been left without a portion.**

And Genesis 48 and 49 show the same thing operating twice more. Jacob, blind and dying, crosses his hands deliberately so that the younger receives the greater blessing — and when Joseph protests, he refuses to be corrected (48:19). Then he gathers all twelve sons and pronounces over each of them *that which shall befall you in the last days* (49:1). The blessing and the prediction are indistinguishable.

**The father is the man who names what his children will be.** And the man who does not name it leaves his children to name themselves, which is precisely what Adonijah did — *then Adonijah the son of Haggith exalted himself, saying, I will be king* (1 Kings 1:5).

### The father in the parable

And the New Testament gives us the Lord's own portrait of a father, and it is instructive that the parable of the prodigal son is not primarily about the son.

*And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him* (Luke 15:20).

*But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him.* The father was watching the road. That is the first thing the parable tells us about him, and it implies a habit of expectation that had been maintained every day of the son's absence.

*And ran.* In a culture where a senior man did not run in public, the verb is deliberately shocking. The father abandons his dignity to reach the son before the son reaches the village.

**And then — and this is the part that answers the whole of this chapter — the father speaks.**

*But the father said to his servants, Bring forth the best robe, and put it on him; and put a ring on his hand, and shoes on his feet: and bring hither the fatted calf, and kill it; and let us eat, and be merry: for this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found* (Luke 15:22–24).

**Six commands, all of them spoken, all of them given in public, in front of servants and the village.** The robe, the ring, the shoes, the calf, the feast, and the reason. Each one is a sentence that restores the son's standing, and the son did nothing to earn a single one of them.

And then note the second half of the parable, which is addressed to the son who never left.

*Now his elder son was in the field: and as he came and drew nigh to the house, he heard musick and dancing ... And he was angry, and would not go in: therefore came his father out, and intreated him* (Luke 15:25, 28).

**The father goes out a second time, to the son who has been obedient all along, and intreats him.** And the elder son's complaint is the complaint of a man who has never been told what he is worth:

*Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment: and yet thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends* (Luke 15:29).

*Thou never gavest me a kid.* The son had satisfied every requirement and had never been given a public blessing. And the father's answer is the remedy:

*And he said unto him, Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine* (Luke 15:31).

**One sentence, spoken to the son who had never needed rescue, stating what he possessed and who he was.** The older son had moved from a position of tremendous standing to a position of grievance, and the whole of the difference was a statement that had never been said to him.

Both sons in the parable are unblessed at the start of the passage. The younger is unblessed because he has forfeited his place; the elder is unblessed because his father never told him what he had.

### A note on discipline

And because this chapter has spoken at length about restraint, it must not be read as a licence for harshness. The Proverbs on the rod are real, and the same book also warns against the opposite failure:

*Fathers, provoke not your children to anger, lest they be discouraged* (Colossians 3:21).

*And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath* (Ephesians 6:4).

The two commands stand side by side in the same letters that command the discipline, and they are not in tension. **The rule is this: correction is aimed at the conduct, and the child is never the object of the anger.** A father who disciplines out of his own frustration produces a discouraged child no matter what technique he uses.

And the model is stated plainly in Hebrews, where the human father's discipline is used as an analogy for God's:

*Furthermore we have had fathers of our flesh which corrected us, and we gave them reverence: shall we not much rather be in subjection unto the Father of spirits, and live? For they verily for a few days chastened us after their own pleasure; but he for our profit, that we might be partakers of his holiness* (Hebrews 12:9–10).

*After their own pleasure* — the Greek phrase acknowledges that human fathers get this wrong. *But he for our profit.* **The standard is profit to the child, and any discipline that is not for the child's profit is not the thing the Bible is commanding.**

### For the fathers

- **Write down the four silences and grade yourself.** What have you never said to your children about God, about their sin, about yourself, and about the future? Put a number beside each from one to ten.
- **Name the sentence you have never said.** For David it was *Why hast thou done so?* For another man it is *I love you*, or *I was wrong about that*, or *I am proud of you*. Find the sentence and say it this month.
- **Do not confuse rebuke with restraint.** Eli rebuked his sons and did not restrain them, and the judgment fell on him for what he did not do. Reproof without consequence is a way of appearing to act.
- **Write the letter now.** Write to each child what you believe about God, what you have learned from your failures, what you see in them, and what you want after you are gone. Give it to them while you can still be asked questions about it.
- **Ask the question.** *Why did you do that?* Ask it about something real, and then be silent and listen to the answer without preparing your response.
- **Bless them out loud.** Correct them and then tell them what you see. Correction without benediction is the most common way a father produces a discouraged child.

### For the young

- **Do not read your father's silence as his verdict on you.** A man who says nothing about your life is usually a man who does not know how, and the deficiency is his father's and his father's before him. Inherit the correction without inheriting the curse.
- **Ask him the direct question.** Most silent fathers will answer a direct question. *What do you think I should do with my life? What have you seen in me? What do you regret?* — these produce more than twenty years of conversation.
- **Write down what you receive.** The word from an older man is a deposit, and deposits are lost by forgetfulness. Keep a page in a book and add to it.
- **Break the pattern deliberately.** If you are a father, the silence you received is not a sentence on what you will pass on. It is the thing you are now required to do differently, and the first time you say it, it will feel unnatural and will probably come out badly.
- **And tell your father what he gave you.** The silent father frequently believes he gave nothing. Naming what he did give is often the unlocking of the whole relationship.

### Questions for reflection

1. Who in your lineage broke a pattern of silence, and what did it cost them to break it?
2. What is the sentence that has never been said to you, and by whom? Are you still waiting for it, and is there another way to receive what it contains?
3. Which of the four silences is yours — silence about God, about sin, about yourself, or about the future?
4. Eli rebuked and did not restrain. Where in your household have you substituted words for consequences?
5. If your children were asked what you have told them about God, what would they say you said?
