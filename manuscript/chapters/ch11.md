## Elisha and the Mantle of Succession

The phrase *passing the mantle* has entered the common vocabulary of institutions, largely by way of this narrative, and mostly with a meaning that the narrative does not support. In common usage it means the transfer of authority from a retiring leader to a designated successor, and it carries a note of ceremony and completion.

The biblical account is stranger, longer, and considerably less comfortable. It is a story about a man who threw his coat over a ploughman and walked away without a word. It is a story in which the *succession* takes roughly ten years, during which the successor is mostly a servant. It is a story in which the decisive moment is not the transfer of the mantle but a request made on a riverbank in the last hours of the older man's life. And it ends with a group of prophets waiting at a distance to see whether anything had actually happened.

It is, in other words, a far better guide to what succession involves than the proverb is.

### The call without explanation

We must begin where Scripture begins, which is immediately after the crisis of 1 Kings 19.

Elijah has just come from the greatest triumph of his ministry — the confrontation on Carmel, the fire from heaven, the execution of the prophets of Baal, and the breaking of a three-and-a-half-year drought. And within twenty-four hours he is running for his life from a woman's threat, has gone a day's journey into the wilderness, has sat down under a juniper tree, and has asked God to take his life: *It is enough; now, O Lord, take away my life; for I am not better than my fathers* (1 Kings 19:4).

It is one of the most human passages in Scripture, and it is worth noting that the man who had called down fire is not rebuked for his despair. God feeds him twice with angel's food, lets him sleep, allows him a forty-day journey to Horeb — the mountain where Moses had met God — and then addresses him in a still small voice.

And then, at the mountain, God gives him three commissions, of which two are about the destruction of dynasties and the third is about the future of the prophetic office.

*And the Lord said unto him, Go, return on thy way to the wilderness of Damascus: and when thou comest, anoint Hazael to be king over Syria: and Jehu the son of Nimshi shalt thou anoint to be king over Israel: and Elisha the son of Shaphat of Abel-meholah shalt thou anoint to be prophet in thy room* (1 Kings 19:15–16).

Three anointings. One for a foreign king who will be an instrument of judgment. One for a domestic king who will end the house of Ahab. And one for a prophet to take Elijah's place.

And then God adds a sentence that rebukes Elijah's despair directly: *Yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel, all the knees which have not bowed unto Baal, and every mouth which hath not kissed him* (1 Kings 19:18).

Elijah had said, *I, even I only, am left* (19:10). God says: seven thousand, and by the way, here is your successor.

### The man in the field

And now the call, which is one of the briefest and most peculiar vocation accounts in Scripture.

*So he departed thence, and found Elisha the son of Shaphat, who was plowing with twelve yoke of oxen before him, and he with the twelfth: and Elijah passed by him, and cast his mantle upon him* (1 Kings 19:19).

Consider the details.

**Twelve yoke of oxen.** A yoke is a pair, so this is twenty-four animals, worked in sequence, and Elisha is driving the last pair himself. This is a substantial farming operation. Elisha is not a poor man with nothing to lose; he is the son of a well-to-do household, engaged in the leadership of a significant agricultural enterprise. The land is his family's livelihood, and he is the one who has to keep the team moving.

**Elijah passed by.** He does not stop. He does not introduce himself. There is no conversation, no explanation, no invitation. He performs a single symbolic act — the casting of his mantle, the garment of a prophet — and continues on his way.

**And he cast his mantle upon him.** This is the whole call. A garment, dropped on a ploughman, in a field, without a word.

The symbolism is not explained in the text, but it is not obscure either. A mantle was the distinctive outer garment of a prophet — Elijah's own mantle will later be used to divide the Jordan (2 Kings 2:8), and it is by his mantle that King Ahaziah identifies him at a distance: *he was an hairy man, and girt with a girdle of leather about his loins* (2 Kings 1:8). To cast the mantle on a man is to claim him, to cover him, and to mark him as belonging to the prophetic calling.

And Elisha's response is immediate and total.

*And he left the oxen, and ran after Elijah, and said, Let me, I pray thee, kiss my father and my mother, and then I will follow thee. And he said unto him, Go back again: for what have I done to thee?* (1 Kings 19:20).

The exchange is subtle. Elisha asks permission to say farewell — a request that Christ will later refuse to one who made it in the Gospels: *No man, having put his hand to the plough, and looking back, is fit for the kingdom of God* (Luke 9:62). Elijah's answer is enigmatic — *Go back again: for what have I done to thee?* — and can be read either as dismissal or as a test. What Elisha does is decisive: he goes back, but not to resume his life.

*And he returned back from him, and took a yoke of oxen, and slew them, and boiled their flesh with the instruments of the oxen, and gave unto the people, and they did eat. Then he arose, and went after Elijah, and ministered unto him* (1 Kings 19:21).

He slaughters the team. He burns the plough for fuel. He feeds the whole community with the proceeds, and then he leaves.

This is not a resignation; it is a *point of no return*. Elisha has destroyed the asset that would have allowed him to return. In modern terms, he sold the family business, distributed the capital, and took up an unpaid apprenticeship. And the last four words of the verse are the most important in the passage: *and ministered unto him.*

### The years of service

*And ministered unto him.* This is the period the proverb of the mantle conceals, and it is roughly a decade long.

We do not have a chronicle of those years. What we have are glimpses, and they are uniformly the glimpses of a servant. Elisha is present when Elijah confronts Ahaziah's messengers (2 Kings 1). Elisha is present at the prophetic gatherings at Gilgal, Bethel and Jericho (2 Kings 2:1–6). Elisha is the one who, when King Jehoshaphat asks whether there is a prophet of the Lord present, is identified by another man's servant: *here is Elisha the son of Shaphat, which poured water on the hands of Elijah* (2 Kings 3:11).

*Which poured water on the hands of Elijah.* That is the identification. Not *the prophet Elisha*, or *the man of God*, or *the one who sees*. *The one who poured water on the hands of Elijah.*

The phrase describes the ordinary domestic service of a household servant in a country without running water: bringing the jug and pouring it over the master's hands before a meal. It is the language of an attendant, not an heir. And it is how Elisha is known — years later, by a third party, in a national emergency.

This decade of service is the whole reason this chapter belongs in a book about generational revelation, and it makes two points that are widely ignored.

**The first point is that the mantle is not the succession.** Elisha received the mantle in 1 Kings 19:19. He became the prophet in his own right in 2 Kings 2. Between those two events are years of pouring water on another man's hands. The symbolic act was instantaneous; the office was not.

**The second point is that the years of service are the curriculum.** Elijah did not run a school. He did not give Elisha a body of instruction, so far as the record shows. What he gave him was his presence, over a long period, in a wide range of circumstances — a drought, a confrontation with a king, a walk to Bethel, a walk to Jericho, a crossing of the Jordan. Elisha's training was *proximity*, and proximity takes time.

There is no shortcut to this, and the modern church is perpetually attempting to invent one. We place young leaders into responsibility at the age when Elisha was still pouring water, and then wonder why they lack the character and the instinct that proximity produces.

### The last walk

The opening of 2 Kings 2 is one of the most beautifully constructed passages of narrative in the Old Testament, and its construction is the argument.

*And it came to pass, when the Lord would take up Elijah into heaven by a whirlwind, that Elijah went with Elisha from Gilgal. And Elijah said unto Elisha, Tarry here, I pray thee; for the Lord hath sent me to Bethel. And Elisha said unto him, As the Lord liveth, and as thy soul liveth, I will not leave thee. So they went down to Bethel* (2 Kings 2:1–2).

And then the pattern repeats. At Bethel the sons of the prophets come out to Elisha with the same question — *Knowest thou that the Lord will take away thy master from thy head to day?* — and Elisha answers, with the same words, *Yea, I know it; hold ye your peace* (2:3). And Elijah proposes to leave him again at Jericho, and again Elisha refuses.

Three times. And then a fourth, at the Jordan.

And at each station, the sons of the prophets are waiting, and at each station they raise the alarm. Fifty men follow them at a distance, watching (2:7).

The narrative is doing several things at once, and one of them is very important for our subject. **The schools of the prophets knew what was coming, and did nothing but watch.** They had received knowledge of the event. They had the institutional intelligence. They had the manpower. And their function in the story is to stand at a distance and observe.

Only one man walks with Elijah to the end, and he is the man who refuses to be sent back.

### The request

And now the decisive moment.

*And it came to pass, when they were gone over, that Elijah said unto Elisha, Ask what I shall do for thee, before I be taken away from thee. And Elisha said, I pray thee, let a double portion of thy spirit be upon me* (2 Kings 2:9).

We have already noted that the phrase translated *double portion* is the legal term for the firstborn son's inheritance — in a family of two sons, the eldest took two shares, and the wording in Deuteronomy 21:17 is *a double portion of all that he hath*. Elisha is not asking for twice Elijah's power. He is asking to be recognised as the **heir**.

And note what this tells us about the nature of spiritual succession. Elisha does not ask for Elijah's authority, or his role, or his reputation, or his standing among the prophetic guilds. He asks for the *inheritance of the firstborn* — the substance, the legacy, that which a father transmits to the son who continues the family.

And Elijah's answer defines the condition:

*And he said, Thou hast asked a hard thing: nevertheless, if thou see me when I am taken from thee, it shall be so unto thee; but if not, it shall not be so* (2 Kings 2:10).

*If thou see me.* The condition of the inheritance is *presence at the departure*. Not seniority, not merit, not examination, not appointment. Presence.

This is a remarkable criterion for the transfer of a spiritual office, and it is the most important sentence in the chapter. The inheritance is given to the one who stays until the end.

There is a hard saying embedded in that condition, and it should be said plainly: **most successions fail because the successor leaves early.** He leaves because the master is difficult, or the waiting is long, or another opportunity is more attractive, or the senior man's methods are antiquated, or he wants to begin his own ministry. And the inheritance is not given to him, because the inheritance is given at the departure, and he was not there.

Elisha's four refusals — at Gilgal, at Bethel, at Jericho, and at the Jordan — are the reason the double portion is his.

### The departure and the mantle again

*And it came to pass, as they still went on, and talked, that, behold, there appeared a chariot of fire, and horses of fire, and parted them both asunder; and Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven. And Elisha saw it, and he cried, My father, my father, the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof. And he saw him no more: and he took hold of his own clothes, and rent them in two pieces* (2 Kings 2:11–12).

*He saw it.* The condition is met.

And then the act that makes the story complete:

*He took up also the mantle of Elijah that fell from him, and went back, and stood by the bank of Jordan* (2 Kings 2:13).

The mantle which Elijah had thrown on him in a field at the beginning is now *his*, because it fell from the departing prophet, and he picks it up.

And now the crucial moment — the moment that the sons of the prophets are watching from their vantage point, and the reason fifty of them followed at a distance.

*And he took the mantle of Elijah that fell from him, and smote the waters, and said, Where is the Lord God of Elijah? and when he had smitten the waters, they parted hither and thither: and Elisha went over* (2 Kings 2:14).

Consider what Elisha does. He does not merely possess the mantle; he *uses* it. And his words are a question, not a claim: *Where is the Lord God of Elijah?*

He is asking whether the God of his master will be his God. And the river divides, and the answer is yes.

And the sons of the prophets, watching from Jericho, draw the correct conclusion: *The spirit of Elijah doth rest on Elisha. And they came to meet him, and bowed themselves to the ground before him* (2 Kings 2:15).

### What the chapter establishes

Let us gather the principles, because this narrative is the fullest account in Scripture of how a spiritual office passes.

**First: the call may be sudden, but the succession is gradual.** The mantle came in an afternoon; the office came after years. Any institution that confuses a commissioning service with the formation of a successor has misread this text.

**Second: the curriculum is proximity, and proximity cannot be short-cut.** Elisha learned by being with Elijah — in the drought, at the confrontation, on the road, at the table. There is no way to compress this, and the attempt to do so is one of the chronic errors of modern ministry.

**Third: the inheritance is granted at the departure, and therefore requires endurance.** *If thou see me when I am taken from thee.* The successor must be present at the end, and the end is often the least rewarding part of the relationship.

**Fourth: the mantle must be picked up and used.** Elisha receives the garment and immediately smites the waters with it. A successor who inherits an office and does not exercise it has not begun his ministry; he has merely accepted a title.

**Fifth: the inheritance must be proved by the successor's own experience of God.** *Where is the Lord God of Elijah?* This is the question every successor must ask, and the answer cannot be borrowed. The God of the fathers must become *my* God.

**Sixth, and most soberly: the office can be inherited by the wrong person.** The sons of the prophets were at Gilgal, Bethel, Jericho and the Jordan. They knew about the departure in advance. Some of them presumably had greater seniority than Elisha, and better connections, and more years in the schools. And they stood at a distance and watched, and one man who refused to leave received the double portion.

The road to the inheritance is walked by very few, because it runs through the least attractive part of the countryside — the part where you pour water on another man's hands and no one thanks you for it.

And the promise attached to that road is astonishing: *Where is the Lord God of Elijah?* — asked at a riverbank by a man who had been a ploughman a decade earlier, and answered by the parting of the waters.
