## Appendix A. Critical appraisal template

Use this template for a single study. Record the source, then answer each prompt in a short paragraph. A completed template is a record of reasoning, not a score.

| Section | Prompts to answer |
|---|---|
| Citation and access | Full reference; did you read the full text, an abstract, or a summary? |
| Question | What question does the study ask? Is it a question about prevalence, mechanism, efficacy, experience or implementation? |
| Design | What design was used? Is it capable of answering the question? |
| Sample | Who was studied, how were they selected, and who was excluded? |
| Measures | What constructs were measured, with which instruments, and what are their reliability and validity? |
| Comparison | What is the comparison group or condition, and is it appropriate? |
| Analysis | Are the analyses prespecified and appropriate? Is multiple testing handled? |
| Effect and precision | What is the effect size, with what confidence interval? Is it clinically meaningful? |
| Bias | What selection, measurement, attrition and reporting biases are plausible? |
| Confounding | Which alternative explanations remain? |
| Applicability | To whom, in what setting, does this result plausibly apply? |
| Conclusion | What does the study support, what does it not support, and what would reduce uncertainty? |

## Appendix D. Statistical and methodological terms

**Bias.** A systematic error that distorts an estimate. Bias differs from random error, which is reduced by larger samples.

**Confidence interval.** A range of values consistent with the observed data at a stated level of confidence. It conveys precision and is more informative than a significance test alone.

**Confounding.** A third variable associated with both exposure and outcome that distorts their apparent relationship.

**Effect size.** The magnitude of a difference or association, expressed in standardised or natural units.

**Heterogeneity.** Variation in effect estimates across studies or subgroups beyond what sampling error would predict.

**Intention-to-treat.** Analysis of participants according to the group to which they were allocated, regardless of the treatment received.

**Power.** The probability of detecting an effect of a given size if it exists. Underpowered studies produce unstable estimates.

**Preregistration.** Public registration of hypotheses, outcomes and analyses before data collection or analysis.

**Sensitivity analysis.** Reanalysis under different assumptions to test whether conclusions are robust.

## Laboratory and classroom teaching protocols

These protocols describe teaching activities only. They do not authorise clinical investigation, and they do not produce diagnostic information. Each requires institutional approval, supervision by a qualified teacher, informed consent from participants, and a risk assessment. Where a protocol involves biological samples, local biosafety, storage and disposal rules apply, and the assay manufacturer's instructions govern the procedure.

### Protocol 1. Salivary cortisol sampling (educational)

**Aim.** To demonstrate diurnal variation and the practical constraints of endocrine measurement.

**Materials.** Salivary collection devices approved for human use; labels; timed reminders; cold storage; assay arranged through an approved laboratory.

**Procedure.** Students review the sampling schedule specified by the assay and the study question; record sleep, food, caffeine, nicotine, medication and exercise; collect samples at the approved times; store and transport them according to the laboratory's instructions.

**Analysis.** Plot individual values against time. Discuss why a single sample cannot characterise a person's stress physiology.

**Limits.** Values depend on time of day, posture, recent activity, oral health and assay method. Results are not interpreted clinically.

### Protocol 2. Heart rate variability demonstration

**Aim.** To show how autonomic regulation can be indexed indirectly.

**Materials.** A validated recording device; electrodes or photoplethysmography sensor; analysis software with documented artefact handling.

**Procedure.** Record resting and paced-breathing conditions; inspect the trace for artefacts; compute time-domain and frequency-domain measures with the software's stated settings.

**Analysis.** Compare conditions within participants and discuss the influence of posture, breathing rate, fitness and medication.

**Limits.** Consumer devices vary in validity; short recordings are unstable; group averages do not describe individuals.

### Protocol 3. Conditioning and extinction demonstration

**Aim.** To illustrate acquisition, extinction and renewal using mild stimuli only.

**Materials.** Computer task with neutral images and brief, non-painful sounds; structured instructions; debriefing.

**Procedure.** Participants learn that one image predicts a sound; the sound is then omitted; the image is presented in a new context. Subjective expectancy ratings and simple reaction times are recorded.

**Analysis.** Plot expectancy across phases and discuss renewal.

**Limits.** Mild laboratory conditioning differs from traumatic learning. No aversive stimulation is used, and participation is voluntary and withdrawable.

### Protocol 4. Neuroanatomy practical

**Aim.** To identify the structures discussed in Chapters 2–4.

**Materials.** Anatomical models, plastinated sections or approved digital atlases.

**Procedure.** Students locate the hippocampus, amygdala, insula, anterior cingulate, hypothalamus, brainstem nuclei and ventricular system; they trace the course of the HPA axis and the main noradrenergic projection.

**Analysis.** Short structured identification exercise with a marking guide.

**Limits.** Models simplify anatomy; imaging appearances differ from specimens.

## Professional standards and scope of practice

This textbook supports doctoral study of trauma neurobiology. It does not confer clinical competence, prescribing authority, imaging privileges, laboratory certification or legal authority to assess trauma in forensic settings.

Students and practitioners should work within their competence and their jurisdiction's law; seek supervision when a case exceeds that competence; obtain informed consent and explain the limits of confidentiality; protect identifiable data; recognise and declare conflicts of interest; and refer for medical assessment when symptoms, history or examination suggest a condition requiring it.

Research with trauma-affected people requires ethical review, valid consent, attention to power and dependency, referral pathways for distress, and arrangements for sharing findings with participants and communities. Authorship should reflect substantive contribution. Findings should be reported honestly, including those that are negative or inconvenient.

Where this book and local law, professional regulation or institutional policy differ, the latter takes precedence.

## Further reading

The following works are cited in the chapters and are useful starting points. Annotations indicate scope, not endorsement.

- **Shonkoff and Garner (2012)** — the American Academy of Pediatrics technical report on childhood adversity and toxic stress; a concise framing of developmental and policy implications.
- **Teicher and Samson (2016)** — a review of the neurobiological correlates of childhood maltreatment, with attention to sensitive periods and methodological limits.
- **McEwen (1998)** — the classic account of allostasis and allostatic load, still the clearest short statement of the framework.
- **Ulrich-Lai and Herman (2009)** — neural regulation of endocrine and autonomic stress responses.
- **Herman et al. (2016)** — detailed physiology of the HPA axis, including feedback and circadian control.
- **LeDoux (2000)** and **Maren (2001)** — complementary accounts of fear circuitry and Pavlovian conditioning.
- **Milad and Quirk (2012)** — extinction as a translational model, with a clear account of relapse phenomena.
- **Nader et al. (2000)** — the reconsolidation experiment that reframed memory stability.
- **Vervliet et al. (2013)** — a critical review of extinction and relapse; useful for judging clinical claims.
- **Craske et al. (2014)** — inhibitory learning and the practical optimisation of exposure.
- **Ehlers and Clark (2000)** — the cognitive model that organises much trauma-focused therapy.
- **Lanius et al. (2010)** — the dissociative subtype and its neurobiological basis.
- **Etkin and Wager (2007)** — meta-analytic imaging findings across anxiety disorders.
- **Germain (2013)** — sleep disturbance as a central feature of PTSD.
- **Hori and Kim (2019)** — inflammation in PTSD, with appropriate caution about causality.
- **Yehuda et al. (2015)** — an authoritative overview of PTSD, from epidemiology to treatment.
- **Felitti et al. (1998)** — the original adverse childhood experiences study.
- **Kessler et al. (1995)** — the National Comorbidity Survey estimates of trauma exposure and PTSD.
- **Page et al. (2021)** — the PRISMA 2020 reporting standard for systematic reviews.
