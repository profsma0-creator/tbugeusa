## 11.11 Stabilisation skills

Stabilisation aims to help a person tolerate arousal without avoidance, so that trauma-focused work becomes possible. It is not a preliminary that must be completed before any meaningful therapy; the evidence supports trauma-focused treatment as the core, with stabilisation as preparation and support.

Grounding techniques direct attention to present sensory information: the feel of a chair, the temperature of the room, the sound of traffic. They work partly by competing with trauma-related imagery and partly by restoring a sense of context. They are more effective when practised before they are needed, and when the person chooses techniques that suit them.

Orienting responses—looking around, naming objects, moving—interrupt the immobility and narrowing of attention that accompany threat. Controlled breathing can reduce hyperventilation-related dizziness, but it is not a universal remedy, and forcing slow breathing can increase panic in some people.

Affect labelling—putting feelings into words—is associated with reduced amygdala reactivity in imaging studies, though the effect is modest and not specific to trauma. Naming an emotion is not the same as explaining it away, and clinicians should avoid implying that a correct label will resolve distress.

The "window of tolerance" is a widely used clinical metaphor for optimal arousal. It has intuitive value but limited empirical grounding, and it should not be treated as a measured physiological boundary. Used as a shared language for pacing, it is helpful; used as a precise diagnostic criterion, it is not.

Stabilisation should be evaluated like any other component: if it is not helping the person engage, the plan should change rather than the person being judged unprepared.

## 11.12 Cultural and spiritual dimensions

Emotion regulation is shaped by culture. Some traditions emphasise restraint and emotional control; others value expression, lament or collective mourning. What counts as appropriate distress, and what counts as recovery, varies.

Spiritual and religious practices—prayer, meditation, ritual, community observance—are used by many people to regulate emotion and to make meaning of suffering. Research on religion and mental health reports modest associations with wellbeing, but the mechanisms are nonspecific and the effects depend on content, community and context. Religious coping can support resilience; it can also intensify guilt, fear or exclusion.

Clinical practice should ask, without presumption, whether spirituality or religion matters to the person and whether they want it included. Clinicians should not prescribe religious practice, denigrate it, or substitute it for evidence-based treatment. Nor should they treat a person's faith as a symptom.

Where a person's distress is expressed through spiritual language—possession, curse, ancestral displeasure—the clinician's task is to understand the meaning, exclude medical and psychiatric causes, and negotiate a plan that respects the person's framework while offering what the service can provide. Collaboration with trusted community figures may help, provided confidentiality and consent are respected.

Cultural competence is not a list of traits attached to groups. It is a disciplined curiosity about this person's language, history, family and expectations, combined with humility about what the clinician does not know.
