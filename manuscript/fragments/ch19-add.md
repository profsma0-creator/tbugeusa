## 19.11 Analysis, visualisation and reporting

Analysis should follow the question, not the other way round. The primary outcome, the comparison and the statistical model should be specified before the data are inspected.

Exploratory analysis is legitimate, but it should be labelled as such. A finding that emerges after examining many subgroups is a hypothesis, not a result, unless it is tested in independent data.

Statistical significance is a poor summary. Report effect sizes with confidence intervals, the number of participants contributing to each analysis, and the amount of missing data. For clinical trials, report the number needed to treat where it is meaningful.

Figures should be honest. Truncated axes exaggerate differences; cherry-picked individual traces mislead; error bars should indicate what they represent (standard deviation, standard error or confidence interval—each tells a different story). Bar charts of continuous data hide distributions; scatter plots or box plots with individual points are often better.

Selective reporting remains common. Studies that find null results are less likely to be published, and published studies often report outcomes that were not prespecified. Registration, reporting guidelines and access to protocols reduce but do not eliminate the problem.

Data sharing is increasingly expected. It allows reanalysis and replication, but it requires attention to consent, de-identification and the risk that participants in small, distinctive populations could be identified.

## 19.12 Writing, peer review and responding to criticism

Scientific writing should be clear, specific and proportionate. Claims should match the evidence: "associated with" is not "causes", and "in this sample" is not "in general".

Reporting guidelines exist for most designs—CONSORT for trials, STROBE for observational studies, PRISMA for systematic reviews, COREQ for qualitative research. They improve completeness and make appraisal easier.

Peer review is imperfect. Reviewers are busy, may lack methodological expertise, and may be biased toward familiar approaches. Review improves many papers but does not guarantee validity. Post-publication critique, replication and data sharing are essential complements.

Responding to criticism well is a skill. Address the substance, acknowledge limitations, provide additional analyses where appropriate, and avoid defensiveness. Reviewers can be wrong, but engagement is more productive than dismissal.

Retraction and correction are part of the system, not a sign that science has failed. What matters is that errors are identified, disclosed and corrected.

Finally, writing for different audiences is not a compromise of rigour. A plain-language summary that is accurate is more useful to participants and policymakers than a technically precise abstract that nobody reads.
