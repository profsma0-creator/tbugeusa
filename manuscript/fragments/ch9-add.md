## 9.11 Model organisms and what they can show

Much of what is known about the molecular biology of stress comes from rodents. Animal models allow controlled exposure, direct tissue sampling and experimental manipulation that would be impossible in humans.

Rodent models of chronic stress—restraint, social defeat, unpredictability—produce behavioural and endocrine changes that resemble aspects of human stress-related disorders. Maternal separation and limited bedding models are used to study early adversity. These models have identified mechanisms: glucocorticoid receptor regulation, CRH expression, hippocampal plasticity, microglial activation and epigenetic modification.

The limitations are substantial. A mouse is not a person, and a laboratory stressor is not a traumatic event embedded in meaning, relationship and culture. Measures such as freezing and corticosterone are proxies, not equivalents of human symptoms. Sex differences are often ignored because early studies used male animals, and this has distorted the literature.

Findings should therefore be treated as mechanistic hypotheses to be tested in humans, not as direct evidence about human experience. When a paper reports that a stress paradigm "causes depression-like behaviour" in rodents, the phrase in quotation marks is doing important work.

## 9.12 Reading genetic association studies critically

Several questions help in appraising a genetic study of trauma-related outcomes.

What was the sample size, and was it adequate for the effect being sought? Small studies in this field have produced many findings that later shrank or disappeared.

Was the phenotype well defined? "PTSD" assessed by self-report questionnaire is not the same as a clinical diagnosis, and trauma exposure is often measured retrospectively.

Was the analysis preregistered? Candidate-gene studies with flexible covariates and subgroup analyses are particularly prone to false positives.

Was the finding replicated in an independent sample, and in more than one ancestry group? Polygenic scores derived from European samples often perform poorly elsewhere.

Were confounders addressed? Population stratification, gene–environment correlation and assortative mating can produce spurious associations.

Does the effect size matter? A variant that changes risk by a few per cent may be scientifically interesting and clinically useless.

A single paper rarely justifies a change in practice. The most useful genetic findings are those that replicate across consortia, converge with other lines of evidence, and illuminate a mechanism.
