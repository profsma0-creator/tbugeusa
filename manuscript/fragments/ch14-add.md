## 14.11 Measuring sleep in clinical and research settings

Polysomnography records electroencephalography, eye movements, muscle tone, respiration, oxygen saturation and often cardiac activity. It is the reference standard for staging sleep and for diagnosing sleep apnoea, narcolepsy and periodic limb movement disorder. It is also expensive, unfamiliar and usually conducted for one night in a laboratory, which may not represent habitual sleep.

Home sleep apnoea testing is simpler and adequate for many cases of suspected obstructive sleep apnoea, but it underestimates severity when total sleep time is unknown. Actigraphy, usually a wrist device, estimates sleep and wakefulness from movement over days or weeks. It is useful for circadian patterns and for tracking change, but it misclassifies quiet wakefulness as sleep.

Self-report instruments, including sleep diaries and validated questionnaires such as the Insomnia Severity Index, capture the person's experience and its daytime consequences. They correlate imperfectly with objective measures, and that discrepancy is itself informative: sleep state misperception is common in insomnia and should be addressed in treatment rather than dismissed.

Consumer wearables increasingly claim to measure sleep stages. Validation studies show wide variation between devices and settings, and proprietary algorithms are often undisclosed. They can support behavioural change, but they should not be treated as diagnostic. Preoccupation with device-derived sleep scores has been called orthosomnia and can worsen insomnia.

## 14.12 Nightmares, dreams and emotional processing

Nightmares are vivid, disturbing dreams that awaken the sleeper and are remembered on waking. They are common after trauma, but not all post-traumatic nightmares replay the event; many involve themes of threat, loss or helplessness. This matters clinically, because imagery rehearsal therapy works with the nightmare as it occurs rather than requiring fidelity to the original event.

Why we dream remains unsettled. Threat-simulation accounts propose that dreams rehearse responses to danger; emotional-processing accounts propose that REM sleep supports the integration of affective experience; other accounts emphasise memory consolidation, network stabilisation or the random activation of circuits. None is established, and clinicians should avoid presenting any of them as fact.

What is better established is that nightmares can be treated. Imagery rehearsal therapy reduces nightmare frequency and distress, and it can be delivered in groups or digitally. Treating nightmares often improves sleep quality and daytime functioning, and it may improve engagement with trauma-focused therapy.

Dream interpretation as a diagnostic tool is not supported. Dreams can be meaningful to the dreamer, and exploring them can be therapeutic, but they do not provide reliable access to hidden memories or to a definitive account of the past.
