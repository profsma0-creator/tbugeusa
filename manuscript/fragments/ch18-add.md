## 18.11 Workforce, training and supervision

Even well-evidenced treatments fail if there are too few trained clinicians, too little supervision and too few hours in the week. Implementation is a workforce problem as much as a scientific one.

Training should include more than attendance at a workshop. Competence develops through observation, practice with feedback, supervised cases and routine outcome monitoring. Short workshops alone rarely change practice; they may improve knowledge and confidence, which are not the same as skill.

Task-shifting—training non-specialists to deliver structured interventions—has been studied chiefly in low- and middle-income settings, where specialist capacity is scarce. Trials of lay-counsellor-delivered treatments for depression and trauma-related conditions have reported benefits, but the evidence base is uneven and supervision remains essential. Task-shifting should extend capacity, not substitute an unaccountable service for adequate staffing.

Supervision protects patients and clinicians. It provides a setting to review complex cases, notice drift from protocol, manage risk and address the emotional impact of trauma work. Where supervision is unavailable, clinicians should create peer consultation and be explicit about the limits of their practice.

Staff wellbeing is an organisational responsibility. Heavy caseloads, inadequate time and unsupportive management predict burnout more strongly than the content of the work itself.

## 18.12 Routine outcome monitoring and service evaluation

Routine outcome monitoring uses standardised measures at the start, during and end of treatment. It can identify deterioration early, support supervision and provide accountability to commissioners and patients.

Measures should be brief, validated in the population served, and used to inform care rather than to rank clinicians. The PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 and measures of depression and functioning are common choices. Session-by-session measures are useful for feedback but add administrative burden.

Three pitfalls are common. The first is missing data: people who drop out often have the worst outcomes, and analysing only completers flatters a service. The second is gaming: targets can incentivise discharging complex cases early or selecting easier referrals. The third is inequity: aggregated data can conceal poorer access or outcomes for particular groups, which requires disaggregation.

Feedback-informed treatment has intuitive appeal, but trials show modest and inconsistent effects on outcomes. Monitoring is most valuable when it changes a clinical decision—adjusting the plan, arranging supervision, or offering an alternative—rather than when it generates reports that nobody uses.

Service evaluation should include access, waiting times, dropout, outcomes, adverse events and patient experience. A service that treats a selected minority very well is not equivalent to one that reaches the population it exists to serve.
