## 20.11 Teaching trauma neurobiology

Teaching this material well requires more than transmitting facts. Students need to understand levels of explanation, the difference between a mechanism and a treatment, and the limits of each method they encounter.

A workable curriculum moves from normal function to disruption to intervention. Students who understand the HPA axis in ordinary circumstances can grasp what changes under chronic stress; students who have never seen a conditioned response will struggle with extinction. Cases and laboratory demonstrations help, provided they are clearly identified as teaching material and do not require students to disclose their own histories.

Assessment should test reasoning rather than recall alone. Questions that ask students to interpret a finding, identify an alternative explanation or design a control are more informative than questions that ask them to name a structure. Where statistics and methodology are taught, they should be applied to real papers from the field.

Clinical and laboratory teaching require boundaries. Students should not be asked to practise assessment on each other's trauma, to disclose personal experiences, or to interpret images or samples beyond their competence. Simulation and standardised cases are safer.

Teachers should model uncertainty. Saying "we do not know" and explaining how the field might find out is more valuable than presenting a tidy account that collapses under scrutiny. Students who learn to tolerate uncertainty are better prepared for practice than those who expect certainty.

## 20.12 What would change practice

Several developments would materially change clinical practice if they were realised. None is guaranteed, and each should be judged by evidence rather than enthusiasm.

Reliable prediction of who will develop persistent symptoms after trauma would allow targeted early intervention. Current models perform poorly at the individual level, and the ethical risks of false positives—stigma, unnecessary treatment, insurance consequences—are considerable.

Brief, scalable treatments with durable effects would extend reach. Exposure-based and trauma-focused therapies are effective but require trained staff and time. Digital and guided self-help formats may help, but the evidence for unguided tools is weak and dropout is high.

Biologically informed stratification—matching people to treatments based on mechanism—remains speculative. The heterogeneity of PTSD suggests that subtyping may eventually help, but current biomarkers are not ready for clinical use.

Better treatment of sleep, pain, substance use and social adversity would improve outcomes without requiring new neuroscience. These are unglamorous priorities, and they are among the most likely to help.

Finally, equitable access would change more lives than any single discovery. Effective treatments that reach only a minority of those who need them are, in public-health terms, a partial solution.
