There is a verse in Deuteronomy that functions as a fence around the whole subject of revelation, and it is easy to read past it because it is so neatly balanced.

*The secret things belong unto the Lord our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law* (Deuteronomy 29:29).

The verse divides reality into two domains and assigns each to a party. The hidden things belong to God. The revealed things belong to *us and to our children*. And note the phrase that completes the second clause — *for ever* — and the purpose clause that follows it: *that we may do all the words of this law*.

This is one of the most important statements in the Old Testament about the economy of revelation, and it establishes something that will occupy this entire chapter: **the category of the concealed is not the opposite of the category of the revealed. It is its precondition.**

God hides things. He hides them on purpose, and He hides them for a period, and the period is not arbitrary. And when a thing is hidden across a generation, the hiding itself becomes the mechanism of the generational pattern this book has been describing.

### The three kinds of concealment

Scripture speaks of concealment in at least three distinct senses, and confusing them produces either presumption or despair.

**The first kind is the permanently secret.** These are the things God has not revealed and will not reveal, the contents of the divine mind that are simply not our business: the timing of the end, the reasons for particular providences, the full architecture of election. *The secret things belong unto the Lord our God.* Paul, considering the destiny of Israel, ends in doxology rather than explanation: *O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out!* (Romans 11:33). This kind of concealment is a rebuke to curiosity, and its proper response is worship rather than enquiry.

**The second kind is the temporarily hidden.** These are things God has decreed, intends to reveal, and has not yet revealed. They are concealed *for a season*, and the season has an end. Daniel is told to seal a vision until the time of the end (Daniel 12:4, 9). Paul speaks of a mystery *which hath been hid from ages and from generations, but now is made manifest to his saints* (Colossians 1:26). This is the category that matters for our subject, because it means that the church's knowledge of God is not static. It grows, and its growth is punctuated by disclosure events.

**The third kind is the deliberately veiled.** These are truths that are available and yet not accessible to a particular audience, because they are conveyed in a form that requires a key. This is the function of parable, of typology, of prophecy spoken in figures. Christ's own explanation of His parables puts it starkly: *Because it is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given* (Matthew 13:11). The concealment here is not about withholding information from the ignorant; it is about the state of the hearer.

Three kinds, then. And the second — the temporarily hidden — is where the generational structure lives, because what is hidden in one generation is frequently revealed in another.

### The mystery that God kept for four hundred years

The most important example in Scripture is the one Paul builds an entire theology upon, and it deserves careful treatment because it shows the mechanism operating at full scale.

*Now to him that is of power to stablish you according to my gospel, and the preaching of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery, which was kept secret since the world began, but now is made manifest, and by the scriptures of the prophets, according to the commandment of the everlasting God, made known to all nations for the obedience of faith* (Romans 16:25–26).

And again, in Ephesians: *Whereby, when ye read, ye may understand my knowledge in the mystery of Christ, which in other ages was not made known unto the sons of men, as it is now revealed unto his holy apostles and prophets by the Spirit; that the Gentiles should be fellowheirs, and of the same body, and partakers of his promise in Christ by the gospel* (Ephesians 3:4–6).

Consider what is being said, because it is astonishing. The inclusion of the Gentiles in the covenant was not a novelty of Paul's invention. It was *in the Scriptures* — the promise to Abraham, *in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed*, is itself a statement of it. And yet Paul says it was *not made known unto the sons of men* as it is now revealed.

How can a thing be in the text and not known?

The answer is that revelation is not merely publication. A truth may be stated in Scripture and remain unperceived by the generations to whom the Scripture is given, because the conditions for perceiving it have not yet arrived. The prophets themselves searched, and did not know what they were searching for. Peter puts it precisely: *Of which salvation the prophets have enquired and searched diligently, who prophesied of the grace that should come unto you: searching what, or what manner of time the Spirit of Christ which was in them did signify, when it testified beforehand the sufferings of Christ, and the glory that should follow* (1 Peter 1:10–11).

*Searching what, or what manner of time.* The prophets had the content and lacked the coordinates. Noah built an ark and did not know the date. Daniel received a timeline of seventy weeks and did not know the calendar. Abraham was told that his descendants would be strangers in a land for four hundred years (Genesis 15:13) and did not know the name of the king who would let them go.

Which means that God conceals things in the *text* as well as in the decree, and the concealment is not a defect in the text but a feature of the design.

### Why God conceals

We must now ask why. And there are, I think, four reasons visible in Scripture, each of which bears on the generational question.

**Concealment protects the timing of the fulfilment.** Habakkuk is told to write the vision plainly, *for the vision is yet for an appointed time ... though it tarry, wait for it; because it will surely come, it will not tarry* (Habakkuk 2:3). The vision is *written* — made permanent, made available, made retrievable — and simultaneously *waiting*. It must be recorded so that a later generation can find it, and it must remain unfulfilled so that the earlier generation does not seize it.

This is the reason for the largest structure in prophetic Scripture. Daniel is given a sequence that extends for hundreds of years, in weeks and times and half a time, and he is told to seal it (Daniel 12:4, 9). The purpose of the sealing is not to prevent the contemporary reader from understanding. It is to preserve the prophecy *for the generation it concerns*.

**Concealment preserves the freedom of God's action.** If everything were published in advance, the divine action could be predicted and, in principle, managed. God will not be reduced to a mechanism. *Remember the former things of old: for I am God, and there is none else; I am God, and there is none like me, declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times the things that are not yet done, saying, My counsel shall stand, and I will do all my pleasure* (Isaiah 46:9–10). He declares; He does not explain in advance.

**Concealment tests the reality of faith.** God told Abraham that his descendants would be afflicted for four hundred years (Genesis 15:13). He did not tell him how the affliction would end for the individuals who endured it. Abraham had to hold a promise whose timetable exceeded his life. And this is the most important function of concealment for our subject: **a promise which is hidden in one generation and fulfilled in another forces both generations to live by faith rather than by sight.**

Hebrews makes this explicit about the whole company of the old covenant's faithful: *These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth* (Hebrews 11:13).

**Concealment humbles the recipient of revelation.** If revelation were complete, the recipient would be complete, and the recipient would begin to function as a substitute for God. Paul, who received more direct revelation than any apostle, was given a thorn in the flesh and told plainly why: *lest I should be exalted above measure through the abundance of the revelations* (2 Corinthians 12:7). Even abundance is rationed, because abundance produces pride.

### The generation that inherits an unexplained promise

Now let us bring this to bear on the subject of the book.

A generation can inherit a promise it does not understand. This is not a marginal case in Scripture; it is the normal case.

Joseph is told in a dream that his family will bow before him (Genesis 37:7), and he has no idea how this will happen, and for thirteen years everything that does happen appears to contradict it. Moses is told that God will deliver Israel (Exodus 3:10) and not whether Pharaoh will agree. David is anointed king (1 Samuel 16:13) and then spends fifteen years in the wilderness with a price on his head. Mary is told she will conceive the Messiah, and is told nothing about the flight into Egypt, the twelve years of obscurity, or the crucifixion.

This is the ordinary texture of receiving a promise, and the pattern has a consequence that most of us have not thought through: **the generation that receives the promise is not usually the generation that sees it fulfilled, and the interval is where faith is exercised.**

And here is the practical implication for a father and a son. When a father receives a promise and dies before its fulfilment, the son inherits two things: the promise, and the *uncertainty* about it. He has a word of God in his hand with no evidence to support it, and he must decide whether to live as though the word were true.

This is where most generational collapse actually happens. It is not usually the case that the next generation denies God openly. It is that the next generation holds a promise whose supporting evidence has died with the previous generation, and the promise quietly ceases to function as a motivation.

### The son who has to inherit an oracle

Second Chronicles contains the account of a transition so abrupt that it is worth setting out in full, because it shows what happens when a promise passes to a generation that did not receive it.

Abijah, king of Judah, stands with a vastly inferior army against Jeroboam of Israel, and delivers a speech that is pure inheritance. He recites the covenant: *Ought ye not to know that the Lord God of Israel gave the kingdom over Israel to David for ever, even to him and to his sons by a covenant of salt?* (2 Chronicles 13:5). He recites the defection of Jeroboam. He recites the Levitical order. And he concludes: *and, behold, God himself is with us for our captain, and his priests with sounding trumpets to cry alarm against you. O children of Israel, fight ye not against the Lord God of your fathers; for ye shall not prosper* (2 Chronicles 13:12).

And God grants him the victory.

But look at the man. Abijah is described in 1 Kings 15 as walking in all the sins of his father, with a list of specific failings, and yet God grants him a lamp in Jerusalem *because David his father* did right (1 Kings 15:4–5). Abijah inherits the benefit of faithfulness he did not exercise. He uses a promise he did not receive. And the narrative lets both facts stand.

This is the mechanism of inheritance in its starkest form. And the question it poses to every generation is: **what do you do with a promise that is yours by inheritance and that you have not personally received?**

### The appointed time

And now the consolation, which is the reason this doctrine is good news rather than fatalism.

Paul's statement about the Incarnation is one sentence long: *But when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law* (Galatians 4:4).

*The fulness of the time.* The phrase implies that the time had a capacity, and that the capacity was reached. There was a point at which the world was ready, and the readiness was God's doing, and the chief evidence for that readiness is the Church's own Bible: the Greek language, the Roman roads, the dispersion of the Jews, the decline of the ancient cults, the despair of philosophy.

But there is a wider point. If the Incarnation waited for a fulness, then hiddenness is not merely a withholding. It is a **ripening**. The centuries in which the mystery of the Gentiles was concealed were not dead time; they were the period in which God was preparing the conditions for it.

This reframes entirely the experience of a generation that holds an unfulfilled promise. Such a generation is not a generation that has failed. It is a generation in the middle of a process whose beginning it did not see and whose end it will not see. And it has one job, which Daniel describes with total clarity when he asks how long the period will last, and is told to go his way: *But go thou thy way till the end be: for thou shalt rest, and stand in thy lot at the end of the days* (Daniel 12:13).

*Go thy way.* Daniel had been shown the largest chronological prophecy in Scripture, and the instruction given to him was to stop asking about the timetable and get on with the work that had been assigned to him in his own generation.

That is what a generation does with a mystery it cannot resolve. It keeps the record, it tells the children, it does the work that the hour requires — and it leaves the calendar to God.

There is no story in the Old Testament that is longer, and none that treats the relation between a revelation and a generation with such sustained attention. The account of Joseph occupies fourteen chapters of Genesis — more than the entire account of creation, fall and flood combined — and its central subject is not famine, or Egypt, or even forgiveness. Its central subject is a word of God given to a young man, and what that word does to a family.

We must read the story of Joseph as a study in generational transmission, because that is what it is. It shows us, in sequence, four things: what happens when a youthful revelation is disclosed to an older generation; what that disclosure costs; what God does in the interval; and how the revelation finally serves the very generation that rejected it.

### The revelation and its disclosure

*And Joseph dreamed a dream, and he told it his brethren: and they hated him yet the more* (Genesis 37:5).

It is worth pausing on the structure of that sentence, because the two halves are causally related, and the causality is the point. He dreamed. He told. They hated.

The dream itself is unambiguous and innocent. Joseph is binding sheaves with his brothers in a field; his sheaf stands upright; and their sheaves gather round and bow down to his (Genesis 37:7). A second dream follows, with the same content in a more emphatic form: the sun and the moon and the eleven stars bow to him (37:9).

The brothers' interpretation of the dream is entirely correct. They ask, *Shalt thou indeed reign over us? or shalt thou indeed have dominion over us?* (37:8). Yes. That is exactly what the dream means. And their father's response is also essentially correct, though it takes a different form: he rebukes Joseph — *What is this dream that thou hast dreamed?* — and then the narrator adds the detail that decides the whole story: *but his father observed the saying* (Genesis 37:11).

Two responses from the older generation. The brothers are angry. The father is watchful. And it is the father's watchfulness that keeps the word alive through the twenty-two years that follow.

### Why the disclosure was costly

Before we ask what the older generation should have done, we should ask the harder question: what should Joseph have done?

There is a reading of this story, common in preaching, in which Joseph is presented as an admirable young man who shares freely what God has shown him, and the brothers are simply wicked. It is possible that this reading is too simple.

Consider the situation. Joseph is seventeen. He has received a genuine revelation. He is the favoured son of a father who has already demonstrated an unwise partiality, and he is surrounded by half-brothers who have every reason to resent him. He then tells them, in the hearing of the whole family, that God has shown him a future in which they will bow to him.

Is this faith or is this folly? Both, perhaps. The revelation was real. The disclosure was unwise.

And there is something in the text that suggests the narrator sees it: the word used for Joseph's action is *he told*, and the immediate consequence is *and they hated him yet the more*. The phrase *yet the more* tells us that the hatred pre-existed the disclosure — the coat had already done its work — but the disclosure inflamed it.

So let us draw the first lesson of the chapter, and let it be stated without any softening on either side.

**A revelation that concerns your future and someone else's place in it is not automatically a revelation that you should announce.** There is a wisdom in timing which is a component of maturity, and the book of Proverbs is largely about it: *A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver* (Proverbs 25:11). Joseph's word was true and unfitly spoken, and the timing was God's business, not Joseph's.

But equally: **the older generation's inability to hear a word from God through a young person is a serious failure.** Jacob observed, which was good, but he did not act. And the brothers, confronted with a genuine divine revelation through their younger sibling, responded with violence. The question of what they should have done is answered by the rest of Scripture, and the answer is uncomfortable: they should have tested the word, remembered it, and watched.

### What God did in the interval

The story now takes a turn that becomes the pattern for everything this book describes, and it is worth naming clearly.

Between the dream and the fulfilment, thirteen years pass, and they are years of degradation. Joseph is thrown into a pit by his brothers, sold to Ishmaelite traders, sold again to Potiphar in Egypt, promoted to oversee the household, falsely accused by Potiphar's wife, imprisoned, given charge of the prisoners, forgotten by a man he helped — and then, in a single day, brought before Pharaoh.

Now consider what has happened to Joseph in those thirteen years. He has learned:
- an Egyptian language and administrative system (Genesis 39:1; 41:40);
- how to manage a great household (39:4–6);
- how a prison is run, and how prisoners are treated (39:22);
- that God gives interpretations (40:8);
- the geography of an empire and the politics of a court.

Look at what he has acquired and ask what use any of it would have been in his father's tents. The nineteen-year-old favourite son who burst into the family circle with a dream about sheaves would have been useless as prime minister of Egypt. The thirty-year-old prisoner who has interpreted a dream for a butler is exactly the man for the job.

**The interval was not a delay. It was a curriculum.**

This is the most important structural lesson of the Joseph narrative for generational revelation, and it can be stated as a principle:

**A revelation is not given in the form in which it will be fulfilled.** Joseph received a picture of the destination and none of the itinerary. And the itinerary — thirteen years of it — was not an interruption of the purpose. It was the means of it.

Which means that when the older generation looks at a young person with a revelation and asks *how could this ever come to pass?*, the honest answer is often *not as he is now*. And the corollary is equally important: a young person holding a revelation should expect, not merely to wait, but to be *changed* in the waiting, in ways he cannot anticipate and would not choose.

### The pride that had to be broken, and the wisdom that replaced it

There is a second thing that happened in those thirteen years, and it is subtler.

The Joseph of Genesis 37 is not a man you would want to work for. He is a teller of dreams, a wearer of a coat, a reporter of his brothers' conduct (37:2), and a man whose certainty about his own future is not matched by any evident humility about his own present.

The Joseph of Genesis 41 is a man who, brought before the most powerful ruler in the world and asked to interpret his dream, answers: *It is not in me: God shall give Pharaoh an answer of peace* (Genesis 41:16).

Compare that sentence to the one in Genesis 37:9. *Behold, I have dreamed a dream more.* And then this: *It is not in me.*

That is the difference thirteen years makes.

And notice how the change occurred. It was not by instruction. Nobody taught Joseph humility. It was taught by the pit, by the chains, by the false accusation, by the prison, by being forgotten, and by the two years in which a man who had done a kindness watched a door that did not open.

This is a word for both generations. For the young: the revelation you are carrying will not be fulfilled in the character you are currently carrying it in, and God will spend years correcting the vessel. For the old: if the young man in front of you is insufferable, this does not mean his revelation is false. It may mean he is seventeen.

### The revelation that serves the generation that rejected it

And now the outcome, which is the theological heart of the whole narrative.

Famine. Joseph, in Egypt, in control of the grain supply. And his brothers arrive, and they bow down — first the ten, and finally, when Benjamin is brought, all eleven. The dream is fulfilled to the letter (Genesis 42:6; 43:26).

And what does Joseph do with a revelation that has been proved right, in front of the men who tried to murder him for claiming it?

*And Joseph said unto his brethren, Come near to me, I pray you. And they came near. And he said, I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt. Now therefore be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that ye sold me hither: for God did send me before you to preserve life* (Genesis 45:4–5).

And then, more than twenty years later, after Jacob's death, when the brothers fear that the reckoning has finally come, he says it again in its definitive form: *But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass as it is this day, to save much people alive. Now therefore fear ye not: I will nourish you, and your little ones. And he comforted them, and spake kindly unto them* (Genesis 50:20–21).

*I will nourish you, and your little ones.*

Here is the pattern fully stated. A revelation was given to a young man. The older generation, unable to receive it, attempted to destroy both the man and the word. God preserved the man through a long education. And when the word was fulfilled, it did not serve the young man's vindication at the expense of his brothers. **It served the survival of the whole family, including the men who had sold him.**

This is the answer to the question the book is asking, and it is found in a story that is four thousand years old. The point of a revelation entrusted to the young is not the promotion of the young. It is the preservation of the old, in the generation when they will need it.

Joseph's brothers could not have seen this. Neither could Joseph. The meaning of the dream did not become clear until the famine came, and by then the brothers had forgotten they had ever hated him.

### The generational consequence: a new generation that did not know

There is one final element in the Joseph story, and it is easy to miss because it comes at the very end, in the next book.

*And Joseph died, and all his brethren, and all that generation. And the children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and waxed exceeding mighty; and the land was filled with them. Now there arose up a new king over Egypt, which knew not Joseph* (Exodus 1:6–8).

The generation that had been saved by Joseph died, and a generation arose in Egypt that had no memory of him. And the new king did not know Joseph, and therefore did not know the covenant that Joseph had kept, and therefore began to enslave a people he had no reason to fear.

Which means that the story of Joseph ends where the story of Judges will later begin. A deliverance, and then a forgetting. A promised preservation of a people, and then a generation that cannot remember the promise.

The mechanism that preserved Israel through the famine failed to preserve Israel's *memory* of it. And three hundred years later, a nation of slaves had to be told the name of the God of their fathers all over again — which is why God could describe Himself to Moses as *the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob*, and not as the God of the men who had been delivered from famine.

Every deliverance has to be told again. That is the lesson of Joseph, and it is the reason for everything in the chapters that follow.
