## The Psalm with a Commission in It

Psalm 78 is one of the longest of the psalms and one of the least used. It is a historical psalm, a recitation of Israel's story from the exodus to the anointing of David, and it is told in the form of a warning. But before it becomes a warning — before the psalmist has said a single word about the failures of the wilderness generation — he says something about *method*, and the method is the point.

*Give ear, O my people, to my law: incline your ears to the words of my mouth. I will open my mouth in a parable: I will utter dark sayings of old: which we have heard and known, and our fathers have told us. We will not hide them from their children, shewing to the generation to come the praises of the Lord, and his strength, and his wonderful works that he hath done* (Psalm 78:1–4).

Consider what has just been claimed. The psalmist is about to tell a story that everyone in his audience already knows, and he justifies the retelling by saying that he received it by being told. His authority is not originality; it is custody. *Our fathers have told us.* The psalm is a link in a chain that was already old when he joined it, and his stated intention is not to conclude the chain but to extend it: *we will not hide them from their children.*

This is the foundational text for everything that follows in this book, and it makes four claims about how the knowledge of God survives.

### Claim one: the truth is delivered, not discovered

The psalmist does not say that he searched out these things. He says he *heard* them. He does not claim to have arrived at them by reflection or experience; he claims to have received them from named human sources, and he names them: *our fathers*.

There is a whole theology in that verb. *Heard.* The knowledge of God's great acts is, from the beginning, auditory before it is anything else. Israel learned who God was by being told about the sea, the manna, the water from the rock, the fire on the mountain, the plagues, the long years in the desert. Almost none of the original recipients of the psalm had seen any of these things. They were not eyewitnesses of the exodus. They were the audience of eyewitnesses.

Which means that the central act of covenant religion is not observation but attention. *Incline your ears.* The word is nearly a command to lean in. It is the physical posture of someone who has decided that what is about to be said matters more than what he could find out by looking around.

This is a point on which modern religious culture is almost completely inverted. We have been formed by an age that prizes the authentic, the personally verified, the first-hand, and we bring that formation to Scripture, and the result is that we approach the Bible looking for our own discoveries rather than for the church's inheritance. There is nothing wrong with discovery. But the psalm assumes that the primary channel is speech and the primary act is hearing, and a reader who comes to the Bible with the expectation of private originality has misunderstood what kind of book the Bible is.

### Claim two: hearing is not a lesser form of knowing

The psalmist's second line has an unexpected construction, and it is worth looking at closely.

*Which we have heard and known.* Two verbs, joined. And they are not in the order we would expect. We would expect *known and heard* — experience first, report second. The psalm puts hearing first and knowledge second, and means by that something specific: the reception of testimony is a genuine mode of knowing.

This matters because there is a persistent assumption that second-hand knowledge is diluted knowledge. It is true that in a courtroom, hearsay is inadmissible; and it is true that in a laboratory, a reported result must be replicated. But it is not true in the covenant, because God has determined that it should not be. When Israel received the law at Sinai, the overwhelming majority of those present did not hear God speak directly. They stood at a distance and said to Moses, *Speak thou with us, and we will hear* (Exodus 20:19). God's response to this arrangement was not disappointment. He approved it: *They have well spoken that which they have spoken* (Deuteronomy 5:28). He then promised to raise up a prophet like Moses, through whom the arrangement would continue.

Which means the mediated word is not a concession to human weakness. It is the design.

Paul states the conclusion with startling force in a letter to a young man: *the holy scriptures, which are able to make thee wise unto salvation* (2 Timothy 3:15). Scripture, not experience. The written word received from childhood, from a mother and a grandmother. And to the Thessalonians he writes, *when ye received the word of God which ye heard of us, ye received it not as the word of men, but as it is in truth, the word of God* (1 Thessalonians 2:13). The two claims sit together without contradiction: it came through men, and it is the word of God.

For a generation raised on the suspicion of inherited claims, this is the sharpest correction the psalm offers. Your faith does not have to be self-generated to be your own.

### Claim three: silence is a decision

The third line is the pivot of the psalm. *We will not hide them from their children.*

Consider what kind of statement that is. It is a vow, and like most vows, it exists because the opposite is a live possibility. The psalmist does not say that the truth is difficult to pass on, or that the next generation is unwilling to receive it, or that the institutions are weak. He says that there is a temptation to *hide*, and he renounces it.

Why would anyone hide a testimony? The psalm does not speculate, but the rest of Scripture supplies several answers, and they recur often enough to be a taxonomy.

**Some hide it through neglect.** Not through malice but through distraction. The psalmist's daily life was harder than most of ours; a man concerned about the harvest, the tribute demanded by a foreign power, and the marriage of his daughters could simply fail to find the evening in which he told his sons what God had done. Neglect is the commonest form of concealment, and it does not feel like concealment. It feels like being busy.

**Some hide it through shame.** The testimony of God's dealings is frequently a testimony about weakness. To tell a son what God did in the year of the famine is, in some sense, to tell him about the famine. Israel's history is full of moments the fathers might prefer not to rehearse — the golden calf, the refusal to enter the land, the mercenaries, the compromises. There is a strong and entirely understandable instinct to hand on a cleaned-up version in which our own generation appears in a favourable light. That instinct is a form of hiding, and it is especially dangerous because what it conceals is precisely what the next generation most needs: the evidence that God rescues people who have failed.

**Some hide it through guarding.** This is the subtlest of the four. A man who has spent forty years acquiring a hard-won understanding may find that the understanding has become part of who he is, and that *giving it away* feels like a diminishment rather than a gift. Such a man will happily discuss the truth professionally and never transmit it personally. He answers questions but does not make disciples.

**Some hide it through an assumption of continuity.** This is the most modern of the four, and perhaps the most destructive. It consists of believing that because the next generation is present in the same building, it has absorbed what the present generation knows. It assumes that children automatically inherit the inner life of their parents by proximity. They do not. They inherit the outer life — the habits, the vocabulary, the social positioning — and they can do all of that with none of the substance.

Against these four, the psalm's vow stands: *we will not hide them from their children*. The echo of Job is exact, and probably deliberate: *If I have walked with vanity, or if my foot hath hasted to deceit; Let me be weighed in an even balance, that God may know mine integrity* (Job 31:5–6). The psalmist is placing transmission in the category of integrity.

### Claim four: the objective is hope, not information

The psalm now changes direction slightly, and states its purpose. And the purpose is not what one might expect from a psalm about history.

*For he established a testimony in Jacob, and appointed a law in Israel, which he commanded our fathers, that they should make them known to their children: that the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born; who should arise and declare them to their children: that they might set their hope in God, and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments* (Psalm 78:5–7).

Notice the structure. Three generations are named in five lines. There is the generation commanded — the fathers. There is the generation to come — the children. And there is a third generation — the children which should be born. The psalmist's target is not the next two decades; it is the generation after the next generation. He is thinking in units of a hundred years.

And the chain terminates in three outcomes, which are really one outcome described three ways:
- *that they might set their hope in God*  
- *and not forget the works of God*  
- *but keep his commandments*

Hope, memory, obedience. The purpose of transmission is to produce people who hope in God, remember what God has done, and obey God's commands. That is a description of the whole of the religious life, and the psalmist says the whole of it is a downstream effect of generational instruction.

This is worth stating as plainly as possible, because it is the argument of the psalm: **the survival of faith in a people is a function of transmission.** Not of the quality of the preaching, though preaching is a means of transmission. Not of the strength of the institutions, though institutions exist to make transmission possible. Of transmission itself — fathers telling sons, mothers telling daughters, elders telling the assembly, one generation deliberately, repeatedly, publicly, privately, formally and informally making known what it has received.

### What this chapter establishes

Four claims, then, and they are the foundation of the whole book.

The knowledge of God is **delivered**, not discovered. It is **known** through hearing, not merely about hearing. It is retained only where it is **spoken**. And its purpose is not the preservation of information but the **production of hope** in people who have not yet been born.

The psalm ends its introduction with a warning it will spend the following sixty-five verses illustrating. *When he slew them, then they sought him ... Nevertheless they did flatter him with their mouth, and they lied unto him with their tongues. For their heart was not right with him* (Psalm 78:34, 36–37).

The psalmist has watched this happen. He is not theorising about the next generation; he has seen men who were given everything and kept nothing. And the psalm he writes is his response — one man deciding, in the middle of national decline, that he will be the link that holds.

Which is precisely the decision this book asks of its reader.

There is a pattern in Genesis so consistent that it has the character of a law, and it is not one we would have chosen. Abraham receives a promise and dies without owning the land. Isaac receives the confirmation of the same promise and dies in a tent. Jacob wrestles with God, receives a new name, and dies in Egypt. Joseph saves the world and ends his life in a coffin in a foreign country, with instructions to be carried home at a date he would never see.

Four men. Four lifetimes. One promise, not one of them complete.

This is the most important thing the book of Genesis says about generational revelation, and it is said not by a statement but by a shape. The promise is received by a generation which cannot fulfil it, and is fulfilled in a generation which did not receive it. If you want to understand why God entrusts the future to the young, begin here: because the men with the experience to receive a promise are, by a simple consequence of age, not the men who will see it kept.

### Abraham: the man who saw it afar off

Abraham's story begins with seven commands and seven promises in a single sentence-and-a-half, which has to be among the densest pieces of language in Scripture. *Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee: and I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee, and make thy name great; and thou shalt be a blessing: and I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee: and in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed* (Genesis 12:1–3).

Consider what he was not told. He was not told which land. He was not told how long the journey would take. He was not told that he would spend the next twenty-five years waiting for the son through whom the promise of a nation would run and that he would be a hundred years old when the son arrived. He was not told about Egypt, or Sodom, or the famine, or Hagar, or the four hundred years of slavery his descendants would endure.

He was given a promise with an enormous horizon and almost no detail, and he walked into it.

Now consider the arithmetic. Abraham was seventy-five when he left Haran (Genesis 12:4). He died at a hundred and seventy-five (Genesis 25:7), which is a hundred years later. In the course of a century of following God, he owned precisely one piece of real estate in the promised land: the cave of Machpelah, which he purchased at full market price from the Hittites in order to bury his wife (Genesis 23:16).

This is the man of whom God said, *For I know him, that he will command his children and his household after him, and they shall keep the way of the Lord, to do justice and judgment; that the Lord may bring upon Abraham that which he hath spoken of him* (Genesis 18:19).

Look carefully at that verse, because it contains the whole mechanism of this book in an unexpected place. God is explaining why He will tell Abraham about the coming judgment on Sodom. And the reason is not that Abraham is a great man or a friend of God in some vague sense. The reason is that God knows Abraham will *command his children and his household after him*. The transmission is the qualification. God can trust Abraham with revelation because Abraham will not keep it to himself.

The epistle to the Hebrews states the conclusion with an image that ought to be famous: *These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth* (Hebrews 11:13).

*Seen them afar off.* That is the vocation of a father. He is given a sight of something he will not inhabit.

### The mechanism of the deferral

Before moving through Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, it is worth asking why the deferral exists at all. There are at least three reasons visible in Genesis, and they are not mutually exclusive.

**The promise is larger than a lifetime.** A land, a nation, a blessing to all families of the earth — these are not things a man can complete, and their incompleteness is intrinsic rather than accidental. Abraham could not have finished even if he had lived longer, because the object was a posterity, and posterity by definition takes time.

**The deferral prevents idolatry of the recipient.** There is a real risk in a promise delivered and fulfilled within one lifetime: that the recipient becomes the hero of the story, and the gift becomes his achievement. God's method avoids this entirely. Because Abraham never saw the fulfilment, he could never boast of it. The promise's fulfilment in later generations is unambiguously God's work, because the man who received it was dust.

**The deferral creates the necessity of transmission.** This is the most important of the three. If promises were always fulfilled in the lifetime of the recipient, no generation would ever need the next one. Because the promise outlives the recipient, the recipient must hand it on — and the act of handing it on is itself a spiritual discipline. Abraham had to explain to Isaac what he had received, and in explaining it, had to articulate what he believed. The structure of the promise forces the structure of the transmission.

This is why so much of Genesis is conversation. Abraham speaks to his servant about the angel who will go before him (Genesis 24:7) and speaks to Isaac about God's oath. Isaac speaks to Jacob. Jacob speaks to his twelve sons from his deathbed in one of the longest speeches in Genesis. The fathers are *talking*, in a way that no earlier chapter of world literature shows anyone talking, because they have something unfinished in their hands and they know it will not be finished in their hands.

### Isaac: the man who redug the wells

Isaac is the least dramatic of the four patriarchs, and it is easy to read his chapters as a pause between Abraham and Jacob. That would be a mistake, because Isaac's story is precisely the story of a man inheriting another man's work.

Genesis 26 is Isaac's chapter. There is a famine, and God speaks to him with an explicit instruction: *Go not down into Egypt; dwell in the land which I shall tell thee of*. Then the promise is repeated verbatim, with one significant addition: *And I will make thy seed to multiply as the stars of heaven, and will give unto thy seed all these countries; and in thy seed shall all the nations of the earth be blessed; because that Abraham obeyed my voice, and kept my charge, my commandments, my statutes, and my laws* (Genesis 26:4–5).

Do you see the mechanism? God is renewing the promise to Isaac *on the ground of Abraham's obedience*. The covenant is not being restarted; it is being continued, and the continuation is explicitly linked to what the previous generation did. Isaac benefits from a faithfulness he did not perform. This is not a moral irregularity; it is the whole point of a generational covenant.

Then comes the detail that makes the chapter unforgettable. Isaac settles in Gerar, digs wells, and the Philistines fill them in. He digs again, and there is a quarrel, and he moves on. He digs again, and there is another quarrel, and he moves on. He digs a third time, they do not quarrel, and he calls the place Rehoboth, saying, *For now the Lord hath made room for us* (Genesis 26:22).

And then the narrator tells us something that at first seems like a footnote: *And Isaac digged again the wells of water, which they had digged in the days of Abraham his father; for the Philistines had stopped them after the death of Abraham: and he called their names after the names by which his father had called them* (Genesis 26:18).

This is one of the most quietly devastating sentences in Scripture. The Philistines, after Abraham's death, went and filled in his wells. It is a specific and malicious act: not a war, not a conquest, just the systematic undoing of a dead man's work. And Isaac's response is to dig them out again, and to give them back their original names.

That is what inheritance looks like in practice. Not the creation of something new, but the reopening of something that was closed, and the restoration of the name it had when the father was alive. A generation that comes after a great man will find the wells filled in. They always do. The question is whether the generation after will recognise the wells as wells.

### Jacob: the man who held on

Jacob is the most difficult of the four, and the most instructive for anyone who has ever suspected that God's choice of a container is careless.

He begins as a supplanter — a grabber, a deceiver, a man who purchases a birthright from a hungry brother for a bowl of lentils and steals a blessing by impersonating his sibling before a blind father. He spends twenty years being deceived in turn by Laban. He returns home in fear, wrestles all night with a man whose name he never learns, receives a dislocated hip and a new name, and limps away.

And at the end of his life, he is in Egypt. The man who was promised a land dies in a country God had told his grandfather not to enter. He blesses his sons with words of prophecy that mostly concern the distant future, and he makes Joseph swear to carry his bones home (Genesis 47:29–31).

If you want a picture of the actual texture of faith across generations, this is it. Jacob's life is not a triumphant narrative of promise received and destiny achieved. It is a long story of a man holding onto something he cannot see, in a place where it does not appear to exist, and handing it to sons who will not see it either.

There is a phrase in Genesis used of the patriarchs which is easy to skim, and which says everything: *and he was gathered unto his people*. Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and at last even the generation of the wilderness — *gathered unto his people*. The phrase carries an implication of continuity past death. To be gathered to one's people is not merely to die; it is to be added to something.

### Joseph: the man who preserved the line

And at last Joseph, who is the pivot of the whole book of Genesis and the first place in Scripture where the pattern of the young servant of a revelation appears in full.

Joseph receives dreams as a young man, in which his family bows before him. He tells his brothers, and they hate him for it. He is sold into slavery, falsely accused, imprisoned, forgotten, and then elevated in a single day from prisoner to prime minister of Egypt.

When his brothers come to him, he says a sentence that deserves to be read carefully as a statement about generation: *But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass as it is this day, to save much people alive* (Genesis 50:20). Then he adds, *fear not: I will nourish you, and your little ones* (50:21). And later, at the end of his own life, comes the final act of transmission: *And Joseph said unto his brethren, I die: and God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land unto the land which he sware to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob. And Joseph took an oath of the children of Israel, saying, God will surely visit you, and ye shall carry up my bones from hence* (Genesis 50:24–25).

Joseph fulfilled the promise to the extent possible in his generation. He preserved the line. He did not, could not, deliver it. His last act is to make his descendants swear an oath about a future he will not see — an oath that will be remembered for four hundred years and discharged by Moses himself (Exodus 13:19) and finally by Joshua (Joshua 24:32).

Joseph's bones. Buried in Egypt in a coffin, carried out of Egypt four centuries later by a generation of slaves, and finally interred in the parcel of ground Jacob had bought at Shechem. That is a four-hundred-year act of generational obedience, and it begins with a dying man taking an oath from his children.

### The law of the unfinished

We can now state the pattern as a principle, because Genesis has stated it by repetition.

**No generation of God's people has ever been permitted to complete the assignment.**

This is not a failure of the men involved. Moses is the greatest figure in the Old Testament, and he does not enter the land. David is the standard by which every subsequent king of Judah is measured, and he is forbidden to build the house of God. The pattern runs through the whole Bible: the epistle to the Hebrews devotes an entire chapter to heroes whose list ends with *and these all, having obtained a good report through faith, received not the promise* (Hebrews 11:39).

Hear the principle plainly: **the promise is inherited by one generation and fulfilled by another.**

There are two sentences that follow from it, and each is a whole way of living.

The first sentence is for the older generation, and it is spoken by David himself: *for the house that I have to build is not for man, but for the Lord God* (1 Chronicles 29:1). A man who understands that the house is not for him can gather the material for a builder he will never meet. He is not diminished by handing on an unfinished project. He is participating in the very thing that makes the project worth doing.

The second sentence is for the younger, and it is the charge given to Joshua when he took up what Moses had left: *Now therefore arise, go over this Jordan, thou, and all this people, unto the land which I do give to them* (Joshua 1:2). Note the change of subject. Moses had led them to the edge. The word *arise* is now addressed to Joshua, and with the *arise* comes a promise that has no dependence on the previous generation at all: *as I was with Moses, so I will be with thee* (Joshua 1:5).

That is how the promise travels. Abraham could not enter; he could only send. Joshua did not receive; he inherited. And all of them had the same God.

There is a sentence, spoken by David to Solomon, which ought to be engraved over the door of every institution that has ever planned for a future it would not inhabit.

*My son, as for me, it was in my mind to build an house unto the name of the Lord my God: but the word of the Lord came to me, saying, Thou shalt not build an house for my name, because thou hast shed much blood upon the earth* (1 Chronicles 22:7–8).

And then, a few verses later: *behold, a son shall be born to thee, who shall be a man of rest; and I will give him rest from all his enemies round about ... He shall build an house for my name; and he shall be my son, and I will be his father; and I will establish the throne of his kingdom over Israel for ever* (1 Chronicles 22:9–10).

This chapter is about the difference between receiving a revelation and executing it. It is a distinction that is almost always blurred in practice, and the consequences of blurring it are considerable, because it determines how a father behaves toward a son, how an elder behaves toward a younger minister, and how a man behaves toward his own unfinished work.

David saw the temple. Solomon built it. And between seeing and building there is a gap that only one thing can cross: a deliberate transfer of vision from the man who saw to the man who will build.

### What David did with his unfinished work

The list of things David did, knowing he would not build, is one of the most instructive documents in Scripture. 1 Chronicles 22 through 29 gives it in order.

He **gathered the material**. *And David prepared iron in abundance for the nails for the doors of the gates, and for the joinings; and brass in abundance without weight; and cedar trees in abundance* (1 Chronicles 22:3–4). This is a man who spent the end of his reign accumulating building supplies for somebody else's project. The quantities given later in the chapter — a hundred thousand talents of gold, a thousand thousand talents of silver, and brass and iron beyond weighing — are almost certainly symbolic of enormity rather than strictly literal, but the point is unaffected: the preparation was lavish, and it was for another man's hands.

He **gave his son the pattern**. *Then David gave to Solomon his son the pattern of the porch ... and the pattern of all that he had by the Spirit* (1 Chronicles 28:11–12). The Hebrew word translated *pattern* is the same word used of the tabernacle pattern shown to Moses on the mountain. This is not a builder's sketch; it is a revelation. The detail continues for more than thirty verses, describing the arrangement of the courts, the chambers, the treasuries, the courses of the priests, the weight of the golden candlesticks, and the weight of every golden utensil. David received it *by the Spirit* and wrote it down for a man who was not yet ready to use it.

He **gathered the workforce**. *And David commanded to gather together the strangers that were in the land of Israel; and he set masons to hew wrought stones to build the house of God* (1 Chronicles 22:2). Alongside which he organised the Levites, the singers, the gatekeepers, and the entire administrative apparatus of the temple service that Solomon would inherit already functioning.

He **charged the leaders**. This is the step that most men in his position omit. David did not only hand the vision to his son; he bound the officers of Israel to it publicly: *Now set your heart and your soul to seek the Lord your God; arise therefore, and build ye the sanctuary of the Lord God, to bring the ark of the covenant of the Lord, and the holy vessels of God, into the house that is to be built to the name of the Lord* (1 Chronicles 22:19). A private vision transferred privately is fragile. A vision transferred publicly, in front of the men who will have to fund and staff it, becomes an institutional obligation, and it can survive the death of the man who conceived it.

He **prayed for him publicly**. The prayer recorded in 1 Chronicles 29:18–19 is a father asking God for his son's inner life: *give unto Solomon my son a perfect heart, to keep thy commandments, thy testimonies, and thy statutes, and to do all these things, and to build the palace, for the which I have made provision*.

And he **prayed with the whole assembly and worshipped** (1 Chronicles 29:20). The handover concludes not with an administrative act but with a doxology. *Thine, O Lord, is the greatness, and the power, and the glory, and the victory, and the majesty* (1 Chronicles 29:11). David's last public act is to make sure that nobody, then or later, mistakes the temple for a monument to himself.

### The distinction

We are now in a position to state the distinction precisely, because David has demonstrated it.

**Revelation is the disclosure of God's intention to a person. Execution is the realisation of God's intention through a person.**

These are not the same event, and they are not even the same kind of event. Revelation is instantaneous, passive in the recipient, and complete. The pattern arrived entire. Execution is slow, active, and partial — it involves materials, craftsmen, money, politics, delays, compromises and a lifetime of management.

There are at least four consequences that follow from confusing the two, and each of them is a recurring failure mode among godly people.

**The first consequence is presumption.** If I have received a revelation, I may conclude that I have received an assignment. But David received the revelation of the temple and was expressly forbidden to execute it. The two acts were separated by a divine prohibition. Anyone who assumes that a vision received is a commission granted has not read the text carefully.

**The second consequence is possessiveness.** If revelation and execution are the same act, then someone else executing my vision is a threat. But David gave the pattern to Solomon, prayed for him, funded him, and did not intervene. He appears never to have criticised the temple Solomon built, even though it was, in many respects, different in detail from anything David could have supervised.

**The third consequence is impatience.** If the revelation is complete, the executor is tempted to think the work should be equally rapid. But the temple took seven years and four months to build (1 Kings 6:1, 38), plus thirteen years for Solomon's own house. And this, with all the materials already prepared. The gap between the completeness of revelation and the slowness of execution is one of the chief sources of discouragement among those who carry visions.

**The fourth consequence is despair.** If I have received a revelation and cannot execute it, and I believe that the two are one thing, I will conclude that I have failed. David might have concluded exactly that. What he actually did was spend his final years preparing for a builder.

### Why the old see and the young build

We must now ask the question the title of this chapter raises, and answer it carefully, because there is a sentimental answer and a structural answer, and only one of them is true.

The sentimental answer is that age brings vision and youth brings energy, as though the two were complementary personality types. This is a platitude that does some harm, because it implies that vision is unavailable to the young (it is not; Joseph and Daniel and Jeremiah would all object) and that energy is the young's contribution to the church (it is not; energy is a property of temperament, not of age).

The structural answer is this: **the old see because they have had the time to see, and the young build because they have the time to build.**

David's insight into the temple was not a function of his years in the abstract. It was the product of a lifetime in which God had shown him things — starting as a boy in a field, continuing through the wilderness years, the wars, the psalms, the failures. The pattern of 1 Chronicles 28 was the deposit of decades. Vision of that quality is simply not available to a twenty-year-old, not because God withholds it, but because it is made of experiences the twenty-year-old has not had.

And Solomon's capacity to build was not a function of his years in the abstract either. It was the product of an available future. Seven years of construction, then thirteen more, then forty years of reign. David, at the end of his life, had neither the time nor the peace. Solomon had both.

This is not a hierarchy. It is a division of labour imposed by the finite nature of human life, and God has built the church to run on it.

### What the young inherit from a vision they did not receive

Solomon, at the beginning of his reign, does something that reveals how much he understood about his position. He goes to Gibeon, where the tabernacle and the altar are, sacrifices a thousand burnt offerings, and God appears to him and says, *Ask what I shall give thee*. Solomon answers: *Thou hast shewed unto thy servant David my father great mercy, according as he walked before thee in truth, and in righteousness, and in uprightness of heart with thee; and thou hast kept for him this great kindness, that thou hast given him a son to sit on his throne, as it is this day. And now, O Lord my God, thou hast made thy servant king instead of David my father: and I am but a little child: I know not how to go out or come in* (1 Kings 3:6–7).

*I am but a little child.* This is not false modesty — Solomon was probably around twenty. It is an accurate assessment of the gap between the inheritance he had received and his own capacity to administer it. And his request is the correct request for a man in that position: *Give therefore thy servant an understanding heart to judge thy people, that I may discern between good and bad: for who is able to judge this thy so great a people?* (1 Kings 3:9).

Note what he does not ask for. He does not ask for a new vision. He asks for the discernment to execute the vision he has inherited. And note what God says in response, because it is the divine endorsement of the whole arrangement: *Because thou hast asked this thing, and hast not asked for thyself long life; neither hast asked riches for thyself, nor hast asked the life of thine enemies; but hast asked for thyself understanding to discern judgment; behold, I have done according to thy words* (1 Kings 3:11–12).

A young man who inherits a revelation should not ask God to give him a fresh one. He should ask for the understanding to build what he has been given. The first request is a form of self-assertion; the second is a form of stewardship.

### The warning that follows

Solomon built the house. And Solomon also, in the end, built high places for the gods of his wives, and the kingdom divided in the generation after him. This too must be said, because it is part of the record and because it shows that *building* is not the same thing as *finishing*, any more than *seeing* was the same thing as *building*.

There was to be no generation that completed the work. David saw. Solomon built. Rehoboam lost ten tribes. And the true temple would be raised in three days, in a generation that had no idea what it was doing, by hands that killed Him.

Which is why the reader of this chapter should hold the lesson soberly. The lesson is not that the young succeed where the old failed. It is that no generation succeeds, and that the God of the generations is the only one who finishes what He starts.

<<<CHAPTER 4
There is a strange feature of the second commandment that readers often pass over because the first half of the verse is so much more arresting.

*Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them: for I the Lord thy God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me; and shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments* (Exodus 20:5–6).

The negative half of this sentence — iniquity visited to the third and fourth generation — is one of the hardest statements in Scripture, and it has occupied theologians for centuries. But the positive half is equally remarkable and far less discussed: mercy shown *unto thousands*. The word in Hebrew is *alaphim*, which can mean thousands of persons or thousands of generations, and it is deliberately set against the *third and fourth generation* of the previous clause.

The contrast is structural. Judgment propagates three or four generations; mercy propagates without stated limit. If the two halves of the verse describe how consequences travel, then God is telling us something essential about the way His dealings move through time: **divine action in Scripture is seldom addressed to an individual as an isolated unit. It is addressed to an individual as a link.**

This chapter is about why revelation is structured as a continuum, and it centres on the most important name in the Old Testament: the name with three generations inside it.

### The name that God chose for Himself

When God appears to Moses in the burning bush, He does not introduce Himself as *Elohim*, or as *the Almighty*, or as *the Creator*. He introduces Himself by a lineage.

*Moreover he said, I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. And Moses hid his face; for he was afraid to look upon God* (Exodus 3:6).

And later in the same encounter, when Moses asks what name he should give to Israel: *And God said moreover unto Moses, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, The Lord God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, hath sent me unto you: this is my name for ever, and this is my memorial unto all generations* (Exodus 3:15).

Consider what has just been said. This is the moment at which God is revealing His personal name, the tetragrammaton, the name by which He will be known. And He frames it as a *memorial*. The word in Hebrew is *zeker*, remembrance — the thing by which someone is called to mind. God's own chosen form of self-remembrance is a genealogy.

There are at least four things this tells us about God, and each of them bears directly on the subject of this book.

**First, God is not embarrassed by time.** A name built out of three generations takes centuries to assemble. The God who reveals Himself in this way is comfortable operating on a timescale that no individual life can encompass. He is not in a hurry, and He does not consider a century an inconvenience.

**Second, God binds Himself to particular people in particular histories.** He does not say *the God of the patriarchs*, a generic formulation. He names them. He names the deceiver and the man who bargained and the man who laughed. The specificity is astonishing. When God describes His own identity, He describes it as a set of relationships with named, flawed, dead men.

**Third, God is known differently by different generations of the same family.** This is important and easy to miss. He is the God of Abraham, who was called out of Ur. He is the God of Isaac, who was forbidden to go to Egypt. He is the God of Jacob, who wrestled at Peniel. The same God, three completely different encounters. Continuity does not mean uniformity, and the fact that a son's experience of God differs from his father's is not a departure — it is exactly what the name predicts.

**Fourth, and most significantly for our subject: God is willing to be known by what He did in the past.** When God says He is the God of Abraham, He is inviting Moses to know Him partly through a relationship that ended centuries earlier. This is the foundation of all testimony. It means that the knowledge of God is *cumulative* — that my knowledge of Him is meant to include what He did for people I never met. The saints who have gone before are not merely examples; they are, in a real sense, part of the evidence.

### Christ's use of the name

It is this name that Christ takes up when the Sadducees challenge Him on the resurrection, and His argument shows that the structure of the name carries more theological weight than even Moses may have realised.

*But as touching the resurrection of the dead, have ye not read that which was spoken unto you by God, saying, I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob? God is not the God of the dead, but of the living* (Matthew 22:31–32).

The Sadducees denied the resurrection because they accepted only the Pentateuch, and the Pentateuch, they argued, contains no explicit doctrine of life after death. Christ's answer is that the doctrine is present in the *grammar* of the divine name.

If Abraham is dead — if he has ceased to exist — then God's statement *I am the God of Abraham* is either a statement about a dead man or an empty formality. But God does not describe Himself as the God of the dead. Therefore Abraham lives. The present tense of the name is the proof of the resurrection.

Sit with the shape of that argument for a moment. Christ proves life after death by appealing to a covenant formula that spans three generations. Which means that the generational structure of the covenant is not incidental to the doctrine of resurrection — it is the vehicle of it. The reason the generations matter is that God's relationship to a man does not end when the man does.

### The continuum in practice

What does it mean, practically, that revelation is structured as a continuum? Three things, at least.

**It means that no one's knowledge of God is complete.** Abraham did not know God as Isaac knew Him, and Isaac did not know Him as Jacob did, and Jacob did not know Him as Moses would. It is not merely that each had different experiences. It is that God's self-disclosure *progresses* — Abraham receives a promise, Moses receives a law, David receives a throne, and finally the Word is made flesh. Revelation is progressive, and progress requires time, and time requires generations.

This is why neither a young Christian nor an old one should be surprised to find that they do not know everything. The older believer has had longer to accumulate; the younger has more of the progress still ahead of him. Neither state is a defect.

**It means that a generation's faithfulness is partly measured by what it hands on.** If God's dealings are a continuum, then the question put to every generation is not only *do you know God?* but *will the generation after you know Him?* This is the question that Judges 2 answers in the negative, and it is the question Paul puts to Timothy in his final letter.

**It means that the failures are cumulative too.** This is the darker side of the same structure, and the second commandment says it plainly. Iniquity travels to the third and fourth generation. The bitterness of a father becomes the cynicism of a son becomes the unbelief of a grandson. This is not superstition; it is observable fact, and it is the reason why Scripture lays such emphasis on the household as a site of transmission. What is handed on, whether good or evil, travels.

### The memorial to all generations

There is one more dimension to the name, and it concerns the future rather than the past.

*This is my name for ever, and this is my memorial unto all generations.*

The phrase *unto all generations* is a standing promise that the memorial will never be obsolete. The God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob will still be freshly knowable millennia after all three are dust. And the mechanism by which this happens is exactly what Psalm 78 describes: a chain of speakers and hearers extending indefinitely.

Which means that when a father tells his son about God, he is not merely being helpful. He is participating in the mechanism by which God has chosen to remain known in the world. And when a father does not tell his son, the mechanism does not have a backup.

The psalmist in the passage we are about to consider had understood this. It is written by a man who would have been conscious of the whole lineage standing around him — the sons, the grandchildren, the generation still unborn — and he makes a vow that is both to them and to God.

*One generation shall praise thy works to another, and shall declare thy mighty acts* (Psalm 145:4).

That is the divine memorial operating as designed. One generation, speaking. Another generation, hearing. And the name of God carried across the gap.

<<<CHAPTER 5
There is a scene in the book of Numbers that ought to be far more famous than it is, because it tells us exactly what a leader can and cannot pass on.

Moses is told by God that he will not enter the land. He responds with a request that is entirely characteristic: *Let the Lord, the God of the spirits of all flesh, set a man over the congregation, which may go out before them, and which may go in before them, and which may lead them out, and which may bring them in; that the congregation of the Lord be not as sheep which have no shepherd* (Numbers 27:16–17).

Notice the pastoral instinct in that sentence. Moses' first thought on learning of his own death is not for his legacy but for the flock. He is concerned about a leadership vacuum, and he asks God to fill it.

God's response is precise, and it distinguishes sharply between things that are transferable and things that are not.

*And the Lord said unto Moses, Take thee Joshua the son of Nun, a man in whom is the spirit, and lay thine hand upon him; and set him before Eleazar the priest, and before all the congregation; and give him a charge in their sight. And thou shalt put some of thine honour upon him, that all the congregation of the children of Israel may be obedient ... And he laid his hands upon him, and gave him a charge, as the Lord commanded by the hand of Moses* (Numbers 27:18–20, 23).

This chapter is about the two halves of that scene: what Moses conferred, and what God had already done.

### The four things that transferred

Read the passage carefully and four distinct actions appear, and they are four distinct kinds of transfer.

**The man was identified by God.** *Take thee Joshua the son of Nun, a man in whom is the spirit.* The initiative is God's. Moses does not select his successor by preference or by seniority among the elders of Israel; God names him. This is the first lesson of succession: the choice of a successor is a matter for prayer, not for the exercise of a leader's taste.

**Authority was publicly conferred.** *Lay thine hand upon him.* The laying on of hands is the biblical sign of identification and commissioning. It does not create a gift that was not there — Joshua already had the Spirit — but it publicly designates the person through whom the gift will now operate.

**An office was transferred.** *Thou shalt put some of thine honour upon him, that all the congregation may be obedient.* The Hebrew word translated *honour* is *hod*, and it denotes the visible dignity or authority of an office. Moses put his *hod* on Joshua. This is what an institution can pass on: a position, with all the authority that attaches to it.

**A charge was given.** *Give him a charge in their sight.* And in Deuteronomy, this is elaborated: *And he gave Joshua the son of Nun a charge, and said, Be strong, and of a good courage: for thou shalt bring the children of Israel into the land which I sware unto them: and I will be with thee* (Deuteronomy 31:23).

So: God identified, Moses laid hands, the honour was transferred, and the charge was given. Four acts, and all four are things that human beings can do, under God, in the presence of witnesses.

### The four things that did not transfer

And yet the very structure of Hebrews tells us that something essential did not pass. Consider the sequence in Joshua 1.

Moses is dead. Joshua is commissioned. And then God speaks directly to Joshua, and the content of what He says is telling.

*Moses my servant is dead; now therefore arise, go over this Jordan, thou, and all this people, unto the land which I do give to them ... There shall not any man be able to stand before thee all the days of thy life: as I was with Moses, so I will be with thee: I will not fail thee, nor forsake thee* (Joshua 1:2, 5).

Look at what God has to do in this passage. He does not say *I was with Moses, therefore you will succeed*. He says *as I was with Moses, so I will be with thee*. This is a fresh promise. Joshua is not inheriting Moses' experience of God; he is being given his own.

This is the crucial distinction, and it is the thesis of this chapter: **inheritance can be transferred; assignment cannot.**

There are four things, at least, which God must give afresh to every person, and which no amount of faithfulness in a mentor can convey.

**A personal call.** God called Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3). God called Joshua directly, with the words *as I was with Moses, so I will be with thee* (Joshua 1:5). God called Samuel by name (1 Samuel 3:10). God called Saul on the Damascus road (Acts 9:4). These are not transferable events. A young man who inherits an office but not a call is in the position of a man wearing a uniform not his own.

**A personal encounter with God.** The God of the fathers must become *my God*. This is said explicitly in Scripture in the context of covenant renewal: at Shechem, Joshua calls on Israel to *choose you this day whom ye will serve* (Joshua 24:15). Sixty years after the crossing of the Jordan, the call is still being given, because the generation that crossed is now dying and the generation in front of Joshua has a mediated relationship with God and needs a direct one.

**A personal knowledge of the Word.** *This book of the law shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night* (Joshua 1:8). Notice that this instruction is given to Joshua, not to Moses. It is part of the fresh charge. Whatever Joshua had absorbed by watching Moses, he still needed his own relation to the book.

**A personal accountability.** And here is the sharpest distinction of all. When Joshua takes over, he becomes personally responsible for his own decisions. The failure at Ai (Joshua 7) is Joshua's failure, not a legacy of Moses'. The Gibeonite covenant (Joshua 9) is Joshua's error. The king of Israel could not say: well, I am simply doing what my father did.

### Why the distinction matters so much

The confusion of inheritance with assignment has consequences in several directions, and each of them is a recognised pathology among Christian institutions.

**For the one receiving:** If I believe that inheriting the office is the same as receiving the assignment, I will enter a role with a confidence that is not warranted by my spiritual condition, and I will be surprised when God does not meet me where I stand. The recurring agonies of young leaders — the sense of being alone, of operating without power, of maintaining a form with no interior — are frequently the symptoms of a man who received an inheritance and assumed it was an assignment.

**For the one giving:** If I believe that conferring the office is the same as conferring the assignment, I may imagine that my job is done when the hands have been laid and the charge has been given. But Moses, who did everything correctly, still had to say to Joshua what God had said to him, and Joshua still had to be told *arise* by God Himself. A mentor's work is preparation, not causation.

**For the community:** If the community believes the two are the same, it will become impossible to tell the difference in practice between a legitimate successor and an impostor. This is exactly the problem that recurs in the prophets, in which the office is occupied by men with the official honour and none of the Spirit — *the prophets prophesy falsely, and the priests bear rule by their means; and my people love to have it so* (Jeremiah 5:31).

### The gift of the fresh encounter

There is a compassionate dimension to this distinction that ought to be stated plainly, because it is a source of great hope.

If assignment cannot be inherited, then it must be given. And God gives it. The entire force of Joshua 1 is the promise of a fresh experience of God's presence, made to a man who had lived his whole adult life, in effect, in the shadow of Moses.

Consider the first event after the crossing. Israel is camped at Gilgal. And then: *And it came to pass, when Joshua was by Jericho, that he lifted up his eyes and looked, and, behold, there stood a man over against him with his sword drawn in his hand: and Joshua went unto him, and said unto him, Art thou for us, or for our adversaries? And he said, Nay; but as captain of the host of the Lord am I now come. And Joshua fell on his face to the earth, and did worship* (Joshua 5:13–14).

Joshua meets the captain of the Lord's host and worships, and is told, *Loose thy shoe from off thy foot; for the place whereon thou standest is holy*. It is a striking echo of Moses at the bush — the same command, the same geography of holiness, a wholly separate encounter.

This is the pattern. Each generation must have its own holy ground. God does not ration the burning bush; He gives one afresh to each generation that requires it.

### The generational claim of Joshua

The book of Joshua concludes with a summary that must have taken considerable courage to write, because it makes a claim about fulfilment that could easily have been falsified.

*And the Lord gave unto Israel all the land which he sware to give unto their fathers; and they possessed it, and dwelt therein. And the Lord gave them rest round about, according to all that he sware unto their fathers ... And there failed not ought of any good thing which the Lord had spoken unto the house of Israel; all came to pass* (Joshua 21:43–45).

*All came to pass.* Four hundred years after Abraham was called out of Ur, in a generation that had never met him, in a land they had never seen, a writer looks at the record and says: everything God promised to the fathers, He has kept.

That is what inheritance is for. Not so that a generation can inherit a task, but so that a generation can inherit a *promise*, and prove it true.

And yet — and this too is part of the pattern — the tribes do not drive out all the inhabitants of the land. Judges opens with failure. The book of Hebrews will later observe of Joshua's rest that *if Jesus had given them rest, then would he not afterward have spoken of another day* (Hebrews 4:8). The fulfilment at Gilgal is real and it is partial. There is always another day, and another generation, and another crossing.

Which is why, at the end of the book that bears his name, Joshua has to gather the people and preach the same sermon Moses preached: *choose you this day whom ye will serve* (Joshua 24:15). Because even after everything God has done — even after the sea and the manna and the walls that fell — the assignment must be received afresh by each generation that means to have it.
