## John Mark: Failure, Restoration and Second Chances

**Every movement that has ever survived its first generation has had to solve the same problem, and it is not the problem of recruiting the young. It is the problem of what to do with a young man who has already failed.**

**And the New Testament gives the question a full answer in the life of one man, recorded in four stages across twenty years, and none of the four stages is decorative.**

**He left an assignment. He was the cause of a quarrel that split the two most senior men in the church. He was restored by a kinsman who risked his own standing to do it. And he ended as one of the four men Paul wanted beside him at the end of his life.**

**And he wrote one of the four Gospels.**

### The young man who went home

**The first mention of him is at the beginning of the first missionary journey, and it is a mention of his usefulness.**

*And Barnabas and Saul returned from Jerusalem, when they had fulfilled their ministry, and took with them John, whose surname was Mark* (Acts 12:25).

*And when they were at Salamis, they preached the word of God in the synagogues of the Jews: and they had also John to their minister* (Acts 13:5).

**The Greek for *minister* is *hypereten*, and in its ordinary use it denotes an under-rower — the man who pulls an oar on the lower deck of a ship.** It is the word for an assistant, and in Luke's usage it is a subordinate role with real work attached.

**So John Mark was taken on a mission as an assistant.** And he was well chosen for it in every visible respect: he was from a Jerusalem family, his mother's house was a place where the church gathered to pray (Acts 12:12), **and he was a kinsman of Barnabas, who was a Levite of Cyprus (Acts 4:36; Colossians 4:10).**

**And then the journey turned inland, and he went home.**

*Now when Paul and his company loosed from Paphos, they came to Perga in Pamphylia: and John departing from them returned to Jerusalem* (Acts 13:13).

**Two facts about that sentence deserve attention.**

**The first is the geography.** The team had come to Perga, on the coast of Pamphylia, and from there the route to the interior ran up through the Taurus mountains to Pisidian Antioch, a climb of several thousand feet through notoriously dangerous passes. **The voyage from Cyprus had been comfortable; the next stage was the hard one.** And from Perga there was still a harbour, and from the harbour a ship, and from the ship a route home to a mother who had a large house in Jerusalem.

**The second is the phrase Luke uses: *departing from them.***

**The Greek is *apochoresas ap' auton* — he withdrew from them, he separated himself from them.** Luke does not write that he was sick, or that he was sent home, or that a family matter required him. **He withdrew from the team, at the point where the cost of continuing rose and the way back was still open.**

**And that is where young men leave, and it is worth naming precisely because it is so frequently misdescribed as a calling.** A man does not usually abandon an assignment at the beginning, when the decision is fresh and the company is encouraging. **He abandons it in the middle of the climb, when the road has become steeper than the estimate, when the results are not yet visible, and when the way home is still available.**

**And there is a further detail, and it comes from the second Gospel, if the tradition is right that the unnamed young man of the arrest is the evangelist himself.**

*And there followed him a certain young man, having a linen cloth cast about his naked body; and the young men laid hold on him: and he left the linen cloth, and fled from them naked* (Mark 14:51–52).

**If that is John Mark, and the detail is so peculiar that it almost certainly is, then the man who wrote the Gospel was the man who ran.** He preserved in his own account the story of a young man who fled in fear and left his clothes behind, **and he did it anonymously, which is the signature of a confession.**

**And this is worth saying at the outset of the chapter, because the New Testament's most detailed account of a young man's failure was written by the man who failed.**

### The quarrel that split an apostolic team

**And the second stage is the most uncomfortable passage in the book of Acts, because it records a division between the two men who had evangelised half the known world together.**

*And some days after Paul said unto Barnabas, Let us go again and visit our brethren in every city where we have preached the word of the Lord, and see how they do. And Barnabas determined to take with them John, whose surname was Mark. But Paul thought not good to take him with them, who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work. And the contention was so sharp between them, that they departed asunder one from the other: and so Barnabas took Mark, and sailed unto Cyprus; and Paul chose Silas, and departed, being recommended by the brethren unto the grace of God* (Acts 15:36–40).

**Set out the two positions honestly, because the passage will not allow us to make one man the villain.**

**Paul's position is stated with precision: *who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work.***

**Note that he does not say Mark was a bad man, or a coward, or a false brother. He states the facts of the case and he states what it meant operationally: he went not with us to the work.** And the proposed journey of Acts 15:36 was a return to the churches of the first journey, to confirm men and women who were being persecuted. **A re-run of the same hard route with the same man who had already turned back was, by any reasonable reading, a risk to the mission.**

**And there is a further consideration that Paul would have felt strongly and that Luke does not mention: the credibility of the work among the churches.** The new believers in Lystra and Iconium had watched the team arrive and had watched one of them leave. **To bring that man back would require the churches to trust a man who had already gone home.**

**So Paul's refusal was not vindictive, and it was not arbitrary, and the charge he attached to it is accurate.**

**And Barnabas' position is not stated in an argument at all; it is stated in an action: *Barnabas determined to take with them John, whose surname was Mark.***

**The Greek for *determined* is *ebouletо*, he resolved, he made a deliberate decision of the will.** And the man who made it is the one whose name means *son of consolation* (Acts 4:36), the man who had taken the young Pharisee Saul to the apostles when everybody was afraid of him.

**Barnabas was not being sentimental.** He was applying a policy that had built the church. **Every significant leader in the first generation had been admitted to the work on Barnabas' advocacy, including the man he was now arguing with.**

**And the outcome is recorded without any editorial comment on who was right: *the contention was so sharp between them, that they departed asunder one from the other.***

**The Greek for *contention* is *paroxysmos*, from which we take *paroxysm* — an outburst, a sharp provocation.** These were not two men disagreeing in a committee. **They separated, and the separation was painful enough that Luke, who records it, was a companion of Paul's later journeys.**

**And the passage teaches four things that no amount of pious polishing should be allowed to remove.**

**The first is that a real disagreement between two good men can cost the church something and still be used by God.** The outcome of the split was two mission teams instead of one — Barnabas to Cyprus and Paul through Syria and Cilicia — and the church in the provinces of the eastern Mediterranean was strengthened by both.

**The second is that the young man's failure was a matter of public record and could not be managed by silence.** Paul named it, and the naming was necessary, and a restoration that requires everyone to pretend the failure did not happen is not a restoration at all.

**The third is that Paul's judgement on the case was provisional and not final, and he changed it.** He wrote the sentence about Mark's desertion once and never wrote it again, and before the end of his life he wrote the opposite.

**And the fourth is that Barnabas paid for his advocacy.** He lost the companionship of Paul on the second journey, and there is no record anywhere in the New Testament of Barnabas and Paul travelling together again. **He bought Mark's restoration with his partnership in the greatest missionary enterprise of the age.** That is the price of the advocate's role, and it is why so few are willing to fill it.

### Barnabas: the career of a restorer

**And the man who bought Mark back deserves a full account of his own record, because the whole of it is the same activity performed four times.**

**The first case: he gave up property for people he did not yet know.**

*And Joses, who by the apostles was surnamed Barnabas, (which is, being interpreted, The son of consolation,) a Levite, and of the country of Cyprus, having land, sold it, and brought the money, and laid it at the apostles' feet* (Acts 4:36–37).

**A Levite was not supposed to hold land in Israel at all, and this one had property in Cyprus, and he sold it and put the money at the disposal of the apostles.** He is introduced to us as a man who had already transferred his resources to a body of men he had no authority over.

**The second case: he vouched for a man everybody else feared.**

*And when Saul was come to Jerusalem, he assayed to join himself to the disciples: but they were all afraid of him, and believed not that he was a disciple. But Barnabas took him, and brought him to the apostles, and declared unto them how he had seen the Lord in the way, and that he had spoken to him, and how he had preached boldly at Damascus in the name of Jesus* (Acts 9:26–27).

**The church in Jerusalem had good reasons for the fear. This was the man who had held the coats at Stephen's stoning and had imprisoned believers house by house.** And Barnabas took him by the hand, brought him into the apostolic assembly, **and stood there as his evidence.**

**Consider what Barnabas did in that room.** He assumed the liability. If Saul of Tarsus had later proved to be a spy, the man who had brought him in would have carried the blame. **Every advocate in every generation is doing the same thing, and it is why advocacy is rare.**

**The third case: he went and fetched an obscure man out of obscurity.**

*Then departed Barnabas to Tarsus, for to seek Saul: and when he had found him, he brought him unto Antioch. And it came to pass, that a whole year they assembled themselves with the church, and taught much people* (Acts 11:25–26).

**Saul had been sent to Tarsus years earlier and, as far as the record goes, had been doing nothing conspicuous.** Barnabas travelled to find him, brought him to the largest opportunity in the Gentile church, and then spent a year working beside him. **The future apostle of the Gentiles had his career because one man went looking for him.**

**And the fourth case is Mark.**

**Four restorations in one career, and the method is identical in all four: he identified a man, he took a measured risk, he put his own name on the line, he gave him a place with responsibility, and he stayed with him long enough for the change to be visible.**

**And the last element is the one that is normally omitted, and it is the element that made Mark's restoration real rather than ceremonial: Barnabas took him back onto the route.**

**They sailed for Cyprus (Acts 15:39) — the island where the first journey had begun, and the region from which Barnabas' family came. And the next stages of that journey are unrecorded, which means that the only surviving fact about Barnabas' last missionary work is that he did it with the young man who had failed.**

**A restoration ceremony is not a restoration. A second assignment on the same road is a restoration.** And the man who supplies it must, like Barnabas, be willing to have his own record attached to the outcome.

### What the restored man did with twenty years

**And the New Testament then records the outcome, and it is a catalogue of full standing.**

**First, he is numbered among Paul's fellowlabourers.**

*Marcus, Aristarchus, Demas, Lucas, my fellowlabourers* (Philemon 24).

**Second, he is commended to the churches by the man who had refused to take him.**

*Aristarchus my fellowprisoner saluteth you, and Marcus, sister's son to Barnabas, (touching whom ye received commandments: if he come unto you, receive him)* (Colossians 4:10).

**Read that parenthetical instruction carefully, because it is a formal act of reception.** Paul is writing to a church he did not plant and giving an explicit instruction about a specific man: **if he comes to you, receive him.** The previous barriers to Mark's credibility in the churches are being removed by an apostolic letter.

**And this is the practical architecture of restoration, and it has three parts.** There must be an advocate who takes the risk, an assignment to re-enter the work, **and a community that is told to receive the man when he arrives.** Without the third, the restored man circulates among churches that still remember the failure, and the memory does the work of a verdict.

**Third, he became a son to Peter.**

*The church that is at Babylon, elected together with you, saluteth you; and so doth Marcus my son* (1 Peter 5:13).

**Peter calls him *my son*.** Which means this young man who began under Paul and was recovered by Barnabas ended under the care of the leading apostle, **and the relationship was close enough to be described paternally in a letter that the whole church would read.**

**And there is a further implication, and it is instructive.** Peter's Gospel is the one Mark wrote. The man who failed on the mission field became the interpreter of the chief apostle and the author of the earliest account of the life of Christ. **Which means the New Testament's second Gospel is the work of a restored deserter, and it exists because an older man decided to take him on another journey.**

**And fourth, he is the subject of Paul's last written request.**

*Only Luke is with me. Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry* (2 Timothy 4:11).

**Consider the situation of that sentence.** Paul is in a Roman prison, awaiting execution; most of his companions have gone; **the two men he wants beside him are Luke and Mark.** And the word he uses of Mark is the word of a satisfied employer: *euchrestos eis diakonian* — useful, serviceable, profitable for the ministry.

**And the same word appears in the letter to Philemon, of another restored man:**

*Which in time past was to thee unprofitable, but now profitable to thee and to me* (Philemon 11).

**Both Mark and Onesimus were once described as useless and are later described as useful, and in both cases it took years and a risk-taking advocate.** Paul's vocabulary of restoration is consistent: he measures men by what they become, and he is willing to say so in writing.

**And the timing should be stated in plain years.** The desertion at Perga happened around AD 46. The refusal at Acts 15 happened around AD 49. The letter to the Colossians and Philemon, with Mark listed as a fellowlabourer, around AD 60. **And 2 Timothy, with the request for Mark, around AD 66.**

**Twenty years between the failure and the final commendation.**

**Which means the whole of the restoration took longer than the whole of the first missionary journey, and nobody in the narrative ever asked Mark to be restored in a week.** The Advocate's work is measured in decades, and a church that expects a fallen young man to be fully restored within a season has not understood the book of Acts.

### The other case in the same band: Demas

**And the New Testament is honest enough to give us the contrary outcome in the same circle of workers, and it must be examined because it prevents us from promising what God has not promised.**

**Demas appears three times, and the three appearances trace a decline.**

*Luke, the beloved physician, and Demas, greet you* (Colossians 4:14). He is a companion, listed without any note of failure.

*Marcus, Aristarchus, Demas, Lucas, my fellowlabourers* (Philemon 24). He is a fellowlabourer, which is a title of standing.

*For Demas hath forsaken me, having loved this present world, and is departed unto Thessalonica* (2 Timothy 4:10). And now he is a deserter, and the reason given is a love of the present world, and the direction he went is named.

**The comparison with Mark must be handled carefully, because it is possible to draw the wrong conclusion from it in both directions.**

**The right conclusion is not that Demas had less opportunity than Mark.** Both travelled with Paul, both knew the churches, both were listed among the workers. **And the difference is not stated to be a difference of environment.**

**And the right conclusion is certainly not that restoration works mechanically.** A community can build the whole apparatus — the advocate, the assignment, the letter of reception — and a man may still depart.

**What the comparison does establish is the necessity of the mechanism and not its sufficiency.** Mark was restored because a man with standing put himself on the line for him, a second journey was made available, and churches were told to receive him. **Demas' case tells us that the machinery must not be confused with the grace; and Mark's case tells us that the absence of the machinery is fatal.**

**And there is a third figure in the same chapter of 2 Timothy, and his case is different again:** *Alexander the coppersmith did me much evil: the Lord reward him according to his works* (2 Timothy 4:14).

**Paul distinguishes between the two.** Alexander is handed over to God's judgement; Demas is recorded with regret and no curse; **Mark is requested by name.** A mature man's responses to failure are not uniform, and the discernment required to tell them apart is one of the marks of eldership.

### The architecture of a second chance

**And the whole subject can now be assembled into a mechanism, because in Mark's case every part of it is visible and none of it was accidental.**

**The first component is an advocate with standing who accepts liability.**

**Barnabas' entire career was built on this act.** He vouched for Saul when the church feared him (Acts 9:27). He fetched Saul from Tarsus (Acts 11:25). He took Mark when Paul refused him (Acts 15:39). **And in each case he attached his own credibility to the man's future.**

**Which means that the restoration of a failed young man is not normally accomplished by a procedure, but by an older man who is willing to be embarrassed if it goes wrong.**

**The second component is an accurate statement of the failure, made once, and never used again as a weapon.**

**Paul's sentence at Acts 15:38 is the model: *who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work.***

**No adjective is used that the facts do not support.** He does not call him a traitor, he does not speculate about his motives, and he does not raise the matter again after the disagreement is over. **Two decades later he uses the word *profitable* about the same man in writing.**

**Which is the practical rule for every older man who has to describe a young man's failure: state the facts of the assignment that was not completed, refrain from the character verdict, and then close the file.**

**The third component is a second assignment with real weight on the same road.**

**Barnabas took Mark back to Cyprus and beyond.** He did not give him a light duty in a safe place. **He put him back in the work of travelling and preaching, which meant he had to face the same conditions that had defeated him.**

**Which is why restoration is slow: it requires a re-entry through the place of failure.** And the young man who wants to be restored must expect to be sent back up the same mountain.

**The fourth component is the community's told obligation to receive him.**

*If he come unto you, receive him* (Colossians 4:10).

**The instruction is necessary because of the memory problem.** A church that has heard the story once will continue to apply the story to every subsequent appearance of the man. **And the apostolic word to a congregation about a restored worker is: receive him. Not supervise him, not remind him, not treat him as a permanent probationer. Receive him.**

**And the fifth component is time.**

**Twenty years, and no announcement, and no ceremony, and no book about the healing of his wound.** Just a young man who went home, and an old man who went and got him, and a long series of ordinary journeys, **until the day an imprisoned apostle asked for him by name because he was useful.**

### And the older man must be allowed to change his mind

**And there is a final lesson in this chapter that is directed to the older generation, and it concerns Paul.**

**The man who refused Mark in Acts 15 and the man who asked for him in 2 Timothy 4 are the same man, and his change of position is recorded in Scripture and is not presented as inconsistency.**

**It is presented as the truth being allowed to update a judgement.**

**And this is a mark of maturity that is frequently mistaken for weakness.** A young man thinks consistency means never revising a decision. **An older man knows that a decision is a response to a set of facts, and that when the facts change, the decision must change, or the decision has become an idol.**

**Paul's revision is also public and in writing.** 2 Timothy 4:11 commands Timothy to bring Mark, in a letter that would circulate among the churches, from the man who had previously written that Mark had abandoned the work. **Anybody in those churches who remembered the earlier judgement would receive the correction in the same medium that had carried the original.**

**And that is how an older man protects a restored young man from the older man's own former words.** If you previously said a thing about a man, and the man has changed, you must say so in writing, in the same place, with the same publicity. **Otherwise the community will continue to obey your earlier sentence long after you have abandoned it.**

**And Barnabas, it should be noted, was right.** Not right in the sense that Paul was wicked — the passage does not permit that reading — **but right in the sense that the judgement he was willing to stake his partnership on turned out to be vindicated in the man's life.** The advocate is not always the sentimental party, and the refusal is not always the hard-headed one.

**And Barnabas himself fell later, and it is recorded too.** At Antioch, when men came from James, he was carried away with Peter's dissimulation, so that even Paul had to withstand him to his face, and the Jewish brethren followed him in the error (Galatians 2:11–13). **Which is a necessary footnote in a chapter about second chances: the man who restored Mark also needed restoring.** The advocate is not sinless, and a community that requires its restorers to be without fault will never have any.

**And Paul and Barnabas were reconciled, and the record contains the evidence.**

*Or I only and Barnabas, have not we power to forbear working?* (1 Corinthians 9:6). And at Jerusalem the two of them received *the right hands of fellowship* together (Galatians 2:9).

**So the quarrel did not end in permanent estrangement, and the young man who had caused it lived to be asked for by the man who had refused him.**

**Which is what a second chance looks like in Scripture: not a ceremony, not an announcement, and not a shortcut. A kinsman's risk, a road back up the same mountain, a letter to the churches, twenty years of ordinary service, and a request from a prison cell.**

### Principles

**First: young men leave at the point where the cost rises and the way home is still open.** *John departing from them returned to Jerusalem* — at Perga, before the climb into the mountains, with a harbour behind him.

**Second: the failure must be named accurately, once, and never turned into a character verdict.** *Who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work.* No adjectives the facts do not support.

**Third: a real disagreement between good men may cost the church something and still be used by God.** *The contention was so sharp between them, that they departed asunder.* Two teams went out instead of one.

**Fourth: restoration requires an advocate who accepts the liability.** *Barnabas took him, and brought him to the apostles*; *Barnabas took Mark, and sailed unto Cyprus.* The advocate stakes his own record on another man's future, and Barnabas lost his partnership with Paul to do it.

**Fifth: the advocate's method is consistent across a whole career.** Provision (Acts 4:37), access (Acts 9:27), opportunity (Acts 11:25–26), and a second journey (Acts 15:39). **Four restorations, one method.**

**Sixth: the community must be told to receive the restored man.** *If he come unto you, receive him.* Without that instruction the churches' memory operates as a permanent verdict.

**Seventh: restoration takes years, and it requires a re-entry through the place of failure.** Twenty years between Perga and *take Mark, and bring him with thee.*

**Eighth: the machinery is necessary and it is not sufficient.** Demas had the same band of companions and the same opportunities and forsook Paul for the present world.

**And ninth: an older man must be allowed to revise his judgement, publicly, in the same medium as the original.** *He is profitable to me for the ministry*, written by the man who had once refused to take him.

### For the fathers

- **Say the failure accurately and then close the file.** *He went not with them to the work.* No adjectives the facts do not support, and no repetition of the charge once it has been answered.
- **Ask whether you are willing to be the advocate.** Restoration is not a procedure; it is an older man attaching his own credibility to a young man's future. Barnabas lost Paul's partnership to do it.
- **Send him back up the same road.** *Barnabas took Mark, and sailed unto Cyprus.* A light duty in a safe place restores nothing; the second assignment must contain the thing that defeated him.
- **Tell the community to receive him.** *If he come unto you, receive him.* Without that instruction, the churches' memory functions as a permanent sentence.
- **Expect years, and do not demand a timetable.** Twenty years passed between Perga and Paul's request for him in prison.
- **And be prepared to revise your own verdict, publicly and in writing.** *He is profitable to me for the ministry* — from the man who had once refused to take him. If you have spoken against a man and he has changed, speak for him in the same place.

### For the young

- **Know where you will be tempted to leave.** It is at the point where the cost rises above the estimate and the way home is still open. That is Perga, and most men who leave do it there.
- **If you have already gone home, the road back is available and it is steep.** Expect to be sent up the same mountain, with the same conditions, and without a ceremony.
- **Let an older man attach himself to your future, and accept the humiliation of being helped.** Mark did not insist on his own standing; he travelled with Barnabas and then with Peter, and was received by churches on the strength of another man's letter.
- **Serve in obscurity long enough for the evidence to accumulate.** Mark's usefulness was eventually a matter of public record because it had been observed for two decades.
- **Do not confuse the machinery with the grace.** Demas travelled with the same companions and departed. Restoration is offered and is not automatic.
- **And record your own failure honestly if you ever have the opportunity to write.** The Gospel of Mark contains a story about a young man who fled into the night and left his linen cloth behind, and it is there because the author would not edit himself out of the record.

### Questions for reflection

1. Who in your care has failed by withdrawing from an assignment, and what have you actually said about him to other people?
2. Are you willing to be the advocate, with your own name attached, or do you prefer to keep the case at a professional distance?
3. What second assignment would take your young man back up the road where he turned around?
4. Whose memory in your congregation is still functioning as a verdict, and who needs to hear *receive him*?
5. Is there a judgement you made about a man ten years ago that the facts have since refuted?
