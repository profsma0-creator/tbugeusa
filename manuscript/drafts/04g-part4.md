## The Wisdom That Must Be Spilled Before the House Falls

This chapter closes Part IV, and it is where the argument of the previous six chapters becomes a single demand.

We have established that elders must speak, that silence is a transgression with a named consequence, that a father's word does the work of a transfer, that Eli's refusal to restrain destroyed a dynasty, that David prepared a temple he would never enter, and that Paul emptied everything he had into writing and successors before his execution.

And all of it rests on one fact about the world: **the house falls.**

Not always immediately, and rarely in the way the people inside it expect. But the judgement pronounced in Scripture on institutions, dynasties, nations and congregations is that they end, and the ending is frequently sudden, and the wisdom they possessed is lost at the moment of collapse unless it has been poured out beforehand.

This chapter takes its title from that fact. **Wisdom that is stored in a vessel and never poured out is destroyed when the vessel breaks.** And the vessel always breaks.

### The most chilling verse in Isaiah

The case study is Hezekiah, and the passage is Isaiah 39, and it contains a sentence which is, on any reading, one of the most disturbing in the prophets.

Hezekiah, king of Judah, had been healed of a terminal illness. The sign had been given, the shadow had gone back ten degrees on the dial, and he had received fifteen additional years. And then envoys arrived from Babylon:

*At that time Merodach-baladan, the son of Baladan, king of Babylon, sent letters and a present to Hezekiah: for he had heard that he had been sick, and was recovered. And Hezekiah was glad of them, and shewed them the house of his precious things, the silver, and the gold, and the spices, and the precious ointment, and all the house of his armour, and all that was found in his treasures: there was nothing in his house, nor in all his dominion, that Hezekiah shewed them not* (Isaiah 39:1–2).

**Read what he did.** He showed a foreign delegation everything he owned. The treasury, the armaments, the spices, the precious things — *there was nothing in his house, nor in all his dominion, that Hezekiah shewed them not.*

The text does not say he made a treaty with Babylon. It does not say he asked for an alliance or gave them gold. **It says he showed them what he had.** And the display was, in the ancient diplomatic world, a statement of capability — a man openly displaying his assets is a man inviting an assessment.

And then Isaiah comes with the question, and the question is a diagnostic tool:

*Then came Isaiah the prophet unto king Hezekiah, and said unto him, What said these men? and from whence came they unto thee? ... Then said he, What have they seen in thine house? And Hezekiah answered, All that is in mine house have they seen: there is nothing among my treasures that I have not shewed them* (Isaiah 39:3–4).

**The prophet does not begin with a rebuke. He begins by asking what was said and what was seen.** And the king answers honestly, and the honesty is the confession.

**And then the sentence:**

*Behold, the days come, that all that is in thine house, and that which thy fathers have laid up in store until this day, shall be carried to Babylon: nothing shall be left, saith the Lord. And of thy sons that shall issue from thee, which thou shalt beget, shall they take away; and they shall be eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon* (Isaiah 39:6–7).

Note the scope of the judgment:
- **Everything in his house** — the treasures he had just shown.
- **And that which thy fathers have laid up in store until this day** — the accumulated inheritance of the previous generations. **The judgment does not fall only on what Hezekiah had accumulated; it falls on the whole family estate.**
- **And of thy sons that shall issue from thee** — his own children, taken, and made servants in a foreign palace.

**The display of the assets in one generation resulted in the seizure of the assets and the enslavement of the next three generations.**

And then the verse this chapter is built on:

*Then said Hezekiah to Isaiah, Good is the word of the Lord which thou hast spoken. He said moreover, For there shall be peace and truth in my days* (Isaiah 39:8).

**Good is the word of the Lord.**

Hezekiah heard that his house would be emptied, that everything his fathers had stored would be carried away, and that his own sons would be made eunuchs in Babylon — and he replied that the word of the Lord was good, **because the destruction would not happen in his own lifetime.**

*For there shall be peace and truth in my days.*

**That sentence is the whole of the failure this book is written against, and it is spoken by a good king.**

And it must be said plainly because it is easy to distance oneself from it: **Hezekiah was one of the best kings of Judah.** He had destroyed the high places, broken the images, restored the temple worship, kept the Passover, and received a miraculous deliverance from the Assyrian army in a single night (2 Kings 18–19). The words *there was no king like him* were said of him as well as of Josiah, in a different sense (2 Kings 18:5).

**And at the end of his life, he was content that the judgement would fall after he was dead.**

The text offers no commentary. It does not say that this was the worst thing he did, and it does not say it was trivial. It simply records the reply and proceeds to the next reign, where the envoys from Babylon come again and the house is emptied exactly as the prophet said.

**A generation's contentment with peace in its own days is the mechanism by which the next generation inherits nothing.**

### The command to spill

And now set the command of Scripture against that failure, because Scripture devotes a great deal of space to the requirement that wisdom be poured out rather than stored.

**Psalm 78 is the clearest statement, and it is a psalm about transmission from beginning to end.**

*Give ear, O my people, to my law: incline your ears to the words of my mouth. I will open my mouth in a parable: I will utter dark sayings of old: which we have heard and known, and our fathers have told us. We will not hide them from their children, shewing to the generation to come the praises of the Lord, and his strength, and his wonderful works that he hath done* (Psalm 78:1–4).

**Note the four-stage chain in that passage, and note that it is a chain of speech.**

**What the fathers told.** *Which we have heard and known, and our fathers have told us.* The psalmist's knowledge is received knowledge, and it arrived by telling.

**And what the psalmist will do: *we will not hide them from their children.***

The Hebrew verb is *kakhad* — to hide, to conceal, to cover over. And the psalmist's commitment is stated as a refusal to do it: **we will not hide.** That is, the default behaviour is concealment, and the righteous response is its deliberate opposite.

**And then the purpose, stated in four clauses:**

*For he established a testimony in Jacob, and appointed a law in Israel, which he commanded our fathers, that they should make them known to their children: that the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born; who should arise and declare them to their children: that they might set their hope in God, and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments* (Psalm 78:5–7).

**Five generations are visible in three verses.** The fathers who received the command; the fathers who are to make it known; the children to whom it is made known; the children yet to be born; and the generation that arises and declares it again.

**The purpose clauses are the substance of the whole doctrine:**
- *That the generation to come might know them* — the knowledge must reach them.
- *That they might set their hope in God* — knowledge produces hope.
- *And not forget the works of God* — knowledge is the antidote to forgetfulness.
- *But keep his commandments* — and knowledge produces obedience.

**And then the negative purpose, which is the sharpest: *And might not be as their fathers, a stubborn and rebellious generation; a generation that set not their heart aright, and whose spirit was not stedfast with God* (Psalm 78:8).**

**The whole psalm is written so that the next generation will not repeat the previous one's apostasy — and the psalm's own history of that apostasy is a record of failure that goes back to Egypt.**

**The transmission of knowledge is not a nicety in Psalm 78. It is the mechanism by which a nation is prevented from repeating its own disasters.**

### Asking for the old paths

And there is a corresponding duty on the receiving side, and it is stated in one of the most direct invitations in the prophets:

*Thus saith the Lord, Stand ye in the ways, and see, and ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein, and ye shall find rest for your souls. But they said, We will not walk therein* (Jeremiah 6:16).

**Break the instruction into its four verbs: stand, see, ask, walk.**

**Stand.** Stop moving. The Hebrew is *amad*, which in this construction means to take up a position and remain there.

**See.** Look. Examine the situation.

**Ask for the old paths.** The Hebrew is *shalu lintibhoth 'olam* — ask for the paths of old, the ancient roads. The word *nethibhah* denotes a trodden path, a track made by people walking.

**And walk therein.** The asking is not the end of the instruction. **You ask, and then you put your feet in the tracks.**

**And the promise: *ye shall find rest for your souls*.** The same phrase Christ will later use in Matthew 11:29.

**And the response, recorded by the prophet without comment: *But they said, We will not walk therein.***

The refusal is not a failure to understand. It is a refusal to walk — a decision against the direction, taken by people who have been shown the road.

**And note who is addressed.** The passage is spoken to a nation at a crisis point, and the invitation is to consult a tradition. The old paths are not the old institutions; they are the old tracks that the feet of the generations have worn into the ground. **They are the accumulated evidence about how to live, and the generation in question was told to look for them and would not.**

### Two texts on the value of the past

And the wisdom literature adds two statements about why the past must be consulted, and both of them are stated as reasons rather than as sentiments.

**Job 8, in the mouth of Bildad — who is wrong about Job and right about method:**

*For enquire, I pray thee, of the former age, and prepare thyself to the search of their fathers: (for we are but of yesterday, and know nothing, because our days upon earth are a shadow:) shall not they teach thee, and tell thee, and utter words out of their heart?* (Job 8:8–10).

**The argument is an argument from brevity.** *We are but of yesterday, and know nothing, because our days upon earth are a shadow.* Bildad's case for consulting the fathers is not that they were wiser, but that **they had more time.**

**That is the entire case for the tradition, and it does not require the fathers to be infallible or the past to be golden.** A living person has, at most, a few decades of observation. The tradition has centuries. To refuse the accumulated record of a hundred generations in favour of one's own forty years is not a matter of principle; it is a matter of arithmetic.

**And Deuteronomy 32:7, in the mouth of Moses:**

*Remember the days of old, consider the years of many generations: ask thy father, and he will shew thee; thy elders, and they will tell thee.*

**Four imperatives, and their objects are precise.** *Remember the days of old* — the distant past, the acts of God in the exodus. *Consider the years of many generations* — the pattern across time. *Ask thy father* — the immediate lineage. *And thy elders* — the wider community of the aged.

**The sequence is deliberate.** It begins with the great acts of God in the remote past and narrows to the personal instruction of one's own father. **Both are required.** The grand narrative does not replace the family conversation, and the family conversation is not sufficient without the grand narrative.

### The four-generation sentence in Joel

And there is one verse in the minor prophets that states the mechanism in the most compressed form in Scripture, and it is placed in the middle of a description of total agricultural and national catastrophe.

*Hear this, ye old men, and give ear, all ye inhabitants of the land. Hath this been in your days, or even in the days of your fathers? Tell ye your children of it, and let your children tell their children, and their children another generation* (Joel 1:2–3).

**Four generations in one sentence.** The old men who are addressed; their children, whom they are to tell; those children's children; and *another generation*.

**And note the framing.** The command to transmit is given in a moment of disaster. The locusts have eaten everything, the fields are wasted, the offerings have ceased, and the prophet's first instruction is: **make sure this is remembered.**

**That is the instinct of a believing generation in a catastrophe, and its opposite is the instinct of Hezekiah.** A devastated people is commanded to tell the story of the devastation, so that a later generation will know what happened and how it came about.

**And the deeper point is that the command to transmit is addressed to *the old men* first.** *Hear this, ye old men.* The aged are the ones who must speak, and the occasion for their speech is the disaster they have lived through.

**The worst thing that happens to your generation is the most important thing your heirs need to know, and the only route by which they can know it is your telling.**

### Wisdom crying in the streets

And then Proverbs 1, where wisdom is personified and where the consequence of refusing her invitation is stated in terms that have no softening.

*Wisdom crieth without; she uttereth her voice in the streets: she crieth in the chief place of concourse, in the openings of the gates: in the city she uttereth her words, saying, How long, ye simple ones, will ye love simplicity? and the scorners delight in their scorning, and fools hate knowledge?* (Proverbs 1:20–22).

**Note where wisdom speaks.** *Without*, that is, outside; in the streets; in the busiest place; at the gate where the courts and the markets are. **Not in the temple, not in the school, not in the council chamber. In the street and the marketplace, in public.**

**Wisdom is not scarce in Scripture. She is loud, public and unmissable. The problem is never that wisdom was unavailable.**

And the offer:

*Turn you at my reproof: behold, I will pour out my spirit unto you, I will make known my words unto you* (Proverbs 1:23).

**And then the refusal, and the consequence.**

*Because I have called, and ye refused; I have stretched out my hand, and no man regarded; but ye have set at nought all my counsel, and would none of my reproof: I also will laugh at your calamity; I will mock when your fear cometh; when your fear cometh as desolation, and your destruction cometh as a whirlwind; when distress and anguish cometh upon you. Then shall they call upon me, but I will not answer; they shall seek me early, but they shall not find me* (Proverbs 1:24–28).

**This is one of the hardest passages in the wisdom literature, and it is the counterpart of Joel.**

**Wisdom speaks first, publicly, at length, and repeatedly.** Then there is a point at which the speaking stops, and at that point the fear comes as a whirlwind and the calling goes unanswered.

**The doctrine is not that God is unwilling to answer. It is that there is a season for listening and it can be exhausted.** And the reason we know this is that the wisdom of Proverbs 1 is not a private mystical experience; **it is public teaching that anyone could have heard.**

**The house fell on people who had been told exactly how it would fall.**

### What must be spilled, and how

And now the constructive statement, because the chapter is a command rather than a lament.

There are five things that a generation possesses and that must be poured out before the collapse, and each one is a thing that cannot be reconstructed afterwards.

**First: the acts of God that you personally witnessed.** Not the theology of providence in the abstract, but the specific things God did in your lifetime — the prayers answered, the deliverances granted, the judgements executed, the guidance given. *I have been young, and now am old; yet have I not seen the righteous forsaken* (Psalm 37:25). **This class of knowledge cannot be obtained by any other means, and it dies with the eyewitness.**

**Second: the pattern of failure and its consequences.** Proverbs 1 and Psalm 78 are both records of apostasy preserved for the instruction of later generations. **The catastrophes must be told**, and Joel 1:3 makes the telling of them a standing command in the middle of one.

**Third: the structure of the doctrine.** Paul's *form of sound words* — the shape of the teaching rather than the items. A list of beliefs can be transmitted by a book; a structure has to be taught by someone who holds it.

**Fourth: the identity and the calling of the people.** Who they are, where they came from, what they have been given, what is required of them. *But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people* (1 Peter 2:9). **A generation that does not know what it is will become whatever it is told to be.**

**And fifth: the names and the record.** Who did what, who paid for it, who failed and was forgiven, who is owed a debt of gratitude. Scripture is full of genealogies and lists of names for this reason. **A community that forgets its names has no ancestors, and a community with no ancestors has no obligation.**

**And the mechanisms by which these are poured out are the ones we have seen throughout this part of the book: writing, ritual, the table, the public reading, the charge, the blessing, and the letter.**

**And the essential characteristic of all of them is that they are wasteful.** Nothing is retained. Everything is given away. Writing a document you will not need, performing a ceremony you will not benefit from, telling a story to people who will outlive you — **spilling is the only accurate word for it.**

### What is stored instead

And we should be honest about the alternative, because the failure is more common than the success and it looks respectable from the inside.

**A generation stores its wisdom when it keeps it as a private possession.** The man who has learned something and never writes it; the elder who carries the institutional memory and has never once told anyone the story; the mother whose knowledge of the family's history is in her head and nowhere else.

**And the storage is usually not deliberate.** It is the ordinary consequence of having no method. Nobody decides that their grandchildren will have to reconstruct them from nothing. It simply happens, because it is always easier to know a thing than to part with it in a transferable form.

**And the cost is paid by the next generation, and by every generation after.** Hezekiah's reply ends the chapter in Isaiah and the next chapter begins with the envoys arriving again, and within a century and a half the treasures he had displayed were in Babylon and the sons of his line were serving in the palace of a foreign king.

**He had peace and truth in his days. And the house was emptied.**

### Principles

**First: the default is loss, and transmission requires deliberate action.** The psalmist's wording is decisive: *we will not hide them from their children.* Hiding is what happens unless a decision is made.

**Second: the acts of God must be told, or they will be forgotten within three generations.** Psalm 78 traces the cycle: the fathers saw the works, the next generation did not know them, and a third generation came that *set not their heart aright*.

**Third: the record of failure is as valuable as the record of success, and Scripture gives proportion to it.** The historical books are largely a catalogue of apostasy, and the psalmist uses them as the material for his instruction.

**Fourth: brevity is the argument for tradition, not superiority.** *We are but of yesterday, and know nothing.* A living person has decades of experience; the tradition has centuries.

**Fifth: the season for listening can be exhausted.** Proverbs 1 does not describe a God who is unwilling to answer. It describes people who were called and refused, and who later called and were not answered.

**Sixth: the alternative to pouring out is display.** Hezekiah showed the Babylonians everything he had instead of giving his own people anything. **A generation that can produce a showcase but not a curriculum has already handed the inheritance away.**

**And seventh, which is the thesis of this chapter: the house falls, and the wisdom must be out of it before it does.** Not because the falling is certain within your lifetime — Hezekiah's own days were peaceful, exactly as he had been promised. **But because the judgement on the house is a judgement on the contents of the house, and the only way to save the contents is to carry them out.**

**Moses wrote the book and put it inside the ark. David wrote the plans and put them in his son's hands. Paul wrote the letters and told the churches to copy them. Hezekiah showed off the treasury and kept his knowledge and lost both.**

**The difference between those four men is not their quality. Three of them sinned substantially and publicly. The difference is that three of them spilled what they had and one of them stored it.**

### The rituals that exist to make children ask

And there is one further mechanism in Scripture for the spilling of wisdom, and it is the most elegant thing in the whole design. **God built a series of rituals whose entire purpose is to provoke a question from the next generation.**

There are four of these passages, and they share a formula that is worth recognising.

**The stones at Gilgal.**

*And he spake unto the children of Israel, saying, When your children shall ask their fathers in time to come, saying, What mean these stones? Then ye shall let your children know, saying, Israel came over this Jordan on dry land* (Joshua 4:21–22).

Twelve stones were taken out of the bed of the Jordan and set up on the bank. They had no function. They were not an altar, a memorial building, or a marker of a boundary. **They were a pile of stones whose only purpose was to be sufficiently inexplicable that a child would have to ask about them.**

**The Passover.**

*And it shall come to pass, when your children shall say unto you, What mean ye by this service? That ye shall say, It is the sacrifice of the Lord's passover, who passed over the houses of the children of Israel in Egypt, when he smote the Egyptians, and delivered our houses* (Exodus 12:26–27).

**The redemption of the firstborn.**

*And it shall be when thy son asketh thee in time to come, saying, What is this? that thou shalt say unto him, By strength of hand the Lord brought us out from Egypt, from the house of bondage* (Exodus 13:14).

**And the statutes themselves.**

*And when thy son asketh thee in time to come, saying, What mean the testimonies, and the statutes, and the judgments, which the Lord our God hath commanded you? Then thou shalt say unto thy son, We were Pharaoh's bondmen in Egypt; and the Lord brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand* (Deuteronomy 6:20–21).

**Now notice the structure that all four share, because it is a piece of deliberate design.**

**First, the ritual is instituted at the moment of the event it commemorates.** The stones were set up on the day of the crossing. The Passover was instituted on the night of the exodus. **The older generation built the memory device while the memory was still fresh, knowing that the freshness would not last.**

**Second, the ritual is designed to be unintelligible to someone who was not there.** A pile of stones by a river, a lamb roasted whole with bitter herbs, a redeemed firstborn — none of these makes sense to a child. **They are questions waiting to be asked.**

**Third, the child asks the father.** *When your children shall ask their fathers.* The instruction is addressed to the household, and the answer is expected to come from the head of it.

**And fourth, the answer is a narrative, not an explanation.** Look at what the father is told to say. Not *this is a symbol of the covenant.* Not *the stones represent the twelve tribes.* **He is told to say: Israel came over this Jordan on dry land. We were Pharaoh's bondmen in Egypt, and the Lord brought us out with a mighty hand.**

**The answer to the child's question is a story about what God did.**

**This is the single most effective pedagogical design in the history of the world, and its logic is that the older generation is required to say things it would not otherwise have volunteered.** The child's question compels the answer. The ritual guarantees the question. And the narrative ensures the answer carries the event rather than a theory about the event.

**And note the last purpose clause in the Gilgal passage: *That all the people of the earth might know the hand of the Lord, that it is mighty: that ye might fear the Lord your God for ever* (Joshua 4:24).**

**The stones were for the nations and for the children at the same time.** A community with visible, inexplicable, unexplained and explained-on-request markers is a community that keeps its own story alive — and it keeps the story alive by making the next generation ask.

**A generation that wants its wisdom to survive should build things children will ask about, and then answer them properly when they do.**

### For the fathers

- **Write the acts of God in your lifetime.** Not a devotional reflection — a record. What did God do, when, through whom, with what result? Date it and name the people.
- **Write the failures too.** The generation that inherits only your victories will not be warned by your failures. Scripture's own history is mostly a record of what went wrong.
- **Do not confuse display with transfer.** Hezekiah showed the Babylonians the treasury. Ask what you have shown to people who admire you, and what you have given to the people who will inherit from you.
- **Answer the question Hezekiah did not ask.** When you are told the judgement will fall after your death, what is your response? *Peace in my days* is the cheerful version of *after me, the deluge*, and it is the same decision.
- **Give the inheritance to your children's children, not merely your children.** Proverbs 13:22 measures a good man by the second generation, and the mechanism is that the first generation made the transfer.

### For the young

- **Ask for the old paths.** *Stand, see, ask, walk.* The asking is commanded, and it is your responsibility, not only theirs. Most silent elders will answer a direct question.
- **Ask for the disasters first.** The stories of what went wrong are the ones that are hardest to tell and the ones you most need. *Hath this been in your days, or even in the days of your fathers?*
- **Write down what you are told.** The word from an elder is a one-time transfer. Keep the record. You are the third link in Joel's chain, and the fourth link depends on you.
- **Do not despise the paths because the path-makers were flawed.** Hezekiah had peace and truth in his days; his fathers had laid up treasure and he showed it to Babylon. Your inheritance may come from compromised hands and still be worth having.
- **And then walk therein.** The instruction in Jeremiah 6:16 does not end at asking. Wisdom that is admired and not followed is not wisdom received; it is a museum.

### Questions for reflection

1. What do you know about God's dealings in your family or your institution that exists nowhere in writing?
2. If the house you belong to fell tomorrow, what would be lost permanently?
3. Who is the last living person who can tell you the story, and how long do you have?
4. Where have you substituted showing the treasury for spilling the wisdom?
5. When you were told the consequence would fall after your death, what did you say?
