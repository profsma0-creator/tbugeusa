## Paul and Timothy: The Apostle Who Refused to Die With His Knowledge

The last letter Paul wrote is addressed to a man thirty years younger than himself, and it was written from a dungeon in Rome with the author's execution approaching. And the letter is not a farewell. It is a **transfer.**

*I charge thee therefore before God, and the Lord Jesus Christ, who shall judge the quick and the dead at his appearing and his kingdom; preach the word; be instant in season, out of season; reprove, rebuke, exhort with all longsuffering and doctrine* (2 Timothy 4:1–2).

That is not the tone of a man winding down. It is the tone of a man emptying the last of his authority into the hands of his successor while he still has breath to do it.

This chapter is about the content of that transfer: what Paul gave away, how he gave it away, and why so few men of comparable stature have done the same.

### The three perfect tenses

Begin with the sentences that stand at the end of the letter, because they establish the footing on which everything else rests.

*For I am now ready to be offered, and the time of my departure is at hand. I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith: henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, shall give me at that day: and not to me only, but unto all them also that love his appearing* (2 Timothy 4:6–8).

**I am now ready to be offered.** The Greek is *spendomai* — I am being poured out as a drink offering. The word belongs to the sacrificial system: the libation of wine poured around the base of the altar, the last act of a sacrifice, the part that is neither eaten nor burned but simply poured away.

Paul describes his death as a libation. **The image is of something being emptied out at the end of a ritual, and the ritual is his life.**

**And the time of my departure is at hand.** The Greek is *analysis* — the noun from which English takes *analysis*, but in the first century it was used of the loosing of a ship from its moorings, or the striking of a tent. Both images are of separation rather than of termination.

**Then three perfect tenses, and the perfect tense in Greek describes a completed action with a permanent result.**

**I have fought a good fight.** The verb is *egonismai*, from *agon*, the athletic contest. Paul has completed the contest, and the result stands.

**I have finished my course.** The verb is *teteleka*, and it is the same verb Christ uses on the cross: *It is finished* (John 19:30). The word means to bring to a conclusion, to accomplish a purpose, to complete. **Paul claims to have finished the course, which means he knew what the course was.**

**I have kept the faith.** The verb is *tetereka*, to guard, to preserve, to keep watch over. And the object is *ten pistin* — the faith, the body of belief delivered once for all.

**Three claims: the contest fought, the course completed, the deposit guarded.** And a man who can say those three things at the end of his life has done everything that can be done.

**And then note what he does not say.** He does not say that he finished the work. The work was not finishable. He says he finished his *course* — *ton dromon*, the race specifically assigned to him.

**The distinction is essential for this whole book.** The purpose of God was not completed by Paul, and Paul did not regard that as a failure. He completed his course; the purpose continues; and the transfer of the deposit to the next runner is the way the purpose proceeds.

### The four deposits

And now the content. Paul names what he is handing over in four separate formulations, and the precision of the vocabulary shows that he thought about this deliberately.

**The first deposit is the good thing committed to trust.** *That good thing which was committed unto thee keep by the Holy Ghost which dwelleth in us* (2 Timothy 1:14). And again in 1 Timothy 6:20: *O Timothy, keep that which is committed to thy trust.*

The Greek noun is *paratheke*, and it means a deposit — something placed with another for safekeeping, an entrustment. It is the language of a banking arrangement or of a will lodged with a third party. The word appears in the New Testament only in the pastoral letters, and it is the technical term Paul uses for the gospel as a *thing stored with a custodian*.

And note the tension inside the sentence, because it is deliberate: **it is a deposit that must be kept, and the keeping is done by the Holy Spirit.** *Keep by the Holy Ghost which dwelleth in us.* The human custodian is responsible, and the divine custodian is the one preserving. Paul also names the other side of the same relationship a few verses earlier: *I am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him against that day* (2 Timothy 1:12).

**Two deposits in two directions.** Paul has deposited his own life with God; Timothy has received the gospel from Paul. Each custody is real and each is required.

**The second deposit is the pattern of sound words.** *Hold fast the form of sound words, which thou hast heard of me, in faith and love which is in Christ Jesus* (2 Timothy 1:13).

The Greek noun is *hypotyposis* — the outline, the sketch, the preliminary model, the pattern from which a full drawing will be made. It is the word for an architect's plan or an artist's study.

**Paul is telling Timothy that there is a *shape* to the teaching, and that the shape must be retained.** Not merely the individual doctrines, but their arrangement, their relative weight, their architecture.

**This is the difference between a list of beliefs and a theology.** A list can be altered by adding and subtracting; a structure has to be understood before it can be preserved. Paul hands over a structure, and the instruction is to hold it fast — the Greek is *eche*, keep, hold, possess, with a continuous force.

**And note the qualification: *in faith and love which is in Christ Jesus*.** The form of sound words is to be held inside faith and love. A man who holds the outline without those two things has the anatomy of a body without the bloodstream, which is the recurring error of the doctrinaire.

**The third deposit is the public teaching.** *And the things that thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also* (2 Timothy 2:2).

We have examined this verse in an earlier chapter for its four-link chain. What matters here is the phrase *among many witnesses*, which Paul uses to establish a fact about the mode of transmission.

**The teaching Timothy received was not given to him privately.** The Greek is *dia pollon martyron* — through many witnesses, or in the presence of many witnesses. Paul's instruction to Timothy was delivered publicly, in the churches, before congregations.

Which means that **Timothy's recollection of the doctrine can be checked.** If the deposit were passed in private from one man to one man, it would be unverifiable, and a tradition that cannot be checked is a tradition that can be quietly altered. Because it was given *among many witnesses*, there is a community that can testify to what was said.

**The fourth deposit is the Scripture itself.** And here we reach the mechanism that made the whole transfer permanent:

*And that from a child thou hast known the holy scriptures, which are able to make thee wise unto salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus. All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works* (2 Timothy 3:15–17).

Set out the four uses Paul attributes to Scripture, because they are four categories of ministry, and they are exhaustive.

**Doctrine** — *didaskalia*, the positive statement of what is true.
**Reproof** — *elegmos*, the conviction of what is false, the establishing of guilt.
**Correction** — *epanorthosis*, the setting straight of what is crooked, the restoring to a right position.
**Instruction in righteousness** — *paideia he en dikaiosune*, the whole formation in right conduct.

**And the purpose clause: *that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.***

The Greek for *perfect* is *artios* — complete, fitted, sound. And for *throughly furnished* is *exertismenos*, fully equipped, from a verb used of fitting out a ship or furnishing a house.

**Scripture is presented here not as a source of devotional material but as the equipment of a person for a task.** And it is written — which is what makes it transferable across generations.

**This is the answer to the problem Paul was facing.** He was about to die. Timothy would not have him in the room. And the doctrine would survive the death of the apostle because it had been written, and because there was a man who had known it from childhood and a public body of witnesses who had heard it taught.

**Paul's whole succession strategy can be summarised in one sentence: he refused to be the sole custodian of anything.**

### He made himself the pattern

And there is a further element of the transfer, which is not a deposit but a method, and which appears in Paul's letters with a frequency that is sometimes uncomfortable to modern readers.

*Be ye followers of me, even as I also am of Christ* (1 Corinthians 11:1).

*Brethren, be followers together of me, and mark them which walk so as ye have us for an ensample* (Philippians 3:17).

*For yourselves know how ye ought to follow us: for we behaved not ourselves disorderly among you; neither did we eat any man's bread for nought; but wrought with labour and travail night and day, that we might not be chargeable to any of you: not because we have not power, but to make ourselves an ensample unto you to follow us* (2 Thessalonians 3:7–9).

**The word *ensample* is *typos*, the same word Paul uses in 1 Timothy 4:12 when he tells Timothy to become a pattern.** And in 1 Corinthians 4:16: *Wherefore I beseech you, be ye followers of me.*

**Paul presented his own life as the curriculum.** He did not merely teach; he invited inspection of himself, and he expected imitation.

And in the Thessalonian passage he is precise about what he was offering for imitation, because it is not his spiritual experiences. It is his **labour** — *we wrought with labour and travail night and day, that we might not be chargeable to any of you.* The pattern he points to is a pattern of work and refusal to be a burden.

**And he gives the reason he had the right to do this: *not because we have not power*.** He was entitled to support (1 Corinthians 9:7–14). He surrendered the entitlement *to make ourselves an ensample*.

**That is the transfer mechanism.** A doctrine can be written down; a manner of life cannot. It has to be watched. And a man who wants his knowledge to survive him must let the people who will carry it see how he works.

**Which is why the older generation's most valuable asset is not its conclusions but its habits.**

### The council at Miletus

And the fullest account of Paul handing over is in Acts 20, and it is worth examining as a ceremony.

He is at Miletus, on his way to Jerusalem, and he sends to Ephesus for the elders of the church. They come. And he delivers a speech which is, in effect, his **paratheke** in spoken form.

*Ye know, from the first day that I came into Asia, after what manner I have been with you at all seasons, serving the Lord with all humility of mind, and with many tears, and temptations, which befell me by the lying in wait of the Jews* (Acts 20:18–19).

**He begins with his conduct.** *Ye know* — the appeal is to what they have observed. Not to his doctrine, not to his results, not to his apostolic authority. *Ye know how I lived.*

**And then the four things he refuses to have withheld:**

*And how I kept back nothing that was profitable unto you, but have shewed you, and have taught you publickly, and from house to house* (Acts 20:20).

**Kept back nothing.** The Greek is *hypesteilamen*, the same verb he uses in verse 27 of the counsel of God. *Nothing that was profitable.* And the two methods: *publickly, and from house to house.* Paul taught in the assembly and in the home, and the two are not interchangeable.

**And then the content of his testimony:**

*Testifying both to the Jews, and also to the Greeks, repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ* (Acts 20:21).

**Then his own indifference to his life:**

*But none of these things move me, neither count I my life dear unto myself, so that I might finish my course with joy, and the ministry, which I have received of the Lord Jesus, to testify the gospel of the grace of God* (Acts 20:24).

**And then the transfer itself:**

*And now, behold, I know that ye all, among whom I have gone preaching the kingdom of God, shall see my face no more. Wherefore I take you to record this day, that I am pure from the blood of all men. For I have not shunned to declare unto you all the counsel of God. Take heed therefore unto yourselves, and to all the flock, over the which the Holy Ghost hath made you overseers, to feed the church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood* (Acts 20:25–28).

**Four moves, and they are the architecture of a handover.**

**He states the fact of his departure.** *Ye shall see my face no more.* No ambiguity, no arrangement for an occasional visit, no honorary role.

**He discharges himself of the watchman's liability.** *I am pure from the blood of all men, for I have not shunned to declare unto you all the counsel of God.*

**He hands the responsibility to them.** *Take heed therefore unto yourselves, and to all the flock, over the which the Holy Ghost hath made you overseers.* The *therefore* is doing work: because I have not withheld anything, the accountability now passes to you.

**And he warns them about what is coming.**

*For I know this, that after my departing shall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock. Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them* (Acts 20:29–30).

**The warning is remarkable for stating that the danger will come from inside.** Not only from outside the church but *of your own selves*. He names the internal threat to the men he is appointing, at the moment he appoints them.

**And then he hands over the only resource they have:**

*And now, brethren, I commend you to God, and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up, and to give you an inheritance among all them which are sanctified* (Acts 20:32).

**And finally the pattern, stated in the practical terms of money:**

*I have coveted no man's silver, or gold, or apparel. Yea, ye yourselves know, that these hands have ministered unto my necessities, and to them that were with me. I have shewed you all things, how that so labouring ye ought to support the weak, and to remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he said, It is more blessed to give than to receive* (Acts 20:33–35).

*These hands.* Paul shows them his hands, and the marks on them are the evidence of a pattern they can imitate.

**And then:**

*And when he had thus spoken, he kneeled down, and prayed with them all. And they all wept sore, and fell on Paul's neck, and kissed him, sorrowing most of all for the words which he spake, that they should see his face no more. And they accompanied him unto the ship* (Acts 20:36–38).

**He handed over, and they wept, and he got on the ship.**

**That is what a transfer looks like when it is done properly: the doctrine stated, the succession appointed, the warnings given, the pattern shown, the liability discharged, and then the ship.**

### Why men die with their knowledge

And now we must account for the failure that this chapter is written against, because it is far more common than the pattern.

Men of knowledge, insight and experience die every day with the substance of what they knew still in them, and the reason is almost never that they were unable to speak. It is that they declined to part with the thing in a form that could survive them.

There are four causes, and they are worth naming so that they can be recognised.

**The first is the identity that comes from being the custodian.** A man who knows something nobody else knows is a man whose standing rests on the possession. To hand it over is to become ordinary. And this operates below the level of conscious decision, which is what makes it so difficult to detect.

**The diagnostic question** is whether the man can describe what he knows in a form that would function in his absence, or whether his knowledge always requires him to be present to be useful. **A man whose knowledge cannot be separated from his person has built no inheritance.**

**The second is the fear of replacement.** *If I give them everything, there will be no need of me.* And the fear is accurate: that is exactly what will happen, and it is the point of the transfer. Moses was told to put some of his honour on Joshua, and Moses would not be needed to ratify it afterwards.

**The third is perfectionism about the successor.** *He is not ready. He does not understand. He has not grasped it.* Sometimes true, and always a way of deferring indefinitely, because no one is ever ready in the sense that would satisfy the argument. **Joshua spent forty years not being ready, and God said to appoint him during that time.**

**The fourth is plain sloth.** Writing is work. Compiling a curriculum is work. Preparing a structure of doctrine so that it can be taught is work, and it is the kind of work that yields nothing visible for years. **Most knowledge that dies with its owner was never written down because writing is harder than talking.**

**And these four causes are why the Scripture commands the transmission rather than merely recommending it.** *The fear of man bringeth a snare* (Proverbs 29:25), and the snare in this case is a lifetime of insight that ends up in a grave.

### What Paul did not do

And to make the contrast concrete, here are the four things that characterise Paul's behaviour in the last years of his life, and each one is an answer to one of the causes above.

**He wrote.** 2 Timothy, 1 Timothy, Titus, Philippians, Ephesians, Colossians — the letters of the imprisonment were written in the period when Paul knew he was finished. **The most productive writing period of his life was the one in which he had lost the ability to travel.**

**He named his successors publicly and repeatedly.** Timothy as co-author of six letters, Titus of Galatians, Timothy in the Philippian letter as the man without a likeness, and *mark them which walk so as ye have us for an ensample* (Philippians 3:17). Nobody had to guess who was to continue.

**He gave away his own authority in writing.** *Let no man therefore despise him* (1 Corinthians 16:11) — Paul's own apostolic standing placed at the disposal of a young man's acceptance.

**And he accepted being left.** *Demas hath forsaken me ... Only Luke is with me* (2 Timothy 4:10–11). And in the very next sentence he asks for Timothy, Mark, and the books. **He did not pretend that the abandonment had not happened, and he did not let it stop the work.**

### The last request

And the letter ends with a list of domestic details that have been the encouragement of every minister since.

*Do thy diligence to come shortly unto me* (2 Timothy 4:9).
*The cloke that I left at Troas with Carpus, when thou comest, bring with thee, and the books, but especially the parchments* (2 Timothy 4:13).
*At my first answer no man stood with me, but all men forsook me: I pray God that it may not be laid to their charge. Notwithstanding the Lord stood with me, and strengthened me; that by me the preaching might be fully known, and that all the Gentiles might hear: and I was delivered out of the mouth of the lion* (2 Timothy 4:16–17).

**A cloak, books and parchments.** The apostle under sentence of death, in a cold cell, asking his son in the faith to bring a coat and the manuscripts.

**And note the two most important things in that list.**

**First: *all men forsook me* — and he prayed that it might not be laid to their charge.** He did not spend his last letter settling scores. The man who handed over doctrine, authority and pattern also handed over the forgiveness of those who had abandoned him.

**Second: *the books, but especially the parchments*.** The Greek distinguishes *biblia* — books, probably scrolls of the Old Testament and possibly other works — from *membrana*, the parchment notebooks, which may well have been Paul's own copies of his letters, or his notes.

**The last recorded action of Paul's ministry is a request for reading material.** The man who had been caught up to the third heaven and heard unspeakable words (2 Corinthians 12:2–4) wanted his books in prison.

**And the reason is that he was still working.** There is nothing in the letter about retirement. The instruction to Timothy at the top of chapter 4 is *preach the word; be instant in season, out of season* — and Paul, facing execution, was asking for his notes.

### Principles

**First: the deposit is a deposit, and it can be handled as an object.** Paul used banking language. The gospel is the sort of thing that can be placed with a custodian, checked against witnesses, written down, and left in a vault. To treat it as a private experience is to ensure that it dies.

**Second: the shape matters as much as the contents.** *Hold fast the form of sound words.* A list can be amended; a structure has to be understood. The two errors — adding to the deposit and subtracting from it — are both errors about proportion rather than about individual items.

**Third: transmission must be public, or it cannot be verified.** *Among many witnesses.* A doctrine that passes privately from one man to one man is uncheckable, and what cannot be checked will be quietly altered within two generations.

**Fourth: the deposit must be written.** Scripture itself is the mechanism by which the apostolic generation became accessible to all the generations that followed. **Paul's entire correspondence exists because he did not trust a purely oral tradition to survive him.**

**Fifth: a man must present his life, not merely his teaching, as the pattern.** *Be ye followers of me.* The doctrine can be written; the manner of life must be seen. And the manner Paul points to is not his revelations but his work.

**Sixth: the handover is completed by departure.** *Ye shall see my face no more ... they accompanied him unto the ship.* A predecessor who remains available has not handed over the liability.

**Seventh: the one who hands over does not get to be the hero of the next generation's story.** Timothy becomes the bishop of Ephesus; Titus goes to Crete and Dalmatia; John Mark becomes, on the ancient testimony, the author of a Gospel; and Paul dies in Rome with a cloak, some books, and one companion.

**He was content with that, and he said so: *I have finished my course*.**

### The letters, and how they were meant to travel

There is one further element of Paul's transfer strategy that is easy to miss because it is embedded in the ordinary business of the letters, and it is the reason we have them.

Paul expected his letters to be read aloud publicly, and he said so explicitly:

*I charge you by the Lord that this epistle be read unto all the holy brethren* (1 Thessalonians 5:27).

*And when this epistle is read among you, cause that it be read also in the church of the Laodiceans; and that ye likewise read the epistle from Laodicea* (Colossians 4:16).

**Two commands, and the second is the more remarkable.** Paul instructs the Colossians to send his letter to Laodicea and to obtain the letter from Laodicea in exchange. **He is arranging for the circulation of his correspondence among the churches while he is still alive.**

Which means the letters were never private documents. They were addressed to congregations, intended to be read in the assembly, and expected to be copied and passed on. **Paul was consciously building a body of written instruction that would function in his absence.**

And the churches did pass them around. Peter, writing later, assumes that his readers have access to Paul's letters and treats them as a recognised body of material:

*And account that the longsuffering of our Lord is salvation; even as our beloved brother Paul also according to the wisdom given unto him hath written unto you; as also in all his epistles, speaking in them of these things; in which are some things hard to be understood, which they that are unlearned and unstable wrest, as they do also the other scriptures, unto their own destruction* (2 Peter 3:15–16).

**Note the last clause: *as they do also the other scriptures*.** Peter places Paul's epistles in the category of *the other scriptures* — meaning that by the time of Peter's writing, they were already being treated as Scripture, and were already being twisted by the unstable.

**So the mechanism worked, and it worked within one generation.** A man who spent his last years writing letters to churches, instructing them to read and circulate them, produced a body of writing that was being treated as authoritative Scripture before the generation that heard him had died.

**That is the most successful transfer of knowledge in the history of the world, and its methods were entirely ordinary: writing, public reading, circulation, and the naming of successors.**

### The young man who came back

And there is one more detail that must be recorded, because it is the most human thing in the chapter and it is a warning about writing people off.

John Mark was the young man who had set out with Paul and Barnabas on the first missionary journey and had turned back at Perga (Acts 13:13). And when Paul proposed a second journey, he refused to take him:

*And Barnabas determined to take with them John, whose surname was Mark. But Paul thought not good to take him with them, who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work. And the contention was so sharp between them, that they departed asunder one from the other* (Acts 15:37–39).

**The disagreement split the apostolic team.** Paul and Barnabas, who had worked together for years, separated over the question of whether a young man who had failed deserved a second opportunity. And the record does not adjudicate.

Now note the end. Twenty years later, in the last letter, writing from the dungeon:

*Only Luke is with me. Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry* (2 Timothy 4:11).

**Take Mark. He is useful to me.**

The man who had refused to take him is now asking for him by name, and the reason given is that he has proved useful. And the ancient testimony of the church is that this Mark wrote the second Gospel.

**Two lessons, and they belong in a chapter about transferring knowledge.**

**The first is that a failure is not a verdict.** Mark deserted at Perga, and Paul wrote him off, and Mark became essential. If Paul had been correct in his original assessment, we would not have his last request.

**The second is that Paul changed his mind, and put it in writing.** He does not explain the reversal or defend it. He simply includes the request in a letter that will be read by the churches for two thousand years, so that everybody knows that the man he had excluded is now the man he wants.

**A leader who refuses to be the sole custodian of the truth must also be a man who can revise his own judgments in public.**

### For the fathers

- **Take an inventory of what is in your head and not in any document.** Write the list. Then begin converting it, one item at a time, into a form that would work without you.
- **Distinguish teaching from transfer.** Teaching happens in a room with you in it. Transfer requires a structure: written material, a named successor, a public endorsement, and a liability handed over.
- **Name your successors in writing.** Paul named Timothy in six letters. Put the names in a document that will outlive you.
- **Write the letters.** The prison epistles are the most productive writing of Paul's life. Whatever season you are in, the document is the thing that survives.
- **Show your hands.** The pattern a successor needs is not your spiritual experiences but how you work, how you handle money, and how you respond to being abandoned.
- **Plan your departure.** Paul told the Ephesian elders he would not see them again so that they could prepare. Ambiguity about your leaving protects you and paralyses them.

### For the young

- **Guard the deposit, and do not improve it.** *Hold fast the form of sound words.* The temptation of each generation is to correct the proportions of the inheritance, and the correction is usually a loss.
- **Check the tradition against the witnesses.** The doctrine you received was given *among many witnesses*. Read the sources; do not accept a digest of a digest.
- **Read the books, and especially the parchments.** The single most practical thing in this chapter is that Paul asked for reading material at the end. An unread young man will be a shallow old one.
- **Receive a pattern, not just a syllabus.** You are being handed a way of working as well as a body of content, and the way of working is transmitted by observation.
- **Watch for what is not being given to you.** If a man's knowledge always requires his presence to be useful, that is worth noticing — and it is more often anxiety than malice.
- **Do not wait for the ideal inheritance.** Paul died with one companion, a cloak and a request for books. You may inherit from a man whose life was messier than his doctrine. Take the doctrine.

### Questions for reflection

1. What do you know that is not written down anywhere?
2. If you died this year, how many of your working assumptions would have to be rediscovered from scratch by the people who came after you?
3. Which of the four causes of dying with knowledge is most active in you — identity, fear of replacement, perfectionism about your successor, or sloth?
4. Who can currently verify that you teach what your teachers taught? Who are the *many witnesses* in your line?
5. What are the *books and the parchments* that are on your table at the moment?
