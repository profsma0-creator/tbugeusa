## Pentecost: When the Spirit Created a Multigenerational People

**The day of Pentecost is usually described in terms of what happened to individuals: the Spirit fell, and people were filled, and three thousand were converted.**

**And all of that is in the text. But the text is doing something larger, and the larger thing is the reason it matters to this book.**

**On that day God created a people, and He created them with a structure designed for duration: a doctrine, a table, a shared purse, a discipline, a government, an address book of fifteen nations, and children included in the promise by name.**

**And the sentence that makes the whole thing multigenerational is one clause in Peter's sermon, and it is the most under-used text on this subject in the New Testament.**

*For the promise is unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off, even as many as the Lord our God shall call* (Acts 2:39).

### Fifteen nations and one sound

**And the day began with a gathering that no human organiser had convened.**

*And there were dwelling at Jerusalem Jews, devout men, out of every nation under heaven. Now when this was noised abroad, the multitude came together, and were confounded, because that every man heard them speak in his own language. And they were all amazed and marvelled, saying one to another, Behold, are not all these which speak Galileans? And how hear we every man in our own tongue, wherein we were born? Parthians, and Medes, and Elamites, and the dwellers in Mesopotamia, and in Judea, and Cappadocia, in Pontus, and Asia, Phrygia, and Pamphylia, in Egypt, and in the parts of Libya about Cyrene, and strangers of Rome, Jews and proselytes, Cretes and Arabians, we do hear them speak in our tongues the wonderful works of God* (Acts 2:5–11).

**Count what Luke lists: fifteen regions, from Parthia in the east to Rome in the west, from Pontus in the north to Libya in the south.**

**And the amazement is not merely at the miracle of languages. It is at the content and the speakers.** *We do hear them speak in our tongues the wonderful works of God* — and the speakers were Galileans, **which to that audience meant men from a rural province with a distinctive accent, unschooled in the languages of the empire.**

**And the event is deliberately constructed as the reversal of an older one, and Luke's readers would have heard it immediately.**

*And the whole earth was of one language, and of one speech ... And they said, Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven; and let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth ... Go to, let us go down, and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another's speech. So the Lord scattered them abroad from thence upon the face of all the earth* (Genesis 11:1, 4, 7–8).

**At Babel, humanity was gathered for the purpose of making a name, and was scattered and confounded in language.**

**And at Pentecost, humanity was scattered across fifteen languages, and each heard the works of God in his own tongue, and three thousand became one body that same day.**

**Which means the church was constituted as a multilingual and multinational community from its first hour, and not as an assembly of people who happened to share a language and a culture.** And it means that the reversal of Babel in the New Testament is not a return to one language. **It is the creation of a community in which many languages praise God together without anyone being required to stop being what he was born.**

**And note the reaction in the crowd, because the new community was not welcomed by everyone.**

*Others mocking said, These men are full of new wine* (Acts 2:13).

**The same event, in the same street, produced astonishment in some and contempt in others.** Which is the normal condition of the church in every generation, **and which is why the young man who is looking for a moment when nobody will mock his community is looking for something the apostles never had.**

### "The promise is unto you, and to your children"

**And now the sentence. It comes at the end of Peter's answer to the crowd's question, and it is the most important statement in the book of Acts about the shape of the Christian community.**

**The crowd, having heard the sermon, asked *what shall we do?* And Peter's answer is repentance, baptism, and the gift of the Spirit, and then the ground of it:**

*For the promise is unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off, even as many as the Lord our God shall call* (Acts 2:39).

**Three addressees, in three expanding circles.**

**The first is *you*.** The men standing in front of him, many of whom had participated in the crucifixion by their presence and their endorsement of it. **The promise reached the guilty first.**

**The second is *your children*.**

**That is the clause this chapter exists to examine.** The promise of the Spirit is announced as belonging to the children of the men who were being addressed, **and it is announced in the same sentence as the promise to the adults.**

**The third is *all that are afar off* — the Gentiles, geographically and covenantally distant — *even as many as the Lord our God shall call.***

**And the whole structure tells us what kind of community the Spirit created.** It is not a community of converts who joined at a particular moment. **It is a household community, in which the promise is held by an adult and is expressly stated to belong to the children as well** — a covenant arrangement, extended outward to the nations.

**And the practical consequence in the New Testament is household conversion, recorded repeatedly.** Lydia and her household (Acts 16:15). The Philippian jailer, of whom Paul said, *believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved, and thy house* (Acts 16:31), and who was baptised, *he and all his, straightway* (16:33). Crispus the ruler of the synagogue, who *believed on the Lord with all his house* (Acts 18:8). **And the household of Stephanas, whom Paul calls *the firstfruits of Achaia* (1 Corinthians 16:15).**

**Which means that the Spirit's method of creating a multigenerational community was to convert adults and to include their children in the promise.** The church's first century growth came substantially through households, **and the households contained the next generation.**

### The congregation that received the Spirit

**And before the day itself, the text tells us who was in the room, and the composition of that prayer meeting is itself a statement about the community being born.**

*These all continued with one accord in prayer and supplication, with the women, and Mary the mother of Jesus, and with his brethren* (Acts 1:14).

**Four categories are named.**

**The apostles** — the eleven who remained, named in the previous verses.

**The women** — the women who had followed the Lord from Galilee and had ministered to Him out of their substance (Luke 8:2–3), and who had stayed at the cross when the men had fled, and who had gone to the tomb at dawn.

**Mary the mother of Jesus** — a mother in the room where the church was being constituted, present in prayer with the men who would lead it.

**And the brethren of the Lord** — the half-brothers of Jesus, of whom John records that *neither did his brethren believe in him* during the ministry (John 7:5).

**So the assembly that the Spirit filled contained the leading men, the women who had supported the work, the mother of the Lord, and a group of brothers who had been unbelievers within the Lord's own lifetime and were converted after the resurrection.**

**And the total was about a hundred and twenty (Acts 1:15).**

**Which means that the church received the Holy Spirit as a family assembly, and not as a clerical body.** A prophetess's daughters, a widow's house, a mother's prayer, a family's late conversion — all of it was in the room when the wind came.

**And the detail about the Lord's brothers is worth holding on to by any older man who has given up on a member of his own household.** They had grown up with Him, they had seen the miracles within their own village, and they had not believed. **And they were in the upper room, praying, six weeks after the crucifixion — and one of them, James, became the presiding elder of the church in Jerusalem and the author of an epistle.**

**A household can be converted late, and it can be converted completely.** And the mechanism is not argument within the family; it is a community of one accord continuing in prayer, into which a family is brought.

### The four marks of the community the Spirit made

**And the immediate description of the first community is given in one verse, and it is a summary of its life, and every element of it is a commitment rather than an experience.**

*And they continued stedfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of bread, and in prayers* (Acts 2:42).

**Four things, and the verb at the head governs all four: they *continued stedfastly*.** The Greek is *esan proskarterountes*, they were persisting, they were attending constantly, they were devoting themselves.

**The community was not defined by a moment. It was defined by a persistence in four activities, and the four are worth naming one at a time.**

**The apostles' doctrine.** *Didache*. **A body of teaching, transmitted from the men who had been with Jesus, and treated as authoritative.** This is the deposit of this book, and it was the first mark of the church.

**And fellowship.** *Koinonia*, the sharing of a common life, which in the next chapter's terms includes the sharing of property. **Not a social club, and not a friendly atmosphere, but a communion — a putting of one's goods and one's life at the disposal of others.**

**And the breaking of bread.** A phrase which in Luke's usage covers both the ordinary meal and the Lord's Supper, and which the community practised *from house to house* (2:46), **which is to say that the ordinance was celebrated in homes, which is where children were.**

**And prayers.** *Tais proseuchais*, plural, the Jewish hours of prayer, which the apostles continued to observe at the temple (3:1).

**What is not in the list is worth noticing.** There is no mention of a building, a hierarchy beyond the apostles, a liturgy, a budget, or a programme. **And there are also no age-segregated activities recorded anywhere in the first generation. The community's four commitments were conducted in a manner in which an entire household could participate.**

**And the effects are listed in the five verses that follow, and they describe a community that people wanted to belong to.**

*And fear came upon every soul: and many wonders and signs were done by the apostles. And all that believed were together, and had all things common; and sold their possessions and goods, and parted them to all men, as every man had need. And they, continuing daily with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house, did eat their meat with gladness and singleness of heart, praising God, and having favour with all the people. And the Lord added to the church daily such as should be saved* (Acts 2:43–47).

**And the description is repeated once more, in the same terms and with more intensity, after the first persecution:**

*And the multitude of them that believed were of one heart and of one soul: neither said any of them that ought of the things which he possessed was his own; but they had all things common ... Neither was there any among them that lacked* (Acts 4:32, 34).

**One heart and one soul, and nobody lacking.**

**Which is the answer to the question of what makes a multigenerational community possible. It is not a programme for youth. It is a community in which the older members have placed their possessions at the disposal of the younger, and the younger have placed their energy at the disposal of the older, and neither regards his own life as his own.**

### The vocabulary of togetherness

**And Luke uses two words with such frequency in the early chapters of Acts that they define the community, and both of them are worth knowing.**

**The first is *epi to auto*, which appears repeatedly and is translated *together*.**

*All that believed were together* (2:44). *They continued daily with one accord in the temple* (2:46) — and the phrase behind *with one accord* in that verse is again a form of unity of purpose. *They were all with one accord in one place* (2:1). *The multitude of them that believed* (4:32).

**The Greek phrase literally means *upon the same* — at the same place, or on the same thing.** And Luke uses it of the community's location, its prayer, and its purpose.

**The second is *homothumadon*, one accord, and it occurs in Acts 1:14 (praying), 2:46 (daily in the temple), 4:24 (lifting up their voice to God), 5:12 (in Solomon's porch), 15:25 (the decision of the council).**

**The word means with one passion, with one impulse, with the same mind set on the same object.** And note that it occurs in the account of the council's decision in Acts 15:25 — *it seemed good unto us, being assembled with one accord* — **which tells us the word describes a unity achieved through argument and not a unity that avoided it.**

**And the practical import for our subject is in the physical arrangement, which Luke records precisely: *continuing daily with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house.***

**The community was simultaneously centralised and distributed.** It met publicly in the temple courts, where it could be seen and where it could grow; **and it met house by house, where an entire family could gather around a table and hear the apostles' doctrine, and where the children of the community were present.**

**Which is the structure most conducive to generational transmission, and it is the structure that most modern churches have abandoned.** A congregation of two hundred that meets only in a hall for ninety minutes a week has almost no capacity to form the next generation. **A congregation of two hundred that meets in twenty houses during the week has an entirely different capacity.**

### The first crisis, and the way it was solved

**And the first serious problem in the history of the church was a complaint about unfairness between two groups of people, and the way the church dealt with it should be studied by every institution that has ever had to make room for a new constituency.**

*And in those days, when the number of the disciples was multiplied, there arose a murmuring of the Grecians against the Hebrews, because their widows were neglected in the daily ministration. Then the twelve called the multitude of the disciples unto them, and said, It is not reason that we should leave the word of God, and serve tables. Wherefore, brethren, look ye out among you seven men of honest report, full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom, whom we may appoint over this business. But we will give ourselves continually to prayer, and to the ministry of the word* (Acts 6:1–4).

**Set out what happened.**

**The church had two cultural groups in it: the Hebrews, the native Aramaic-speaking Jews of Palestine, and the Grecians, the Greek-speaking Jews of the diaspora who had moved to Jerusalem.** And the distribution of food to widows was being conducted in a way that left the Grecian widows out.

**The aggrieved party complained. And the leadership's response was not to defend the distribution system and not to urge patience.**

**They changed the structure, and then — and this is the decisive part — they put the responsibility into the hands of the complainants.**

*And the saying pleased the whole multitude: and they chose Stephen, a man full of faith and of the Holy Ghost, and Philip, and Prochorus, and Nicanor, and Timon, and Parmenas, and Nicolas a proselyte of Antioch* (Acts 6:5).

**Read that list again and notice what every one of the seven names has in common.**

**They are all Greek names.**

**Stephen, Philip, Prochorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas — and Nicolas, a proselyte of Antioch, which is to say a Gentile-born convert.** In a dispute between Hebrews and Grecians, the seven men appointed to administer the funds were all from the Grecian side, or from the diaspora, or beyond it.

**The church handed the money to the people who had complained.**

**And the outcome is recorded in the next verse, and it is one of the great summary statements of the book:**

*And the word of God increased; and the number of the disciples multiplied in Jerusalem greatly; and a great company of the priests were obedient to the faith* (Acts 6:7).

**Note the three elements: the word increased, the disciples multiplied greatly, and a great company of the priests believed.**

**The last is the most remarkable.** *Polys te ochlos ton hiereon* — a large crowd of the priests, the men whose livelihood and social standing depended on the temple system, came into the community. **And that happened immediately after the church had given its funds to seven men with Greek names.**

**Which is a law of the house of God and one that every older generation needs to hear. An institution that will not give real authority to the constituency that is complaining will not be joined by the constituency that is watching.** The priests of Jerusalem watched the Grecian widows' grievance being handled by giving the complainants control, **and they concluded that the community was safe enough to join.**

**And the second element in the verse — the multiplication — is stated in the passive: the word increased.** The Greek is *exuxanen*, it grew. **The growth of the word of God is the subject of the sentence and the growth of the institution is not.**

### The young men who carried it out of Jerusalem

**And the men appointed in Acts 6 are the men who appear in Acts 7 and 8 doing the work that took the gospel out of Jerusalem, and the pattern of how it left is one of the most important facts in the New Testament.**

**Stephen preached the longest sermon recorded in Acts, was taken by a mob, and became the first martyr (Acts 7:59–60).** And his speech before the council is a history of Israel from Abraham to the prophets, delivered by a man whose name is Greek, to a court of Hebrews, **and it ends with the accusation that they had always resisted the Holy Ghost (7:51).**

**And at that execution a young man stood by holding the coats, whose name was Saul of Tarsus, and whose conversion we will come to in the next chapter.**

**And Philip went to Samaria.** The Samaritans were the half-breeds of Israel, despised by Jerusalem, and the Spirit sent the church's first cross-cultural mission there — **and then sent Philip down a desert road to meet a single Ethiopian eunuch, a court official of an African queen, reading Isaiah and unable to understand it.**

**And then the persecution came, and the community was scattered, and the scattering did the work the institution had not done.**

*And Saul was consenting unto his death. And at that time there was a great persecution against the church which was at Jerusalem; and they were all scattered abroad throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria, except the apostles ... Therefore they that were scattered abroad went every where preaching the word* (Acts 8:1, 4).

**Note the exception: *except the apostles*. The leadership remained in Jerusalem and the members were dispersed.** And the dispersion did what a centralised body could not have done — **it planted the word in every place the refugees reached.**

**And the pattern continues, and by Acts 11 the effect is enormous:**

*Now they which were scattered abroad upon the persecution that arose about Stephen travelled as far as Phenice, and Cyprus, and Antioch, preaching the word to none but unto the Jews only. And some of them were men of Cyprus and Cyrene, which, when they were come to Antioch, spake unto the Grecians, preaching the Lord Jesus. And the hand of the Lord was with them: and a great number believed, and turned unto the Lord* (Acts 11:19–21).

**Anonymous men, from Cyprus and Cyrene, in the third-largest city of the Roman Empire, preached to the Greeks.**

**And the community that resulted became the church at Antioch, which is where the disciples were first called Christians, and which is where Paul was sent out from, and which funded the mission to the Gentiles for the next thirty years.**

**The greatest missionary base in the history of the church was planted by refugees whose names are not recorded.**

**Which is the second law of this chapter for a house of God. The institution that will not send its young people out will eventually be scattered by force instead; and either way the message leaves, because the message belongs to no institution.**

**And the house that sends will find that it gains children in the third and fourth generation, because it occupies ground it never planned to occupy.**

### The first generational decision of the church

**And the last element of Pentecost's legacy is the way the church made its first great decision, and the method is a model of multigenerational government.**

**The question was whether Gentile converts needed circumcision and the law of Moses. And the account of the deliberation is deliberately detailed.**

*And when they were come to Jerusalem, they were received of the church, and of the apostles and elders, and they declared all things that God had done with them. But there rose up certain of the sect of the Pharisees which believed, saying, That it was needful to circumcise them, and to command them to keep the law of Moses. And the apostles and elders came together for to consider of this matter* (Acts 15:4–6).

**Four things in the mechanics of the council must be extracted.**

**The first is the composition: *the apostles and elders*.** The founders and the local leadership, in the same room. **And verse 4 adds that they were *received of the church*** — the whole congregation was present at the reporting.

**The second is that the argument was conducted openly and at length.** *When there had been much disputing* (15:7), Peter speaks, then Paul and Barnabas report what God had done among the Gentiles, **and finally James gives a judgement grounded in the words of the prophets (15:13–21).** The decision was reached by testimony, discussion, and Scripture — and not by the senior man settling it.

**The third is that the decision was carried to the churches by chosen men rather than by letter alone.**

*Then pleased it the apostles and elders, with the whole church, to send chosen men of their own company to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas; namely, Judas surnamed Barsabas, and Silas, chief men among the brethren* (Acts 15:22).

**And note the phrase *with the whole church***. The decision was not the elders' private judgement; **the congregation was party to the appointment of the messengers, and the messengers were *chief men among the brethren* — men of standing in the community.**

**And the fourth is that the decision was written down.** *And they wrote letters by them after this manner; the apostles and elders and brethren send greeting unto the brethren which are of the Gentiles* (15:23). **The greeting names all three parties: apostles, elders, and brethren.**

**And the effect of the whole process is stated when the messengers delivered it:**

*And so were the churches established in the faith, and increased in number daily* (Acts 16:5).

**Which means that a document produced by a multi-generational council, carried by witnesses, and received openly, produced a stable and growing church across two continents.**

**And that is what the Spirit created on the day of Pentecost: not a religious experience, but a people with a doctrine, a table, a shared purse, a discipline, a government in which old men and young men argued in public and recorded their decisions, and a promise that was explicitly extended to the children before the parents had finished being converted.**

### Principles

**First: Pentecost created a people, not a meeting.** A doctrine, a fellowship, a table, prayers, a common purse, and a promise extended to children by name.

**Second: the promise is addressed in three circles, and the second is children.** *Unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off.* And the New Testament records household after household coming in.

**Third: the community was defined by persistence, not by a moment.** *They continued stedfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of bread, and in prayers.*

**Fourth: the Spirit reversed Babel without removing the languages.** Fifteen nations heard the works of God in their own tongues, and became one body the same day. **The community is not the erasure of what men are; it is the union of them.**

**Fifth: the church was simultaneously gathered and distributed.** *Continuing daily with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house.* The house is where children are, and the New Testament knew it.

**Sixth: the first crisis was solved by giving authority to the complainants.** Seven Greek names were appointed to administer funds for Grecian widows, and the next verse records a great company of priests obeying the faith.

**Seventh: the message left Jerusalem by scattering rather than by expansion.** *They that were scattered abroad went every where preaching the word*, and the greatest missionary church in the world was planted by refugees from Cyprus and Cyrene.

**Eighth: the first great decision was made by apostles and elders with the whole church, by testimony and Scripture, and it was written down.** *It seemed good unto us, being assembled with one accord* — a unity reached through argument.

**And ninth: the result of that governance was establishment and daily increase.** *So were the churches established in the faith, and increased in number daily.*

### For the fathers

- **Include the children in the promise by name, and build the house accordingly.** *Unto you, and to your children.* A congregation whose structures cannot hold a child has not yet adopted the arrangement the Spirit established.
- **Keep the house-to-house structure, or recover it.** *Breaking bread from house to house.* The household is the only institution in Scripture that reliably carries a deposit across generations.
- **Give real authority to the people who complain, and do it before the crisis.** Seven Greek names administering funds for Grecian widows. The priests of Jerusalem were watching, and then they joined.
- **Do not defend the distribution system.** The apostles did not answer the accusation; they changed the structure and then handed it over.
- **Write the decisions down, and send witnesses with them.** *They wrote letters by them*, and the messengers were *chief men among the brethren.* A decision that is not documented is a decision that will be re-litigated by the next generation.
- **And do not be afraid of being scattered.** The institutional centre is not the safety of the work. *They that were scattered abroad went every where preaching the word.*

### For the young

- **You are in a community with four commitments, and they are all unglamorous.** Doctrine, fellowship, the table, prayer — and persistence in them, which is the whole of the Christian life in one verse.
- **Expect the church to be multilingual and incompatible, because it was designed that way.** A publican and a zealot among the twelve; fifteen nations on the first day. If your circle is comfortable, you are not yet at Pentecost.
- **Do not wait for a platform to preach.** The men who took the gospel to Antioch were refugees with no title, no commissioning service, and no name in the record.
- **And accept the possibility that your generation's greatest work will be done after a scattering.** *They that were scattered abroad* — the pressure that feels like the end of an institution is often the beginning of its multiplication.
- **Sit in the councils and speak, even when you are the youngest in the room.** The council of Acts 15 reached its decision through *much disputing*, testimony, and Scripture, and the outcome was a written document that established the churches.
- **And let the promise be for your children.** The community you build is not for your own spiritual experience. It is a house with a doctrine, a table, and a name for the generation that follows you.

### Questions for reflection

1. Which of the four commitments in Acts 2:42 does your house actually persist in, and which is nominal?
2. Have the children in your community been given a promise, or only a programme?
3. When a constituency in your church complains about unfairness, what has your instinct been — to defend the system, or to change it and hand it over?
4. What would your church be doing if it were structured to be scattered rather than gathered?
5. Whose names will be in the record of the next twenty years of your work, and what are you doing to prepare them to be there?
