In the year 640 BC, a boy of eight years old was placed on the throne of Judah. His father had been assassinated after two years of misrule, his grandfather had reigned for fifty-five years and had been the worst king in the nation's history, and the temple built by Solomon was standing in Jerusalem with altars to Baal inside its courts.

Thirty-one years later, the same king died in a valley in the north, killed by an Egyptian archer, and the historian who recorded his reign gave him a sentence that no other king of Israel or Judah received: *And like unto him was there no king before him, that turned to the Lord with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his might, according to all the law of Moses; neither after him arose there any like him* (2 Kings 23:25).

He was the greatest king in the history of the southern kingdom. And God told him that the judgment on his nation was irreversible.

This chapter is about the last of that paradox, and about a generation that inherited a debt it could not repay.

### The inheritance: fifty-five years

To understand Josiah, we must first measure what he inherited, and that requires looking at his grandfather.

Manasseh became king at the age of twelve and reigned fifty-five years (2 Kings 21:1). It is the longest reign in the history of either kingdom. And the record of what he did is the most comprehensive catalogue of apostasy in Scripture.

*For he built up again the high places which Hezekiah his father had destroyed; and he reared up altars for Baal, and made a grove, as did Ahab king of Israel; and worshipped all the host of heaven, and served them. And he built altars in the house of the Lord, of which the Lord said, In Jerusalem will I put my name. And he built altars for all the host of heaven in the two courts of the house of the Lord. And he made his son pass through the fire, and observed times, and used enchantments, and dealt with familiar spirits and wizards: he wrought much wickedness in the sight of the Lord, to provoke him to anger. And he set a graven image of the grove that he had made in the house* (2 Kings 21:3–7).

Take the list:
- **He rebuilt what his father had demolished.** Hezekiah's reforms (2 Kings 18:4) were reversed.
- **He imported the religion of Ahab.** The *grove* — the Hebrew *asherah*, a wooden pole representing the Canaanite goddess — was the central symbol of the Baal cult.
- **He worshipped the host of heaven.** Astral religion, practised openly in the temple courts.
- **He built altars inside the house of the Lord.** Not outside, not alongside. Inside the two courts of the temple that Solomon had built for the name of the Lord.
- **He burned his own son as a sacrifice.** *Made his son pass through the fire* — a child offered to Molech in the valley of Hinnom.
- **He institutionalised the occult.** *Observed times, and used enchantments, and dealt with familiar spirits and wizards.* This is a developed state apparatus of divination.
- **And he installed an image in the temple itself.**

And then the sentence that reveals the scale of the crime:

*Moreover Manasseh shed innocent blood very much, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another; beside his sin wherewith he made Judah to sin, in doing that which was evil in the sight of the Lord* (2 Kings 21:16).

*Filled Jerusalem from one end to another.* The phrase is the same used of Amos' complaint that justice is turned to wormwood, and of the description of Jerusalem's blood in Isaiah 1:15 — *your hands are full of blood*. Josephus and the rabbinic tradition both understood this to refer to the systematic murder of the prophets and the righteous during Manasseh's reign, and tradition places the death of Isaiah in that period.

**Fifty-five years of it.** Manasseh began at twelve and reigned until he was sixty-seven. Every institution in the nation was staffed with men he had appointed. Every family in Jerusalem had been formed to some degree under the cult he had established. The priests in the temple had been ordained under the regime that put an image in the holy place. And the children of the city had grown up knowing only that world.

Then he died, and his son Amon reigned two years in the same pattern (2 Kings 21:20–22), and then Amon's own servants assassinated him, and the conspirators were executed by *the people of the land*, and Josiah, aged eight, was made king (2 Kings 21:23–24).

**That is the world Josiah inherited.** A nation that had spent fifty-seven years being systematically formed in idolatry, with a religious establishment that had made its peace with it, and a temple defiled by an image of a goddess in the house of God.

And the king who is to undo it is eight years old.

### The decree: a boy named three centuries in advance

Before we examine his reign, we must notice the most extraordinary thing about Josiah, and it is a matter of a prophecy delivered in about 930 BC, roughly three hundred years before his birth.

After Solomon's death, the kingdom divided, and Jeroboam the son of Nebat established an alternative religion in the north to prevent his subjects from travelling to Jerusalem for the feasts. He made two golden calves, set one at Bethel and one at Dan, and built an altar at Bethel — and as he stood by it to burn incense, a prophet from Judah arrived.

*And he cried against the altar in the word of the Lord, and said, O altar, altar, thus saith the Lord; Behold, a child shall be born unto the house of David, Josiah by name; and upon thee shall he offer the priests of the high places that burn incense upon thee, and men's bones shall be burnt upon thee* (1 Kings 13:2).

*Josiah by name.* Three centuries in advance. The prophecy names the unborn king, identifies the dynasty he will come from, and specifies the action he will perform — the desecration of Jeroboam's altar by the burning of the bones of its priests upon it.

There is nothing else quite like this in the Old Testament. But it belongs in this book for a specific reason, and the reason is not the marvel of prediction. It is this:

**Between the prophecy and its fulfilment are three hundred years, and the fulfilment required a young man who did not know the prophecy was about him.**

Josiah was not told, so far as the text records, that he was the subject of a three-hundred-year-old prediction. He grew up, sought God at sixteen, purged the land at twenty, repaired the temple at twenty-six, and then went north and did at Bethel what had been decreed three centuries before.

The fulfilment was not something he aimed at; it was something he walked into by being faithful in his own generation.

And this is a principle of considerable importance for our subject. **You will very often be the fulfilment of a word you have never heard.** The prophecies that concern your generation were frequently given to people you will never meet, and they are fulfilled in the ordinary course of obedience rather than in the pursuit of the prediction.

### The ages of young Josiah

The Chronicler gives us a detail that the writer of Kings omits, and it is the most practically useful verse in the whole account.

*For in the eighth year of his reign, while he was yet young, he began to seek after the God of David his father: and in the twelfth year he began to purge Judah and Jerusalem from the high places, and the groves, and the carved images, and the molten images* (2 Chronicles 34:3).

Add the numbers to the ages:
- **Age eight (2 Kings 22:1).** He becomes king. The people of the land install him.
- **Age sixteen (2 Chronicles 34:3, eighth year of his reign).** He begins to *seek after the God of David his father*. Note the phrase — *the God of David his father*. He did not inherit a living tradition; he inherited a name. He went looking for the God of a king who had been dead for three hundred years.
- **Age twenty (2 Chronicles 34:3, twelfth year).** He begins to purge. The seeking precedes the purging by four years.
- **Age twenty-six (2 Kings 22:3, eighteenth year).** He commissions the repair of the temple, and the book of the law is found.
- **Age thirty-one to thirty-nine.** The great reform, the Passover, and then Megiddo.

Now look at the sequence, because it is the shape of every genuine young reformer's life.

**The seeking came before the purging.** For four years, Josiah sought the God of David before he removed a single altar. Most young men in his position would have begun with the demolition. Josiah began with the search. He was not a reformer with a plan; he was a young man looking for God, and the reform follows.

**And he began *while he was yet young*.** The Chronicler's phrase is deliberate. He is not recording an extraordinary old man's achievement; he is recording what a sixteen-year-old did, and he wants the reader to know that the age is part of the point.

**And the movement is from the personal to the structural.** Seeking, then purging, then repairing. Interior, then local, then institutional. A young person who attempts the third before the first will break things, and a young person who attempts the second before the first will do so with a wrong spirit.

**And he did it slowly.** Twenty-three years pass between the beginning of the purge and the great Passover. That is longer than the whole of some ministries.

The account also raises a question the text leaves open, and it is worth stating. Where did a boy raised in the household of Amon, with the cult of Manasseh in full operation, learn to seek the God of David? The text does not say. But the answer is partly available in the surrounding narrative: *the people of the land* — the phrase used three times in 2 Kings 21:24 and 23:30 — were the freeholders of Judah, and they had opposed the conspiracy, executed the assassins and set the boy on the throne.

There was a faithful remnant in the countryside, and they made a king, and the king they made went looking for God at sixteen.

### The book found

At twenty-six, Josiah turned his attention to the temple, and it had not been repaired for at least seventy years.

*And it came to pass in the eighteenth year of king Josiah, that the king sent Shaphan the son of Azaliah, the son of Meshullam, the scribe, to the house of the Lord, saying, Go up to Hilkiah the high priest, that he may sum the silver which is brought into the house of the Lord, which the keepers of the door have gathered of the people: and let them deliver it into the hand of the doers of the work, that have the oversight of the house of the Lord* (2 Kings 22:3–4).

The instruction is administrative: collect the money, pay the workmen, repair the building. Note the detail that follows: *Howbeit there was no reckoning made with them of the money that was delivered into their hand, because they dealt faithfully* (2 Kings 22:7).

And then, in the middle of a building project, the discovery.

*And Hilkiah the high priest said unto Shaphan the scribe, I have found the book of the law in the house of the Lord. And Hilkiah gave the book to Shaphan, and he read it* (2 Kings 22:8).

*I have found the book of the law in the house of the Lord.* It had been *in the house* — not lost in a foreign land, not carried off by an enemy, not destroyed. It had been there, in the temple, and nobody had been reading it. The roof was being repaired while the book lay unopened on the premises.

The book is generally identified with some portion of the Deuteronomic law, and the reason for that identification is that everything Josiah subsequently does corresponds to Deuteronomy's regulations: the centralisation of worship, the destruction of high places, the abolition of the Asherim and the host-of-heaven cult, and the observance of the Passover.

And Shaphan does what a scribe does. He reads it. And then he does something more:

*And Shaphan the scribe came to the king, and brought the king word again ... And Shaphan the scribe shewed the king, saying, Hilkiah the priest hath delivered me a book. And Shaphan read it before the king* (2 Kings 22:9–10).

He reads the whole thing aloud to the king. And then:

*And it came to pass, when the king had heard the words of the book of the law, that he rent his clothes* (2 Kings 22:11).

The rending of garments is the standard biblical gesture for grief, and it is performed here by a king who has heard a document read aloud in his own palace.

**And what he had heard was the curses of Deuteronomy.** It is worth pausing on this, because a modern reader who has not read Deuteronomy may underestimate what landed on the young king. The book of Deuteronomy ends with a chapter of blessings for obedience and a much longer chapter of curses for disobedience — sieges, famine, the eating of children, captivity, dispersion among the nations, and the reversal of the exodus (Deuteronomy 28:15–68). It is the most terrible chapter in the Pentateuch.

Josiah had the outline. He knew the history: the ten northern tribes had been deported to Assyria in 722 BC, less than a century earlier, and their land was full of foreigners. He was listening to a document that described exactly that outcome, and he was king of what was left.

### The king's reaction and the generational confession

And his response contains a sentence that must be examined closely.

*And the king commanded Hilkiah the priest, and Ahikam the son of Shaphan, and Achbor the son of Michaiah, and Shaphan the scribe, and Asahiah a servant of the king's, saying, Go ye, enquire of the Lord for me, and for the people, and for all Judah, concerning the words of this book that is found: for great is the wrath of the Lord that is kindled against us, because our fathers have not hearkened unto the words of this book, to do according unto all that which is written concerning us* (2 Kings 22:12–13).

*Because our fathers have not hearkened.*

That is a confession, and it is remarkable for several reasons.

**First, it is correct.** The nation was in its condition because of Manasseh and Amon, and Josiah says so. He does not blame God, or the Assyrians, or the Babylonians, or fate. He identifies the cause.

**Second, it includes him in the sentence.** He says *our* fathers. He is the son of Amon, and he is not asking to be exempted from the family history.

**Third, it does not minimise his own responsibility.** He has already begun to purge the land. He is doing everything a man can do. And he does not say *but I have done my part*. He asks for an enquiry about the wrath that is kindled against the nation he rules.

**And fourth, he does not assume that the earlier reforms have settled the matter.** The purge began four years before the book was found, which means Josiah had been dealing with the external symptoms before he understood the diagnosis. Now that he has read the diagnosis, he is far more frightened than before.

That is a sign of genuine spiritual seriousness. The man who has done more than anyone else in his generation to fix the problem is the man most alarmed by the scale of the problem.

### Huldah

And now the delegation goes to a prophet, and the text gives us an unusually detailed identification of her.

*So Hilkiah the priest, and Ahikam, and Achbor, and Shaphan, and Asahiah, went unto Huldah the prophetess, the wife of Shallum the son of Tikvah, the son of Harhas, keeper of the wardrobe; (now she dwelt in Jerusalem in the college;) and they communed with her* (2 Kings 22:14).

The party sent to her is extraordinary: the high priest, the king's secretary of state, a senior official, the king's own servant, and one other. This is the highest-level delegation the court could assemble, and it goes to consult a woman.

And note what we are told about her. She is identified by her husband's lineage and his office — *keeper of the wardrobe*, which is a court position of some significance, since the royal wardrobe was part of the treasury apparatus. And she *dwelt in Jerusalem in the college*, the *mishneh* — the second quarter, the newer part of the city.

The detail is important because the text bothers to give it. A woman prophet consulted by the highest officials of the state, resident in the city, married into the court. The Bible records no hesitation about this, no apology, no qualification. Huldah holds the highest prophetic authority in Jerusalem at the time, and she is the one who authenticates the book that a young king has just heard read for the first time.

And her oracle is a double word, and the second half is the part that matters here.

*Thus saith the Lord, Behold, I will bring evil upon this place, and upon the inhabitants thereof, even all the words of the book which the king of Judah hath read: because they have forsaken me, and have burned incense unto other gods, that they might provoke me to anger with all the works of their hands; therefore my wrath shall be kindled against this place, and shall not be quenched* (2 Kings 22:16–17).

*Shall not be quenched.* The judgment is fixed. The book was right. The nation is going into exile.

And then:

*But to the king of Judah which sent you to enquire of the Lord, thus shall ye say to him, Thus saith the Lord God of Israel, As touching the words which thou hast heard; Because thine heart was tender, and thou hast humbled thyself before the Lord, when thou heardest what I spake against this place, and against the inhabitants thereof, that they should become a desolation and a curse, and hast rent thy clothes, and wept before me; I also have heard thee, saith the Lord. Behold therefore, I will gather thee unto thy fathers, and thou shalt be gathered into thy grave in peace; and thine eyes shall not see all the evil which I will bring upon this place* (2 Kings 22:18–20).

*Because thine heart was tender.*

The Hebrew is *rakh levavkha* — soft of heart. Huldah's answer identifies the specific quality that moved God on behalf of a man whose nation was doomed: a heart that received the word of God in its full severity and was broken by it.

And the mercy granted is a postponement, personal and total: he will die in peace, and he will not see the evil. Twenty-two years after his death, the temple he repaired would be burned to the ground.

**Every young person who hopes to reform anything should sit with that answer for a while.** Josiah received no promise of national rescue. He received a promise that his own life would not have to witness the end. And he went back to work.

### The reform

And it is at this point that the young king does what no other king in the history of Judah did, and he does it in a manner that has no parallel.

**First, he assembles the nation and reads the book to them.**

*And the king sent, and they gathered unto him all the elders of Judah and of Jerusalem. And the king went up into the house of the Lord, and all the men of Judah and all the inhabitants of Jerusalem with him, and the priests, and the prophets, and all the people, both small and great: and he read in their ears all the words of the book of the covenant which was found in the house of the Lord* (2 Kings 23:1–2).

*Both small and great.* The whole population, from the smallest to the greatest, is assembled in the temple courts, and the king reads them the book. This is the only recorded public reading of the law by a king in the Old Testament — and it is a young man reading it.

**Second, he makes a covenant and binds the people to it.**

*And the king stood by a pillar, and made a covenant before the Lord, to walk after the Lord, and to keep his commandments and his testimonies and his statutes with all their heart and all their soul, to perform the words of this covenant that were written in this book. And all the people stood to the covenant* (2 Kings 23:3).

And the phrase *made a covenant* — the Hebrew is *karath berith*, to cut a covenant — is followed by *all the people stood to the covenant*. They stand for it. It is a corporate act, entered by a nation, at the initiative of a king of twenty-six.

**Third, he purges.** What follows in 2 Kings 23:4–20 is the most thoroughgoing programme of religious demolition in Scripture, and it is worth cataloguing because the thoroughness is the point:

- the vessels made for Baal, for the Asherah and for the host of heaven, burned outside Jerusalem and their ashes carried to Bethel (23:4);
- the idolatrous priests deposed and their high places broken down from Geba to Beersheba (23:5, 8);
- the Asherah brought out of the temple, burned, beaten to powder and the powder scattered on the graves of the common people (23:6) — a deliberate act of defilement;
- the houses of the sodomites in the temple, and the women who wove hangings for the Asherah (23:7);
- all the priests out of the cities of Judah (23:8);
- **Topheth in the valley of the children of Hinnom, defiled so that no man might make his son or daughter pass through the fire to Molech** (23:10);
- the horses and chariots of the sun, kept by the kings of Judah at the entrance of the temple, removed and burned (23:11);
- the altars on the roof of the upper chamber of Ahaz, and those Manasseh had made in the two courts, broken down, carried away and cast into the brook Kidron (23:12);
- **the high places at Bethel, built by Jeroboam**, broken down, the altar burned and beaten to powder, and the bones burned on it (23:15–16);
- and the bones of the priests taken out of the high places and burned upon the altars (23:20).

**Every one of the categories of Manasseh's sin is systematically addressed.** The altars, the grove, the host of heaven, the temple image, the child sacrifice, the familiar spirits, the high places. Josiah read the book, identified the violations, and removed them.

**And at Bethel, the three-hundred-year-old prophecy was fulfilled.** He desecrated the altar of Jeroboam, burned it, beat it to powder, and burned human bones on it (2 Kings 23:15–16) — exactly what had been cried out against that altar before he was born.

**Fourth, he kept the Passover.**

*And the king commanded all the people, saying, Keep the passover unto the Lord your God, as it is written in the book of this covenant* (2 Kings 23:21).

And the verdict: *Surely there was not holden such a passover from the days of the judges that judged Israel, nor in all the days of the kings of Israel, nor of the kings of Judah* (2 Kings 23:22).

Not since the days of the judges. Not since Samuel. Not in David's reign, or Solomon's, or Hezekiah's. A young man of twenty-six presided over the greatest Passover in the history of the nation.

### The limits of reform

And then the sentence that turns the whole story, and that most treatments of Josiah skip.

*Notwithstanding the Lord turned not from the fierceness of his great wrath, wherewith his anger was kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations that Manasseh had provoked him withal. And the Lord said, I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel, and will cast off this city Jerusalem which I have chosen, and the house of which I said, My name shall be there* (2 Kings 23:26–27).

*Notwithstanding.* After the greatest reform in the history of the southern kingdom, the judgment stands, and the reason given is *because of all the provocations that Manasseh had provoked him withal.*

This is one of the hardest statements in the Old Testament and it must be handled without evasion. God had said it to Manasseh himself in advance: *Behold, I am bringing such evil upon Jerusalem and Judah, that whosoever heareth of it, both his ears shall tingle. And I will stretch over Jerusalem the line of Samaria, and the plummet of the house of Ahab: and I will wipe Jerusalem as a man wipeth a dish, wiping it, and turning it upside down. And I will forsake the remnant of mine inheritance, and deliver them into the hand of their enemies* (2 Kings 21:12–14).

The sentence was pronounced in Manasseh's reign. Josiah's reform was genuine, and God accepted the king's repentance and promised him personal mercy. But the sentence on the nation had been passed, and the reform of the next generation did not reverse it.

**What follows from this for a book about generational revelation?**

**It means that a generation can inherit a debt it cannot repay.** Josiah could remove the altars, restore the temple, and reinstate the Passover. What he could not do was un-shed the innocent blood his grandfather had poured into the streets of Jerusalem, un-burn the children who had passed through the fire in the valley of Hinnom, or un-form the priests who had been ordained under a regime of apostasy.

There are consequences of a previous generation's sins that the next generation cannot undo by diligence, however faithful it may be. And one of the most painful tasks of a young reformer is to discover that his reforms are genuinely accepted by God and nonetheless insufficient to avert the outcome.

**It means that the mercy given to the reformer is not the mercy given to the nation.** Josiah was told that he would die in peace and not see the evil. His people were not. The reformer's consolation was personal; the nation's course was unchanged.

**And it means that faithfulness is not measured by outcome.** Josiah did the right thing in a doomed nation. And God's estimate of him is on the record: there was no king like him before or after, who turned to the Lord with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his might.

That is a very important thing to know before you begin. Because the young person who enters into a work of reform with the expectation of visible success will be destroyed by the first evidence that the tide does not turn. The person who enters it knowing that God may accept the obedience and still allow the judgment to fall will survive.

### Megiddo

And the story ends in the most unexpected place, and the ending is a warning.

In 609 BC, Pharaoh Necho of Egypt marched north to support the collapsing Assyrian empire against the rising Babylonians. His route took him through the coastal plain of Palestine, past the pass at Megiddo — the same valley where Deborah and Barak had destroyed Sisera's chariots, and where the language of Revelation locates the final battle.

And Josiah went out to oppose him.

The Chronicler records that Necho sent envoys with a warning, and the warning is extraordinary:

*But he sent ambassadors to him, saying, What have I to do with thee, thou king of Judah? I come not against thee this day, but against the house wherewith I have war: for God commanded me to make haste: forbear thee from meddling with God, who is with me, that he destroy thee not* (2 Chronicles 35:21).

*God commanded me to make haste ... forbear thee from meddling with God, who is with me.*

A pagan Pharaoh, in the book of Chronicles, testifies that God had commanded him to hurry, and warns the king of Judah not to interfere with a divine commission given to a Gentile.

And Josiah did not listen.

*Nevertheless Josiah would not turn his face from him, but disguised himself, that he might fight with him, and hearkened not unto the words of Necho from the mouth of God, and came to fight in the valley of Megiddo* (2 Chronicles 35:22).

The Chronicler's phrase is unambiguous: *hearkened not unto the words of Necho from the mouth of God.* The greatest reforming king in the history of Judah died because he would not receive a word from an unexpected source.

*And the archers shot at king Josiah; and the king said to his servants, Have me away; for I am sore wounded* (2 Chronicles 35:23). He died at Megiddo, and his servants carried his body back to Jerusalem in a chariot, and *all Judah and Jerusalem mourned for Josiah* (2 Chronicles 35:24). And Jeremiah wrote a lamentation for him that was sung in Israel for generations (2 Chronicles 35:25), and the mourning at Megiddo became a byword in Israel's memory — a hundred and fifty years later the prophet Zechariah would use it as the measure of grief itself: *as the mourning of Hadadrimmon in the valley of Megiddo* (Zechariah 12:11).

**So the greatest king since David died at thirty-nine, in a battle he should not have fought, having refused a warning that came from a king of Egypt.**

And the kingdom fell within twenty-two years. Jehoahaz reigned three months, Jehoiakim eleven years, Jehoiachin three months, Zedekiah eleven years, and then the temple was burned and the people were carried to Babylon.

Which brings us to a principle that this book will return to repeatedly, and which this chapter must state plainly:

**The godliness of one generation is not transferred automatically to the next, and the quality of a man's reform does not secure his own judgment.** Josiah's last recorded act is a decision he made against a clear word, and it was fatal.

### What the young should take from Josiah

Let us gather the principles, because this chapter contains more practical instruction than any other in this part of the book.

**First: a young person can be the hinge of a generational reversal.** Eight years old at his accession, sixteen when he began to seek God, twenty when he began to purge. Twenty-three years of patient work. The largest reform in the nation's history was led by a man in his twenties and thirties, and it began when he was a teenager looking for the God of a king who had died three hundred years earlier.

**Second: the seeking precedes the reforming.** Four years pass between Josiah's beginning to seek God and his beginning to purge. A young person who wants to change an institution should spend a long time on the first before touching the second.

**Third: the rediscovery of the book is the mechanism.** The turning point of the reign is not a vision, a miracle, or a political event. It is a scroll found in a building during a renovation, read aloud, and taken seriously. **Every true reform in the history of the people of God has been a rediscovery of a text they already had.**

**Fourth: the young reformer must be willing to describe the nation's condition as his fathers' fault, and include himself in the sentence.** *Our fathers have not hearkened.* That is the confession, and it is honest, and it does not exempt the man who makes it.

**Fifth: he must be willing to hear the judgment in full.** Josiah did not ask for a favourable reading. He asked for an enquiry, and he received a sentence of national destruction, and he did not dispute it. The leaders who will not receive bad news from God will not last, and they will not reform anything.

**Sixth: he must accept that God may grant him personal mercy and withhold national rescue.** *Thou shalt be gathered into thy grave in peace, and thine eyes shall not see all the evil.* That was the promise. It was not what a young man would have hoped for. He went back to work.

**Seventh, and finally: the young reformer can still die of a single act of presumption.** Josiah's reform was unprecedented, his heart was tender, his mourning was national — and he went to Megiddo against a word from God delivered by a pagan king, and he did not come back.

**A young man's greatest danger is not the idolatry he inherited; it is the confidence his own success has produced.**

### The last word

And yet we should not end on Megiddo. We should end where the historian ends.

*And like unto him was there no king before him, that turned to the Lord with all his heart, and with all his soul, and with all his might, according to all the law of Moses; neither after him arose there any like him* (2 Kings 23:25).

That verdict stands in Scripture permanently. The nation fell. The temple burned. The judgment was not averted. And the man who read the book, rent his clothes, wept before God, pulled down the altars, and restored the Passover is recorded as the greatest of all the kings of Judah — greater than David, greater than Hezekiah, greater than Solomon.

Which means that the standard by which God evaluates a generation is not the outcome of its circumstances but the completeness of its turning.

A boy of eight inherited fifty-seven years of apostasy, a defiled temple, and a nation under a sentence. He could not undo any of it. He could only turn, and read, and obey, and take down what was standing where it should not stand.

And that was enough to be written down, and kept, and read for twenty-six hundred years.
