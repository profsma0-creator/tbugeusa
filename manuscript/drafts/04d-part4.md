## David's Final Instructions to Solomon

No leader in Scripture spends as much time preparing a successor as David, and no book of the Bible devotes as much space to the mechanics of a handover. The last chapters of 1 Chronicles and the first chapters of 1 Kings, read together, amount to a manual: they contain a written charge, a set of architectural drawings delivered in writing, a treasury, an org chart, a public ceremony, a private list of unresolved matters, and a prayer.

And the whole thing exists because of a refusal.

David wanted to build the house of God and was forbidden to do it. What he did with the refusal is the subject of this chapter, and it is the clearest model in Scripture of what it means for one generation to prepare the ground for the next.

### The refusal

The request is made in 2 Samuel 7, and it is a good request.

*Then Nathan said unto the king, Go, and do all that is in thine heart; for the Lord is with thee. And it came to pass that night, that the word of the Lord came unto Nathan, saying, Go and tell my servant David, Thus saith the Lord, Shalt thou build me an house for me to dwell in?* (2 Samuel 7:3–5).

And the answer, given through Nathan and confirmed to David in the same chapter, is no — with a qualification: the house will be built by David's son (2 Samuel 7:12–13).

David's own account of the refusal, given to the assembled leadership of Israel in the last year of his life, is in 1 Chronicles:

*Then David the king stood up upon his feet, and said, Hear me, my brethren, and my people: As for me, I had in mine heart to build an house of rest for the ark of the covenant of the Lord, and for the footstool of our God, and had made ready for the building: But God said unto me, Thou shalt not build an house for my name, because thou hast been a man of war, and hast shed blood* (1 Chronicles 28:2–3).

**The reason given is David's own history.** He is a man of war who has shed blood, and the house of rest is not for him to build. The disqualification is not a punishment for a specific sin. It is a statement about the incompatibility between the vocation he had been given and the one he wanted.

**And this is the first thing this chapter has to say about generational revelation.** The most common experience of a man who loves God and has been given a vision by Him is that he is told it is not for him to complete. Moses saw the land and died before it (Deuteronomy 34:1–4). David received the temple vision and could not build it. Abraham was given a promise that took four hundred years to fulfil. **Almost every significant work of God is handed over unfinished.**

And the question that separates one generation from another is: **what does a man do with a vision he has been told he cannot complete?**

### What David did with the refusal

He gave the whole of his remaining strength to preparing the ground for the man who would.

*And David said, Solomon my son is young and tender, and the house that is to be builded for the Lord must be exceeding magnifical, of fame and of glory throughout all countries: I will therefore now make preparation for it. So David prepared abundantly before his death* (1 Chronicles 22:5).

**Read the reason clause carefully.** *Solomon my son is young and tender* — the Hebrew is *na'ar varakh*, a boy and soft, the same phrase David himself had used before Goliath. David's reasoning is that the successor is not yet able to bear the load, and therefore the predecessor will bear it for him.

*I will therefore now make preparation for it.* Not *I will therefore resign*, not *I will therefore wait for him to grow up*, not *I will therefore hand over a vague intention*. **Preparation.**

*So David prepared abundantly before his death.* The Hebrew is *hekhin lerov* — he prepared in abundance, in quantity. And the phrase *before his death* tells us the timing: this was the work of the last years of his life, done deliberately as he approached the end.

**That is the model, and it is not complicated.** A man who cannot do the thing should spend the remainder of his life making the thing possible for the man who can.

### The six things David handed over

And now we must catalogue what David actually transferred, because the comprehensiveness of it is the point. He handed over six distinct things, and each one answers a different question a successor will have.

**The first is a written charge.** Spoken, in 1 Kings, to Solomon privately:

*Now the days of David drew nigh that he should die; and he charged Solomon his son, saying, I go the way of all the earth: be thou strong therefore, and shew thyself a man; and keep the charge of the Lord thy God, to walk in his ways, to keep his statutes, and his commandments, and his judgments, and his testimonies, as it is written in the law of Moses, that thou mayest prosper in all that thou doest, and whithersoever thou turnest thyself* (1 Kings 2:1–3).

*I go the way of all the earth.* The sentence is a statement of mortality without euphemism, and it comes first because everything else depends on the son's understanding that the father is leaving.

*Be thou strong therefore, and shew thyself a man.* The Hebrew is *vehaya le-ish* — literally *and become a man*. The charge to manhood is given in the moment of succession. And Joshua had received the same charge: *Be strong and of a good courage* (Joshua 1:6).

**And then the substance of the charge is the law of Moses.** Not the throne, not the treasury, not the alliances, not the political strategy. The charge is to keep the commandments, statutes, judgments and testimonies of God.

Look at what David does *not* say. He does not instruct Solomon about the Philistines, or Edom, or the northern tribes, or the economy, or the army. **He gives him the covenant and leaves the statecraft to him, because the covenant is the thing that cannot be reconstructed and the statecraft is the thing he will acquire.**

**And the second thing is a public restatement of the same charge, in a different register.** In 1 Chronicles 28:9–10, spoken before the assembled princes, captains, officers and mighty men:

*And thou, Solomon my son, know thou the God of thy father, and serve him with a perfect heart and with a willing mind: for the Lord searcheth all hearts, and understandeth all the imaginations of the thoughts: if thou seek him, he will be found of thee; but if thou forsake him, he will cast thee off for ever. Take heed now; for the Lord hath chosen thee to build an house for the sanctuary: be strong, and do it.*

**Know thou the God of thy father.** The most important five words in the passage, and the whole doctrine of generational faith in one sentence. Not *know the God of Israel*, not *know the God of Abraham* — *the God of thy father*. Solomon's access to God runs through his father, and his father says so publicly.

And then: *if thou seek him, he will be found of thee; but if thou forsake him, he will cast thee off for ever.* **The same conditions, stated plainly, with the negative stated openly.** David does not conceal the possibility of apostasy in a public charge to the heir.

**The third thing is the plans, delivered in writing.**

*All this, said David, the Lord made me understand in writing by his hand upon me, even all the works of this pattern* (1 Chronicles 28:19).

This is one of the most striking verses in the Old Testament on the subject of revelation. David claims that the pattern of the temple — and the list in 28:11–18 is technical and detailed, including the porch, the houses, the treasuries, the upper chambers, the inner parlours, the place of the mercy seat, the courses of the priests, the weight of gold for the candlesticks and the lamps, the weight of the fleshhooks and the bowls — **was given to him in writing, by the hand of God upon him.**

The phrase *by his hand upon me* is the language of prophetic inspiration elsewhere in Scripture (Ezekiel 1:3; 3:14; 8:1). David received the design of the temple as a piece of revelation.

**And then he gave it to his son in written form.** Not described, not explained, not entrusted to memory. Written down.

**This is the ancestor of every architectural drawing, every specification document, every constitution and every trust deed in the history of the people of God. Revelation received by one generation, written down, and handed to the next.**

**The fourth thing is the materials and the treasury.** And the scale of it is worth pausing on.

*Now, behold, in my trouble I have prepared for the house of the Lord an hundred thousand talents of gold, and a thousand thousand talents of silver; and of brass and iron without weight; for it is in abundance: timber also and stone have I prepared; and thou mayest add thereto* (1 Chronicles 22:14).

The numbers are enormous to the point of being difficult to interpret — the exegetical arguments about the talent and whether these are precision figures or conventional exaggerations are extensive, and we need not settle them here. What matters is the qualitative statement David makes about his own effort:

*Now I have prepared with all my might for the house of my God* (1 Chronicles 29:2).

**With all my might.** The Hebrew is *bekhol-kohi*. And then, over and above the national preparation, David adds his own personal gift:

*Moreover, because I have set my affection to the house of my God, I have of mine own proper good, of gold and silver, which I have given to the house of my God, over and above all that I have prepared for the holy house* (1 Chronicles 29:3).

**Note the phrase *I have set my affection to the house of my God*.** The Hebrew verb is *rachatsi*, from a root denoting delight, pleasure, the taking of pleasure in something. David's provision was not a duty performed by a king; it was the extravagance of a man who loved the thing he was not allowed to build.

**And the fifth thing is the workforce and the organisation.** *Moreover there are workmen with thee in abundance, hewers and workers of stone and timber, and all manner of cunning men for every manner of work* (1 Chronicles 22:15). And the whole of 1 Chronicles 23–27 is the organisation of the Levites, the priests, the singers, the gatekeepers, the treasurers, the judges, and the army. David gave Solomon a functioning institution, not a task.

**And the sixth thing is the ceremony.** 1 Chronicles 28–29 records the assembly of all the leadership of Israel, David's speech standing on his feet, the presentation of the plans, the public giving, and then:

*And they made Solomon the son of David king the second time, and anointed him unto the Lord to be the chief governor, and Zadok to be priest* (1 Chronicles 29:22).

*The second time* — because there had already been a hasty anointing at Gihon when Adonijah rebelled (1 Kings 1:39). The second was the deliberate, public, national transfer, performed before the whole leadership of the nation.

**And notice who stands.** *Then David the king stood up upon his feet* (1 Chronicles 28:2). The old man, described elsewhere as old and cold and unable to get warm (1 Kings 1:1), stands to deliver his charge.

**The last great act of David's life is standing in front of the whole leadership of his nation to hand everything to his son.**

### The prayer

And then David prays, and the prayer is the theological summary of the whole handover.

*Wherefore David blessed the Lord before all the congregation: and David said, Blessed be thou, Lord God of Israel our father, for ever and ever. Thine, O Lord, is the greatness, and the power, and the glory, and the victory, and the majesty: for all that is in the heaven and in the earth is thine; thine is the kingdom, O Lord, and thou art exalted as head above all. Both riches and honour come of thee, and thou reignest over all; and in thine hand is power and might; and in thine hand it is to make great, and to give strength unto all* (1 Chronicles 29:10–12).

The doxology is one of the finest in Scripture and it became the foundation of the Lord's Prayer's closing cadence in Christian liturgy. And it is spoken **by a man handing over everything he has built.**

**Look at what the prayer does.** It reassigns the whole of David's achievement to God. Greatness, power, glory, victory, majesty, the kingdom — all of it belongs to God. And then the confession that removes even the possibility of ownership:

*For we are strangers before thee, and sojourners, as were all our fathers: our days on the earth are as a shadow, and there is none abiding* (1 Chronicles 29:15).

The Hebrew for *strangers* is *gerim*, resident aliens, and for *sojourners* is *toshavim*, temporary dwellers. And *our days on the earth are as a shadow* — the image of something with no substance, cast by something else.

**A man giving away a kingdom describes himself as an alien and a shadow, and means it, and the people believe him, because the whole nation has just watched him do it.**

And then the last request:

*And give unto Solomon my son a perfect heart, to keep thy commandments, thy testimonies, and thy statutes, and to do all these things, and to build the palace, for the which I have made provision* (1 Chronicles 29:19).

**The last recorded prayer of David is a request for his son's heart, and the request is not for success, longevity, or wealth. It is for a perfect heart, so that he may keep the commandments — and, subordinate to that, build the building.**

### The unfinished business

And now we must handle the difficult material, because Scripture does not present David's handover as flawless, and pretending otherwise would make the model unusable.

The charge in 1 Kings 2 contains a list of matters David had never settled, and it is morally mixed.

*Moreover thou knowest also what Joab the son of Zeruiah did to me, and what he did to the two captains of the hosts of Israel, unto Abner the son of Ner, and unto Amasa the son of Jether, whom he slew, and shed the blood of war in peace, and put the blood of war upon his girdle that was about his loins, and in his shoes that were on his feet. Do therefore according to thy wisdom, and let not his hoar head go down to the grave in peace* (1 Kings 2:5–6).

*But shew kindness unto the sons of Barzillai the Gileadite, and let them be of those that eat at thy table; for so they came to me when I fled because of Absalom thy brother* (1 Kings 2:7).

*And, behold, thou hast with thee Shimei the son of Gera, a Benjamite of Bahurim, which cursed me with a grievous curse in the day when I went to Mahanaim: but he came down to meet me at Jordan, and I sware to him by the Lord, saying, I will not put thee to death with the sword. Now therefore hold him not guiltless: for thou art a wise man, and knowest what thou oughtest to do unto him; but his hoar head bring thou down to the grave with blood* (1 Kings 2:8–9).

**Set out the three instructions honestly.**

**Joab.** The commander of David's armies, who had murdered Abner (2 Samuel 3:27) and Amasa (2 Samuel 20:9–10) and had backed Adonijah's coup. David had never dealt with him, because Joab was indispensable. Now, at the end, he hands the problem to his son.

**Barzillai.** The Gileadite who had supplied David during Absalom's revolt (2 Samuel 17:27–29). The instruction here is pure gratitude, and it is the finest element of the charge.

**Shimei.** The Benjamite who cursed David publicly on the road out of Jerusalem (2 Samuel 16:5–8), and who met him at the Jordan asking forgiveness, and to whom David had sworn an oath not to kill him (2 Samuel 19:18–23). David now instructs his son to find a way to execute him anyway.

**And note the difficulty. David has sworn an oath, and by handing the matter to Solomon he preserves the letter of his own oath while procuring the death.** The instruction is not heroic. It is a man ensuring that a grudge is settled after his own death, by the hand of another.

**The text does not commend this and does not condemn it in so many words. It records it.** And what it establishes, with painful clarity, is that **a man can hand over a kingdom and a temple pattern and a treasury and still hand over the residue of his own unfinished business.**

Which gives us the principle, and it is one of the most practical in this whole part of the book:

**The matters you do not resolve in your lifetime do not disappear at your death. They become your successor's first assignment.**

Solomon's reign begins, in 1 Kings 2, with the execution of Adonijah, the removal of Abiathar from the priesthood, the killing of Joab, and the killing of Shimei. Every one of those is either David's problem or a direct consequence of it. **The first four acts of the golden age were clearing the ground of the previous generation's postponed decisions.**

And a man who wants to hand over clean should ask, before he dies, what his successor will have to do on his behalf.

### "Served his own generation"

And the New Testament gives the summary of this life, and it does it in a single clause that is the best definition of a successful old age in Scripture.

*For David, after he had served his own generation by the will of God, fell on sleep, and was laid unto his fathers, and saw corruption* (Acts 13:36).

*He served his own generation.* The Greek is *idios genea hypretesas* — having served, or ministered to, his own generation, and the word *hypretes* denotes an under-rower, the man who rows beneath the deck of a galley. It is the language of subordinate labour.

**And then: *by the will of God*.** The phrase qualifies the service: he served his own generation in accordance with the counsel of God for his own generation.

**And then: *fell on sleep*.** The simplest description of the death of a man who has finished his work.

**The sentence is the whole theology of a generation in eleven words.** A man is not given the whole purpose. He is given a *generation*, and a portion of the purpose that belongs to his generation, and his task is to serve it — not to own it, not to complete the plan, not to see the end of it, and not to be remembered as the one who finished it.

**And when he has served his generation by the will of God, he falls asleep, and the next man wakes up and begins.**

That is what David did. He could not build the house. He spent his last years preparing for the man who could. He gave him the charge, the written plans, the materials, the organisation, the ceremony, and the prayer. He also gave him Joab, Shimei and Adonijah, because he had not dealt with them.

**And the building was built, by a man who had not carried the ark through the wilderness and had not killed a giant and had not written the psalms.**

Which is exactly how it is supposed to work.

### The first attempt and its failure

Before the handover could be completed, there was a failure, and it belongs in this chapter because it is the link between the older man's preparation and the younger man's competence.

David's first attempt to bring the ark up to Jerusalem was made with enthusiasm, with the endorsement of the whole assembly, and with the wrong method: *And they carried the ark of God in a new cart out of the house of Abinadab* (1 Chronicles 13:7). The cart was the Philistines' solution, not Israel's. The law required the Levites to carry it on their shoulders by the staves (Numbers 4:15), and nobody consulted the law.

And the procession ended in a death: *And the anger of the Lord was kindled against Uzza, and he smote him, because he put his hand to the ark: and there he died before God* (1 Chronicles 13:10).

**And David stopped.** *And David was afraid of God that day, saying, How shall I bring the ark of God home to me? And David brought not the ark home to himself to the city of David, but carried it aside into the house of Obed-edom the Gittite* (1 Chronicles 13:12–13). And it stayed there three months.

**The lesson for this chapter is in the contrast between the two attempts.** The second one is recorded in 1 Chronicles 15, and every element has changed:

*Then David said, None ought to carry the ark of God but the Levites: for them hath the Lord chosen to carry the ark of God, and to minister unto him for ever ... For because ye did it not at the first, the Lord our God made a breach upon us, for that we sought him not after the due order* (1 Chronicles 15:2, 13).

**Not after the due order.** The Hebrew is *kammishpat*, according to the ordinance. The failure was not a failure of zeal, resources, or national unity. It was a failure to go and read what had been written.

**And then the corrected procession:**

*So the priests and the Levites sanctified themselves to bring up the ark of the Lord God of Israel. And the children of the Levites bare the ark of God upon their shoulders with the staves thereon, as Moses commanded according to the word of the Lord* (1 Chronicles 15:14–15).

*As Moses commanded according to the word of the Lord.* Given to Moses, written down, preserved for centuries, and recovered by a generation that had the sense to look it up.

**And the connection to David's temple preparation is direct.** David could not build the house, and he had already been given the pattern of the house *in writing* (1 Chronicles 28:19). He understood by the time of the ark's second procession that a work done for God must be done according to the instructions — and the whole of his final provision for Solomon is an act of obeying that lesson while he still had time.

**A man who has had a failure of presumption learns to work from the specification.** Everything David does in his last years is specification work: the drawings, the weights, the courses, the treasuries, the organisation, the written charge. He is handing over a project that cannot be ruined by enthusiasm because he has removed the necessity for anyone to improvise.

### What the transfer does not guarantee

And now we must say the hardest thing in the chapter, because Scripture says it and the model is worthless without it.

**Solomon received the charge, the plans, the materials, the organisation, the ceremony and the prayer. Thirty years later his heart was not right with God.**

*But king Solomon loved many strange women, together with the daughter of Pharaoh, women of the Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites, Zidonians, and Hittites; of the nations concerning which the Lord said unto the children of Israel, Ye shall not go in to them, neither shall they come in unto you: for surely they will turn away your heart after their gods: Solomon clave unto these in love* (1 Kings 11:1–2).

And the verdict:

*For it came to pass, when Solomon was old, that his wives turned away his heart after other gods: and his heart was not perfect with the Lord his God, as was the heart of David his father* (1 Kings 11:4).

**Note the two clauses of the verdict.**

*When Solomon was old.* The drift did not happen in his youth or in his middle age. It happened in his old age, which means the man who began with the best possible preparation ended worse than he began.

**And his heart was not perfect, *as was the heart of David his father*.** So the comparison is drawn, in the text, between a man who was a man of war with blood on his hands and a man who built the temple — and the one with the flawed record is the one whose heart was right.

**Then the sentence:**

*Wherefore the Lord said unto Solomon, Forasmuch as this is done of thee, and thou hast not kept my covenant and my statutes, which I have commanded thee, I will surely rend the kingdom from thee, and will give it to thy servant* (1 Kings 11:11).

The kingdom was torn in two in the next generation, and the golden age lasted one reign.

**Why does this belong in the chapter on David's preparation?** Because it establishes the limit of what one generation can do for another, and the limit is severe.

**No amount of preparation can substitute for the successor's own obedience.** David did everything that could be done. He had the word of God on the matter (2 Samuel 7:13). He had the plans by revelation. He had the materials in abundance. He had the workforce and the organisation and the public endorsement and the prayer. **And Solomon still drifted, and it was his own doing, and he was judged for it.**

Which means:

**First, the older generation is responsible for the preparation and not for the outcome.** David's duty was to prepare; Solomon's was to keep the charge. David did his and Solomon did not do his, and Scripture does not blame David for Solomon's failure.

**Second, the success of a successor is not the measure of a predecessor's faithfulness.** If it were, David would be judged by Solomon's last years, which would be unjust.

**Third, and for the young: you are responsible for what you do with what you were given.** Solomon could have said that everything was done for him and that the temple was his father's vision. He could have complained that he had inherited an institution rather than received a calling. And the angel of the Lord had appeared to him twice (1 Kings 11:9), and he had asked for an understanding heart and been given it (1 Kings 3:9–12), and he still turned aside.

**The inheritance is not an excuse and it is not a substitute.** Every generation must know God for itself — which is what David said in the charge: *know thou the God of thy father ... if thou seek him, he will be found of thee; but if thou forsake him, he will cast thee off for ever* (1 Chronicles 28:9).

**That sentence contains both halves. The God of thy father is accessible to you, and the forsaking of him is possible for you.** David said it in public, in the ceremony of transfer, in front of the whole leadership of Israel. There was no ambiguity about what he was handing over, and no guarantee about what would be done with it.

### For the fathers

- **Name the thing you have been told you cannot complete.** Almost every man of God is given one. Write it down, and then ask what portion of it your remaining years can prepare.
- **Prepare in the successor's direction, not your own.** Everything David built in his last years was of no use to David. It was all useable only by Solomon.
- **Give the plans in writing.** The most transferable thing you possess is documentation. A vision that exists only in your head dies with you, and the successor has to receive it again from scratch.
- **Assemble the leadership and do it publicly.** The second anointing at 1 Chronicles 29:22 was a ceremony, and its function was to tell the whole nation who the successor was. Do not leave the succession to be inferred.
- **Pray for their heart, not their success.** David's last prayer asks for a perfect heart, and the building comes afterwards in the sentence. The order is the point.
- **List what you have not resolved and decide whether to resolve it or to hand it over.** Both are legitimate, but a man should know which he is doing and why. David handed over Joab and Shimei because he had spent forty years avoiding them, and his son paid for it.

### For the young

- **You will receive an unfinished work, and the unfinished part is usually the part nobody wanted to touch.** Solomon's first acts were the clearing of his father's ground.
- **Receive the written plans as revelation, not as a suggestion.** David told Solomon that the pattern had been given to him by God. If a man hands you a document he believes came from God, read it accordingly.
- **Do not be discouraged that you did not see what your father saw.** Solomon did not carry the ark or fight the giant. He built what those actions had made possible.
- **Add to what you were given.** *Timber also and stone have I prepared; and thou mayest add thereto* (1 Chronicles 22:14). The inheritance is a foundation, not a ceiling.
- **Pray the prayer your father prayed for you, for yourself.** *Give unto Solomon my son a perfect heart.* If you were not prayed for, pray it for yourself. Nothing in the doctrine of this book depends on having had a faithful father.

### Questions for reflection

1. What vision were you given that you have been told is not yours to complete? What are you doing with it?
2. If your life ended tomorrow, what would your successor have to guess at, and what would he have in writing?
3. Which of your unfinished matters are you planning to leave for someone else?
4. Who have you failed to thank, and could you name them in a public charge as David named Barzillai?
5. What is the one sentence you would want written about your generation?
