There is a pattern in Scripture so consistent, so widely distributed across its genres and centuries, and so scandalous to every settled institution, that it cannot be treated as an accident of storytelling. Again and again, when God intends to do something decisive, He selects the person every competent observer would have passed over — and He does it in a way that leaves no room for anyone to claim credit for the outcome.

The pattern is not a preference for youth. That would be sentimental, and Scripture is not sentimental. The pattern is a preference for **the one nobody would have chosen**, and youth happens to be the most obvious category in which that preference expresses itself, because in every culture and every era the young are, by definition, the ones with the least standing.

This chapter is about the pattern. It looks at why God behaves this way, what it accomplishes, and what it demands of the young person who finds himself inside it.

### The text that names the principle

The principle is named most directly in a verse that is stranger than it looks.

*Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings hast thou ordained strength because of thine enemies, that thou mightest still the enemy and the avenger* (Psalm 8:2).

*Babes and sucklings.* The Hebrew is *'olelim we-yonqim* — the first is a word for a child at the age of babbling, the second for an infant still nursing. Together they describe the very beginning of human life: a creature that cannot speak and cannot walk and cannot feed itself.

*Hast thou ordained strength.* The noun is *'oz*, and the verb is *yasad*, which means to found, to establish, to lay a foundation. God has *laid a foundation* of strength in the mouth of the weakest class of beings that exists.

*Because of thine enemies.* The reason is given, and it is a reason that concerns God's opponents. *That thou mightest still the enemy and the avenger.* The Hebrew verb for *still* is *shabath* — the verb that gives us the Sabbath. To *shabath* something is to cause it to cease, to bring it to rest, to put an end to its activity.

So the psalm is making an astonishing claim: **God establishes a foundation of strength out of the mouths of infants, and the purpose of it is to shut down His enemies.**

Why would that shut down an enemy? Because an enemy can be defeated by power and contend with power. What an enemy cannot do is lose to a nursing child. That is a defeat which leaves no category in which to lodge a rebuttal.

The psalm has been used for three thousand years as a hymn of wonder at the dignity of man — *What is man, that thou art mindful of him?* (8:4) — and that is a proper use of it. But the second verse is a strategic statement before it is a devotional one. It tells us the *method* God uses against opposition, and it tells us that the method involves the weakest possible instrument.

The New Testament takes up the verse twice, and both times it applies it to exactly this dynamic.

### The temple and the children

The first New Testament use is a scene of extraordinary tension, and Matthew places it at the end of the Lord's public ministry, in the last week.

Jesus has just entered Jerusalem to the shouting of the multitude (Matthew 21:9), has driven the money-changers out of the temple (21:12–13), and has healed the blind and the lame in the temple courts (21:14). And then:

*And when the chief priests and scribes saw the wonderful things that he did, and the children crying in the temple, and saying, Hosanna to the son of David; they were sore displeased, And said unto him, Hearest thou what these say? And Jesus saith unto them, Yea; have ye never read, Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings thou hast perfected praise?* (Matthew 21:15–16).

Set out the parties.

**The chief priests and scribes.** The official religious leadership of the nation. Trained, ordained, credentialed, and institutionally responsible.

**The children.** The Greek word is *paidas*, children. They are in the temple, and they are shouting the same acclamation the crowd had shouted on the road — *Hosanna to the son of David*, which is a Messianic claim.

**And the leaders were *sore displeased*.** The Greek verb *aganakteo* denotes strong indignation. Note what they are displeased about. Matthew says they saw *the wonderful things* that Jesus did — and then they were displeased at what the children said. The miracles did not settle the question for them; the children's acclamation is what provoked them.

And their question to Jesus is loaded: *Hearest thou what these say?* It is not an enquiry. It is an appeal for correction. *Are you going to let them say that? Do you not know who they are? Do you endorse this?*

And Jesus answers by quoting Psalm 8:2 and applying it to the children standing in front of Him. In His citation the Greek word for *ordained strength* becomes *perfected praise* — the Septuagint's rendering of the psalm, which translates *'oz* by *ainos*, praise. And He addresses it directly to the leaders: *have ye never read*.

The construction of the moment is perfect. The custodians of the temple, who had read Psalm 8 all their lives and had it sung in their liturgies, are asked whether they have ever *read* it — and are told that the verse is being fulfilled in their own building, by the mouths of children, as a rebuke to their displeasure.

**The praise that silenced the objection was not the trained praise of the Levitical choir.** It was the untutored shouting of children who had heard the crowd on the road and were still saying it in the temple courtyard.

### The hiding from the wise and the revealing to babes

And the Lord stated the principle explicitly, not as a quotation but as a declaration of His own practice.

*At that time Jesus answered and said, I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes. Even so, Father: for so it seemed good in thy sight* (Matthew 11:25–26).

And Luke, recording the same saying, adds a detail that Matthew does not:

*In that hour Jesus rejoiced in spirit, and said, I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes: even so, Father; for so it seemed good in thy sight* (Luke 10:21).

*In that hour Jesus rejoiced in spirit.* The revelation of the Father's method produced joy in the Son. This is not a lament about the failure of the educated; it is a thanksgiving for the wisdom of God's chosen distribution.

Look at the contrast: *wise and prudent* against *babes*. The Greek for *prudent* is *sunetos*, which denotes people who are intelligent, able to put things together. These are not fools. They are the competent. They are the ones with the qualifications and the analytical capacity to evaluate a claim.

And God hides from them. Not because intelligence is a sin, but because He has chosen a distribution that rules out a specific outcome — namely, anybody boasting that they worked it out.

And then He reveals to *babes* — *nepiois*, the same word Paul uses for the immature in 1 Corinthians 3:1 and the author of Hebrews in 5:13. It is not always a term of praise in the New Testament. Here it means the ones without standing, without training, without the apparatus of discernment. And to them, the things hidden from the competent are revealed.

Why would God do that? The answer comes from Paul.

### The argument of 1 Corinthians 1

Paul's argument to the Corinthians is a systematic exposition of this principle, and it is one of the most radical passages in the New Testament on the subject of who gets chosen.

*For ye see your calling, brethren, how that not many wise men after the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble, are called* (1 Corinthians 1:26).

Note Paul's precision. He does not say *none*. He says *not many*. The point is not that God excludes the wise, the powerful or the well-born. The point is proportion: the called community does not, as a class, consist of those categories. And the Corinthians do not need to be told this; they can look around the congregation.

Then the reasoning:

*But God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise; and God hath chosen the weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty; And base things of the world, and things which are despised, hath God chosen, yea, and things which are not, to bring to nought things that are* (1 Corinthians 1:27–28).

Set it out as a table, because Paul is constructing an argument by deliberate opposition.

| God has chosen | To bring to nought |
|---|---|
| the foolish (*mora*) | the wise (*sophous*) |
| the weak (*asthene*) | the mighty (*ischyra*) |
| the base (*agenē*) | — |
| the despised (*exouthenēmena*) | — |
| things which are not (*ta mē onta*) | things that are (*ta onta*) |

The final pair is the sharpest. *Things which are not.* Paul uses the language of ontology for people the world regards as having no existence. And God has chosen them *to bring to nought things that are* — the verb is *katargeo*, to render inoperative, to reduce to nothing.

And the purpose clause, which is the whole reason for the method: *That no flesh should glory in his presence* (1 Corinthians 1:29).

**This is the answer to the question.** God selects the unexpected instrument so that the outcome cannot be attributed to the instrument. Every component of a victory that could be credited to human capacity has been removed in advance, and what is left is an outcome that has to be credited to God.

Which means the preference for the young is not a compliment to the young. **It is a strategy for eliminating every alternative explanation for what God does.**

### The pattern across the canon

And now let us survey the pattern, because once it is seen it is everywhere, and its persistence is the proof that it is a design rather than a coincidence.

**Isaac over Ishmael.** The son of the promise comes through Sarah's barrenness, thirty-five years after the promise, when the natural capacity was gone (Genesis 21:1–2).

**Jacob over Esau.** The younger twin receives the birthright and the blessing; and the oracle given before the birth is explicit: *the elder shall serve the younger* (Genesis 25:23).

**Judah over Reuben.** The birthright, forfeited by the eldest (Genesis 35:22; 49:3–4), passes through the fourth son of Leah and a daughter-in-law, in a narrative of deliberate scandal (Genesis 38).

**Joseph the eleventh son.** Sold by his brothers, imprisoned by his master, forgotten by the butler — and made prime minister at approximately thirty (Genesis 41:46).

**Ephraim over Manasseh.** And here we must look closely, because the deliberate crossing of the hands in Genesis 48 is the moment the principle becomes an enacted sign. Joseph, Egyptianised and expectant, brings his sons in the correct order and places Manasseh the firstborn at Jacob's right hand. And: *But his father refused, and said, I know it, my son, I know it: he also shall become a people, and he also shall be great: but truly his younger brother shall be greater than he* (Genesis 48:19).

Jacob, who had himself been the younger, deliberately crossed his hands. He did it with his eyes failing and his body failing and his understanding fully intact.

**Moses over Egypt.** The deliverer is drawn out of the river as an infant, raised in the household of the oppressor, and returns at eighty years of age — which is to say, after the entire prime of his life had been spent in the wilderness and the palace of the enemy.

**Joshua over Caleb.** Both are faithful spies, both are promised the land. The naming of the successor falls on Joshua, Moses' attendant, who *departed not out of the tabernacle* (Exodus 33:11).

**Gideon over the tribe of Manasseh.** *Behold, my family is poor in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house* (Judges 6:15).

**Jephthah the son of a harlot** (Judges 11:1).

**Samuel the child.** Already treated at length in this book.

**David the youngest of eight.** Not one of the seven brothers first presented to the prophet (1 Samuel 16:10–11).

**Elijah the Tishbite** — *the strangers that sojourned in Gilead*, and the word for *sojourner* is the same used for a resident alien (1 Kings 17:1).

**Elisha the ploughman.**

**Amos the sycomore-dresser.** And his own account of the matter is one of the clearest statements of the principle in the Old Testament:

*Then answered Amos, and said to Amaziah, I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: And the Lord took me as I followed the flock, and the Lord said unto me, Go, prophesy unto my people Israel* (Amos 7:14–15).

*I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son.* This is a double disclaimer: no personal standing, and no dynastic standing. He is not in the prophetic guild and he is not the son of a prophet. He is scratching sycamore figs so that the fruit will ripen for the poor.

**Esther the orphaned exile.** A young woman with no father and no mother, in a foreign court, with no standing in the empire's power structure (Esther 2:7).

**Jeremiah the priest from a disgraced house.**

**Daniel the teenage captive.**

**Hosea, Joel, Micah, Zephaniah, Zechariah, Malachi** — nearly all of the writing prophets, called from outside the power structures.

**The twelve disciples.** Fishermen, a tax collector, a zealot, men described in Acts 4:13 in the very terms the principle requires: *Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were unlearned and ignorant men, they marvelled.* The Greek phrase is *aggrammatoi eisin kai idiotai* — unlettered and common. And the officers who came to arrest Jesus returned empty-handed and reported: *Never man spake like this man* (John 7:46).

**Mary of Nazareth.** A young woman of a village of no consequence, in a family with no recorded standing: her consent is obtained, and her song, sung before the birth, states the principle as a fixed rule of God's governance: *He hath shewed strength with his arm; he hath scattered the proud in the imagination of their hearts. He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree* (Luke 1:51–52).

**And the Lord himself** — born to a woman whose betrothal produced a question, in a stable, in a town so insignificant that a future disciple asked, *Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth?* (John 1:46).

There is no period of Scripture in which the pattern is absent. It is the ordinary mechanism of God's work.

### The motif of the child in Matthew

The Gospel of Matthew sustains the theme with unusual care, and three incidents in particular repay attention.

**The little child set in the midst.** The disciples ask who is greatest in the kingdom of heaven, and Jesus calls a child, sets him in the middle of the group, and says: *Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. Whosoever therefore shall humble himself as this little child, the same is greatest in the kingdom of heaven* (Matthew 18:3–4).

The Greek word for *little child* is *paidion*, a diminutive. In the social world of first-century Palestine, a child had no status, no rights, and no claim. And the Lord makes that position the condition of entry.

**The children brought for blessing.** *Then were there brought unto him little children, that he should put his hands on them, and pray: and the disciples rebuked them. But Jesus said, Suffer little children, and forbid them not, to come unto me: for of such is the kingdom of heaven* (Matthew 19:13–14).

Note who does the rebuking. The disciples — the inner circle, the future leadership of the church — take it upon themselves to protect the Lord from an unworthy interruption. And it is the disciples who are corrected. *Suffer* is *aphete*, let them alone, permit them. The institutional instinct to screen access was already operating among the twelve, and it was rebuked on the spot.

**And the praise at the entry.** *Out of the mouth of babes* — the verse He chooses to answer the chief priests with. Of all the texts He could have cited in that confrontation, He selects the one that establishes His own method.

### The lad with the loaves

And then there is the incident that is almost a parable of the whole principle, and it is easy to read past.

*There is a lad here, which hath five barley loaves, and two small fishes: but what are they among so many?* (John 6:9).

The speaker is Andrew, and his tone is one of despair — the question *what are they among so many?* is rhetorical and expects the answer *nothing*. A lad — *paidarion*, a boy — has a lunch of barley bread, the cheapest grain, and two small fish, probably salted and dried.

And the Lord takes the boy's lunch, blesses it, distributes it, and feeds five thousand men plus the women and children, with twelve baskets of fragments left over (John 6:10–13).

Now note several things about the miracle that its reputation as a story about sharing tends to obscure.

**The crowd was fed by a boy's provisions, not by the disciples' resources.** The twelve had already done the calculation: *Two hundred pennyworth of bread is not sufficient for them, that every one of them may take a little* (John 6:7). Their estimate was 200 denarii, roughly two hundred days' wages, and Philip was right that it would not have been enough. The disciples had the budget analysis and no food. The boy had no analysis and all the food there was.

**The boy is not named.** He appears in the narrative without a name, without a family, without a history. His sole contribution to the Gospels is a lunch, and the lunch becomes the meal of a multitude.

**And the Lord did not create bread from nothing, as He could have done.** He could have summoned bread from the ground, as He had turned water to wine. Instead He chose to work *through* the inadequate provision of a child, and the multiplication began with something a boy was carrying.

That is the pattern in miniature, and it is the most concentrated illustration of it in the New Testament. The Lord took what the institution had not counted and the boy had not valued, and fed the crowd with it.

### The little maid in the house of Naaman

And there is a further instance from the Old Testament which deserves its own treatment, because it is one of the most remarkable stories in 2 Kings and it is rarely examined as the statement it is.

*And the Syrians had gone out by companies, and had brought away captive out of the land of Israel a little maid; and she waited on Naaman's wife. And she said unto her mistress, Would God my lord were with the prophet that is in Samaria! for he would recover him of his leprosy* (2 Kings 5:2–3).

Consider the elements with great care, because each one matters.

**She is a captive.** She was taken in a raid by the very army that employs Naaman. She is in Syria because soldiers came into Israel and carried children away. She is a victim of the man she serves.

**She is a servant.** The Hebrew word is *qetannah*, a small girl, and she is *before* Naaman's wife — attending her, at her disposal.

**She is a slave in the household of the enemy of her people.** Which means she had every moral warrant to hate Naaman and everything he stood for, and no conceivable reason to wish him well.

**And she speaks.** Her words describe the prophet in Samaria as *the* prophet — *with the prophet that is in Samaria* — which means she knows there is one, and evidently knows something of what he does. And her wish is for the healing of the man who had helped to destroy her home.

The consequences of her one sentence are the whole of 2 Kings 5. Naaman goes to the king of Syria, and the king writes a letter to the king of Israel, and the king of Israel tears his clothes, and Elisha intervenes, and Naaman is cleansed of leprosy, and Naaman confesses, *Behold, now I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel* (2 Kings 5:15).

The healing of the commander of the army of Israel's greatest enemy, and his confession of the God of Israel as the only God in the earth, are the direct result of a sentence spoken by a kidnapped girl in a domestic interior.

**There was no prophet in Syria. There was a slave girl who knew where one was.** And the word of the Lord reached Damascus through her.

This is what the pattern looks like at its most extreme, and it establishes the point the whole chapter is making. The delivery mechanism God chose for one of the great moments of revelation to the Gentiles was not a delegation, a diplomatic mission, or the prophetic office. It was a nameless child in the wrong country, in the service of the wrong master, who told the truth about a man she had heard of.

### Why God does it this way

We are now in a position to state the reasons, and Scripture gives us several.

**First, so that no flesh should glory in His presence.** Paul states it as the purpose clause of the whole argument (1 Corinthians 1:29). If the instrument is capable, the instrument will be praised. God is not willing to share the credit for His own work, and the most efficient way to secure that is to select instruments whose contribution cannot be mistaken for the cause.

**Second, because it demonstrates what God is able to do and therefore what He deserves.** The doxology produced by the exodus is greater than the doxology that would have been produced by a negotiated release. Egypt's power had to be broken publicly, and it had to be broken by a shepherd with a staff.

**Third, because the neglected categories are the ones with unspent capacity.** Those who have been given everything have usually spent it. The ones who have been given nothing have been kept. This is why God says of Moses' descendants in the tribe of Levi that He *is their inheritance* (Numbers 18:20), and why the prophets come from outside the establishment.

**Fourth, because the young have time to be followed by the consequences of their own faithfulness.** God gives the long work to the young because the long work takes a lifetime. Moses at eighty still had forty years of the wilderness. Jeremiah's ministry ran forty years and more. Daniel served four kings. God is patient with centuries and impatient with nothing.

**Fifth, and most uncomfortably: because it is a judgment upon those who should have done it and did not.** When the children cried *Hosanna* in the temple, the chief priests were *sore displeased* — and their displeasure was itself the evidence that the office had been emptied of its function. The children had to cry it because the custodians would not. God's use of the young is not only a mercy to the young; it is a sentence on the old.

And that last point brings the whole pattern into focus. Every biblical instance of the Lord choosing an unexpected instrument is simultaneously a statement about His freedom and a mirror held up to human expectation. And the mirror is not flattering.

### The demand this places on the young

And now, because the pattern is true, the young person who finds himself inside it faces obligations that the pattern itself does not remove.

**The first is humility, and it is not optional.** If God chose you precisely because you cannot claim credit, then claiming credit forfeits the very thing you were chosen for. This is the trap that catches the young. They are told from the pulpit that God used David when nobody expected it, and they hear an endorsement of themselves rather than a revelation of God's method. The purpose clause — *that no flesh should glory in his presence* — is the one they leave out.

**The second is honour for those who did not see what you see.** David did not revile Saul; he cut off a corner of his robe and his heart smote him for that (1 Samuel 24:5). And when he had the chance a second time, he refused again, and would not permit his men to speak of killing the king (1 Samuel 26:9–11).

**The third is preparation, and it is measured in years.** Every person in the pattern above had a formation before a function. David had the lion and the bear. Moses had forty years in the palace and forty in Midian. Elisha had a decade of pouring water on his master's hands. Jeremiah had twenty-three years of unheeded preaching before the crisis arrived. The pattern selects the unexpected; it does not select the unprepared.

**And the fourth is patience with the institution.** This is the hardest of the four and it has almost no examples of success, which is itself instructive. The pattern does not license the young to despise the old. It requires them to serve the old while seeing what the old cannot see — which is precisely what Samuel did with Eli, what David did with Saul, and what Daniel did with four kings of Babylon.

### The word from Elihu

We should close this chapter with the man who says the thing most directly and who is himself the junior party in the argument.

Elihu, the fourth friend of Job, is introduced with an apology for speaking at all:

*And Elihu the son of Barachel the Buzite answered and said, I am young, and ye are very old; wherefore I was afraid, and durst not shew you mine opinion. I said, Days should speak, and multitude of years should teach wisdom. But there is a spirit in man: and the inspiration of the Almighty giveth them understanding. Great men are not always wise: neither do the old understand judgment* (Job 32:6–9).

Read that passage slowly, because it is the theological statement of this chapter and Elihu makes it while conceding everything the older men would want him to concede.

*I am young, and ye are very old.* He begins with a full acknowledgment of the facts.

*Wherefore I was afraid, and durst not shew you mine opinion.* He begins with the proper reticence. He did not presume.

*I said, Days should speak, and multitude of years should teach wisdom.* He begins with a statement of the conventional expectation — the one his elders held.

And then: *But there is a spirit in man: and the inspiration of the Almighty giveth them understanding.*

The word rendered *inspiration* is *neshamah*, the breath of life. The word rendered *understanding* is *bin*. Elihu's argument is that discernment is not a function of seniority but of the divine breath.

And then the conclusion, which is stated twice for emphasis: *Great men are not always wise: neither do the old understand judgment.*

The Hebrew is tighter than the translation suggests. *Lo rabim yachkimu* — the great are not necessarily wise. And: *zeqenim lo yabinu mishpat* — the aged do not necessarily perceive justice.

Elihu is not contemptuous and he is not triumphant. He is answering a man's suffering with a piece of theology that had been absent from the conversation for thirty-one chapters, and he does it while explicitly honouring the age of the men he is correcting.

And the fact that God later rebukes Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar — the three elders — while saying nothing against Elihu is the vindication of the youngest speaker in the book (Job 42:7).

That is the pattern in its mature form. Not the young man shouting down his elders. A young man who waited until the older men had finished, who acknowledged their years, and who then said the one thing none of them had said — because the Spirit who gives understanding is not bound to a birthday.

And that is the claim of this chapter, and of the pattern in Scripture it examines. God has bound Himself to no generation. He has bound Himself to a promise, and He will place it in whatever hand is available — including, and characteristically, a hand no one had thought to look at.
