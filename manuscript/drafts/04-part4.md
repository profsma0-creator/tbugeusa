## Why Elders Must Speak

There is a species of silence that looks exactly like wisdom and is in fact a decision. It is the silence of the older man who knows something the younger man does not, who sees a consequence approaching that the younger man cannot see, and who says nothing — because saying it would cost him something.

Scripture does not treat that silence as neutrality. It treats it as a transgression with a named consequence, and the consequence is measured in blood.

The book of Ezekiel is where the matter is stated in its most severe form, and the language of the watchman is the architecture of the whole doctrine.

### The watchman

*Son of man, I have made thee a watchman unto the house of Israel: therefore hear the word at my mouth, and give them warning from me* (Ezekiel 3:17).

The metaphor is drawn from city defence. A walled town posted a watchman on the wall, whose task was to watch the horizon and sound the trumpet when an army appeared. Everything about his function was of one piece.

**His position determined his knowledge.** He was on the wall because he could see further than anyone inside the gate. The merchants in the market could not see what he could see. This was not a matter of superiority; it was a matter of vantage.

**His knowledge created an obligation.** Having seen, he was required to speak. There was no category in which a watchman could see the dust cloud and then decide, on balance, that announcing it would cause a panic.

**And his silence did not change the outcome for the city — it changed the outcome for him.**

*But if the watchman see the sword come, and blow not the trumpet, and the people be not warned; if the sword come, and take any person from among them, he is taken away in his iniquity; but his blood will I require at the watchman's hand* (Ezekiel 33:6).

Read the sentence clause by clause, because the logic is exact.

*If the watchman see the sword come* — the condition is knowledge. Not diligence, not intention, not capacity. He saw.

*And blow not the trumpet* — the failure is silence, and it is a failure of a specific act, not of a disposition.

*And the people be not warned* — the consequence falls on the city, and the city suffers for its own iniquity. The text is careful to say that the person taken is *taken away in his iniquity*. His death is his own responsibility.

**And then: *but his blood will I require at the watchman's hand.***

That is the sentence. The man who died died for his own sin, and yet God holds the watchman accountable for the blood. The two liabilities are not in competition. The city's guilt does not cancel the watchman's, and the watchman's duty does not excuse the city.

**This is the single most important statement in Scripture about the duty of the older generation, and its severity has no equivalent in the language of modern institutional communication.**

And the alternative is stated plainly as well: *Nevertheless, if thou warn the wicked of his way to turn from it; if he do not turn from his way, he shall die in his iniquity; but thou hast delivered thy soul* (Ezekiel 33:9).

*Thou hast delivered thy soul.* The watchman's deliverance does not depend on whether the city repents. It depends on whether the trumpet was blown.

**The older man's duty is discharged by the act of speech, not by its reception.** This is the only arrangement under which the duty is bearable, because the reception is not in his control and the speech is.

### The law of the withheld witness

The principle is not confined to the prophets. It is present in the legal code of Leviticus, in a case law of great precision.

*And if a soul sin, and hear the voice of swearing, and is a witness, whether he hath seen or known of it; if he do not utter it, then he shall bear his iniquity* (Leviticus 5:1).

The setting is a public adjuration — a formal call for testimony, *the voice of swearing* — issued in a community where the facts of a matter were known to someone who could testify. And the law establishes that a person in that position, who hears the call and does not speak, *shall bear his iniquity*.

**The law recognises a category of sin that consists entirely of not saying something.** The man has done nothing. He has not lied, stolen, hurt anyone, or broken any prohibition. And he is guilty, and he must bring a trespass offering, because his silence has made him a participant in the wrong he declined to expose.

This is a principle the young need to understand about their elders, and it is a principle the old need to apply to themselves. **Silence in the presence of knowledge is not a neutral act. It is an act, and it is judged.**

### "Pure from the blood of all men"

And now the clearest statement of the doctrine in the New Testament, and it is made by the apostle Paul to a room full of the elders of the church at Ephesus.

Paul is at Miletus, on his way to Jerusalem, and he knows he will not see them again. He has called them to the shore to say a final word, and in the middle of that speech he makes a claim about himself that is impossible to read as a mere figure of speech.

*Wherefore I take you to record this day, that I am pure from the blood of all men. For I have not shunned to declare unto you all the counsel of God* (Acts 20:26–27).

Notice the structure.

**He claims to be *pure from the blood of all men*.** The phrase is the language of the watchman. Paul is applying Ezekiel 33 to himself, and he is stating that the liability described in that chapter does not attach to him.

**And he explains why:** *for I have not shunned to declare unto you all the counsel of God.*

Two words carry the weight.

*I have not shunned.* The Greek is *hypesteilamen* — I did not draw back, I did not conceal, I did not shrink from. The verb describes a withdrawal, a stepping back from something one has the capacity to do. Paul's claim is not that he said everything well, or that he said everything effectively. It is that he did not draw back from saying it.

*All the counsel of God.* The Greek is *pasan ten boulen tou Theou* — the whole counsel, the entire plan and purpose. Not the parts that were welcome. Not the parts that were safe. The whole thing.

**And Paul qualifies this claim with the fact that he said it *with tears*.**

*Therefore watch, and remember, that by the space of three years I ceased not to warn every one night and day with tears* (Acts 20:31).

Three years. *Every one*, individually. *Night and day*, without cessation. *With tears.*

So the man who claimed to be free of blood-guiltiness was a man who wept while he spoke, and who spoke to individuals as well as to crowds, and who did it for three years without stopping.

**That is the pattern the older generation is given.** The duty of speech is discharged with enormous emotional cost, at personal exposure, over long periods, in person.

And note that Paul immediately goes on to warn them about what is coming: *For I know this, that after my departing shall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock. Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them* (Acts 20:29–30).

*Of your own selves.* He tells the elders that the danger will come from among their own number. He says it to their faces, in his last meeting with them, and then he kneels down and prays with them all (20:36).

**The old man said the hard thing and then knelt with them and wept. He did not choose between the two.**

### "Necessity is laid upon me"

And there is a further dimension to the compulsion, stated by Paul in a context where he is defending his right to be paid for his ministry and where he makes a striking concession.

*For though I preach the gospel, I have nothing to glory of: for necessity is laid upon me; yea, woe is unto me, if I preach not the gospel!* (1 Corinthians 9:16).

*Necessity is laid upon me.* The Greek noun is *ananke* — compulsion, constraint, a necessity imposed by circumstance. And then the terrible conditional: *woe is unto me, if I preach not the gospel.*

**The apostle's own charitable act — preaching without pay — becomes an occasion for him to state that he could not have done otherwise.** His preaching was not a career, a choice, a preference, or a ministry he could have declined. It was a constraint, and the alternative was *woe*.

Which is the answer to every older man who has decided that he will not say the thing because it might cost him his position. **Paul had already given up the position.** The compulsion was prior to the sacrifice, and it survived it.

### The plea of ignorance

And there is one more text, from the Proverbs, and it closes the loophole that every silent elder eventually reaches for.

*If thou forbear to deliver them that are drawn unto death, and those that are ready to be slain; If thou sayest, Behold, we knew it not; doth not he that pondereth the heart consider it? and he that keepeth thy soul, doth not he know it? and shall not he render to every man according to his works?* (Proverbs 24:11–12).

The passage describes people *drawn unto death*, being carried away to slaughter, and the addresses a command to those who could intervene. And then it anticipates the defence that will be offered:

**"We knew it not."**

The Hebrew phrase is *hen lo yadanu zo* — behold, we did not know this. And the answer is a series of rhetorical questions, and they are devastating:

**He that pondereth the heart — does He not consider it?** The Hebrew is *tochen libboth* — He who weighs the hearts. The claim of ignorance is assessed by the one who weighs motives, not by the one who makes the claim.

**He that keepeth thy soul — does He not know it?** God is the keeper of the very soul that is offering the excuse.

**And shall not he render to every man according to his works?**

**The plea of ignorance is a plea about the interior state, and the interior state is precisely what God is examining.** An elder who says *I did not know* is asking to be judged on his own account of his own mind — and the Proverbs text says that account will not be the one used.

### The five mechanisms of silence

And now we must examine how the silence actually happens in a functioning institution, because no older man decides one morning to be a coward. The process is gradual, plausible, and supported by excellent reasons.

**The first mechanism is the vocabulary of timing.** *It is not the right moment. The time will come. You must choose your moment.* This is the most effective of the five, because it contains a genuine truth. There are times when speech is premature and a word spoken a year early does nothing. But the mechanism is diagnostic in one respect: **a concern with timing that never produces a time is not a concern with timing.** A man who has been waiting for the right moment for six years has not found the right moment; he has decided not to speak.

**The second is the language of peace.** *We must not divide the house. Let us keep the unity.* The New Testament does command peace and unity. And the New Testament never uses them as a reason to refrain from saying what is true. Christ said: *Think not that I am come to send peace on earth: I came not to send peace, but a sword* (Matthew 10:34). The peace that is preserved by not telling the truth is a peace that is purchased with the blood of the people who are not being warned.

**The third is the false virtue of prayer.** *I am praying about it. I will wait on the Lord.* Prayer is commanded, and one does not say the thing without having said it to God first. **But there is a form of waiting on God that is simply postponement with a religious name.** Jeremiah said: *His word was in mine heart as a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was weary with forbearing, and I could not stay* (Jeremiah 20:9). That is what waiting on God looks like when the word is actually given. It does not look like a settled silence.

**The fourth is the theology of inevitability.** *God will raise up someone. The Lord will vindicate the truth in His time.* This sounds like faith and functions as absolution. It is worth asking, whenever this argument is used: **if God intends to speak through someone, why not through you?** And the answer is usually that the speaker has already decided, on other grounds, not to be the one.

**The fifth is fear, and it is the actual cause of the other four.** Fear of losing income, position, seniority, friendship, family standing, a mother's good opinion, or the possibility of a quiet retirement. And Scripture names this plainly and does not dignify it. *The fear of man bringeth a snare* (Proverbs 29:25). *Be not afraid of their faces: for I am with thee to deliver thee, saith the Lord* (Jeremiah 1:8).

**Note the last of those, and its context. It is addressed to a young man, at his commissioning, and the fear it names is not the fear of a fate worse than death. It is the fear of faces.** That is the whole of it. The fear that keeps an older man silent is the fear of the expressions on the faces of people he knows.

### What must be said before the end

And this brings us to the constructive half of the chapter. If the older generation has a duty to speak, what is the content of the speech? Scripture gives a shape to this in the closing speeches of its great men, and the shape is consistent.

Consider the final addresses in the canon, and note what each one contains:

**Jacob, to his twelve sons (Genesis 49).** A blessing and a prediction, and the two are not separated. He tells each son what he is and what will come of him — including the terrible verdicts on Reuben, Simeon and Levi. He does not omit the bad chapters from the family record he delivers.

**Moses, to Israel on the plains of Moab (Deuteronomy).** A recitation of what God has done, a restatement of the law, a statement of blessings and curses, the appointment of Joshua, a written copy of the law deposited with the priests, and a song. The full content of the covenant, delivered by a man of a hundred and twenty who knew he would not enter the land.

**Joshua, to the assembled tribes at Shechem (Joshua 23–24).** A history of what God has done, a specific list of the ways they could fail, and then a demand that they choose: *choose you this day whom ye will serve* (24:15).

**Samuel, at Saul's coronation and again at his retirement (1 Samuel 12).** An account of his own stewardship, an invitation to be challenged on it — *witness against me before the Lord ... whose ox have I taken?* — and a warning about the consequences of the kingship they had demanded.

**David, to Solomon (1 Kings 2:1–4; 1 Chronicles 28–29).** A charge to keep the law of God, the plans and provisions for the temple handed over in writing, a public ceremony of transfer, and a private list of unfinished business.

**Paul, to the Ephesian elders (Acts 20).** A review of his ministry among them, the claim of blood-guiltlessness, a warning about wolves from outside and men from inside, the appointment of those who will carry on, and a final prayer.

**And Peter, in his second letter (2 Peter 1:12–15).** Which we must examine in detail, because it is the most theologically precise account of why an old man speaks.

*Wherefore I will not be negligent to put you always in remembrance of these things, though ye know them, and be established in the present truth. Yea, I think it meet, as long as I am in this tabernacle, to stir you up by putting you in remembrance; knowing that shortly I must put off this my tabernacle, even as our Lord Jesus Christ hath shewed me. Moreover I will endeavour that ye may be able after my decease to have these things always in remembrance* (2 Peter 1:12–15).

Set out what Peter says, because it is a complete theology of the older generation's speech.

**He says that his hearers already know it.** *Though ye know them, and be established in the present truth.* The apostle is not proposing to give them new information. This matters enormously, because it removes the most common excuse for not speaking: *they already know this.* **Peter's answer is that knowledge is not the same as remembrance, and that the difference is the whole reason he speaks.**

**He says he will not be negligent.** The Greek is *ouk ameleso* — I will not be careless, I will not be neglectful. The word carries the sense of failing to do something that ought to be done, and it is the same root Paul uses when he tells Timothy, *Neglect not the gift that is in thee.* Peter is stating that silence would be a form of negligence.

**He calls his speech *stirring up*.** The Greek is *diegeirein* — to wake fully, to rouse from sleep. His purpose is not instruction but arousal. The older man's task is to wake people up to what they already believe.

**And the measure he gives for how long to continue is his own life.** *As long as I am in this tabernacle.* The word is *skenoma* — a tent, a temporary dwelling, the kind of accommodation a traveller uses. Peter describes his body as a tent and his death as *the putting off* of it, and he takes the fact that the tent is coming down as the reason to speak now.

**And the purpose clause is the charter of every successor's inheritance.** *That ye may be able after my decease to have these things always in remembrance.* The Greek is *meta ten emen exodon* — after my departure, my exodus. Peter writes so that the truth will remain available to them when he is gone.

**He is arranging for his own absence to be survivable.** That is what an old man's final speech does. It deposits into the younger generation a body of remembrance that will function after the voice is no longer there to say it.

### The counter-example: a father who never said "Why hast thou done so?"

And now we must set beside this the two most instructive failures in Scripture, because they are both failures of the fathers, and both are recorded with painful brevity.

**The first is Eli**, whose silence is described in a sentence that became the warning of this whole book: *For I have told him that I will judge his house for ever for the iniquity which he knoweth; because his sons made themselves vile, and he restrained them not* (1 Samuel 3:13).

**The second is David**, and the verse is one of the most frightening in the Old Testament.

*Then Adonijah the son of Haggith exalted himself, saying, I will be king: and he prepared him chariots and horsemen, and fifty men to run before him. And his father had not displeased him at any time in saying, Why hast thou done so? and he also was a very goodly man; and his mother bare him after Absalom* (1 Kings 1:5–6).

Set out what the text tells us, because every clause is doing work.

**Adonijah exalted himself.** The Hebrew is *mithnasse* — he lifted himself up. He is self-appointed. Nobody anointed him; nobody nominated him; he has decided.

**He prepared him chariots and horsemen, and fifty men to run before him.** He is reproducing the apparatus of a royal procession. This is not a private ambition; it is a public claim, staged in front of the court and the city.

**And the sentence that explains it: *And his father had not displeased him at any time in saying, Why hast thou done so?***

The Hebrew is *velo 'atsabo aviv mi-yamav lemor maddua kakhah 'asitha* — and his father had never pained him in all his days, saying, Why have you done this?

**David, a man after God's own heart, a warrior, a psalmist, and the greatest king of Israel, had never once in his son's life asked him why he had done something.**

That is the whole failure, compressed into a clause. The father's objection was never registered, so the son's self-assessment was never corrected, and by the time the son was preparing to seize the throne, the father was old and cold and unable to stop him.

**And note the second clause: *and he also was a very goodly man*.** The Hebrew is *tov-to'ar me'od* — very good in appearance. The same phrase used of David himself as a young man (1 Samuel 16:12). Adonijah was handsome and impressive, and David had been a handsome and impressive young man, and it may be that the father saw himself in the son and could not bring himself to be the obstacle.

**This is the most common failure of the older generation in Scripture, and it is not contempt. It is affection without truth.** A father who will not displease his son is not being kind; he is deferring a cost, and the cost will be paid later, at interest, by the son.

And the consequence is immediate and terrible. Adonijah's attempt is made, the court divides, Nathan and Bathsheba have to manoeuvre to secure Solomon's succession, and Solomon is crowned in haste at Gihon while Adonijah is feasting at En-rogel (1 Kings 1:5–9, 32–40). Within a short time Adonijah is executed (1 Kings 2:24–25) — and David, on his deathbed, has to instruct Solomon to do what he had never been willing to do himself.

**The old man's unfinished business became the young man's first crisis.**

### What must be said, in four categories

And this gives us the content of the older generation's duty. Scripture's final speeches fall into four categories, and a father should be able to deliver all four before he dies.

**First: what you know about God that they do not.** This is the deposit — the accumulated knowledge of God's character, His dealings, His faithfulness, that is only available to someone who has walked with Him for decades. *I have been young, and now am old; yet have I not seen the righteous forsaken, nor his seed begging bread* (Psalm 37:25). That sentence can only be said by an old man. It is the report of a lifetime of observation, and it cannot be transmitted to anyone who has not been told.

**Second: what you have learned by failure.** This is the hardest to give and the most valuable to receive. Scripture records the failures of its own heroes with a frankness that is otherwise unknown in ancient literature, and the reason is that the failures were meant to be handed on. David's dying instruction to Solomon includes the counsel to deal firmly with Joab and Shimei (1 Kings 2:5–9) — a list of unfinished business that David had allowed to stand for decades. The old man who tells you only his victories has given you nothing that will keep you in a crisis.

**Third: what you see coming.** The watchman's function. Paul told the Ephesian elders about wolves from outside and men from inside (Acts 20:29–30). Samuel told Israel what a king would take from them (1 Samuel 8:11–18). Moses told the tribes what would happen if they turned aside (Deuteronomy 31:16–21). **The old have the vantage to see the consequences of the young's decisions, and they owe the warning whether or not it will be heard.**

**And fourth: what you believe about them.** Jacob blessed his sons; Moses blessed the tribes; Paul told Timothy *I have no man likeminded* (Philippians 2:20). **The older generation owes the younger an explicit statement of what they see in them.** This is the element most often omitted, and its omission is the reason so many young people spend their lives uncertain whether they were ever regarded as anything.

### Principles

Let me draw the chapter together.

**First: silence in the presence of knowledge is a transgression, not a neutral act.** Ezekiel 33 attaches blood-guiltiness to the failure to blow the trumpet. Leviticus 5:1 makes the withholding of testimony an offering-requiring sin. Proverbs 24:12 closes the plea of ignorance. The older man who knows and does not speak is not being diplomatic; he is bearing an iniquity.

**Second: the duty is discharged by the speech, not by the outcome.** *If thou warn the wicked ... thou hast delivered thy soul.* The watchman is not responsible for whether the city repents. He is responsible for the trumpet.

**Third: the speech is owed to individuals, at length, with tears.** Paul warned *every one night and day with tears* for three years. The generalised sermon that never names the person and the issue is a way of appearing to have spoken while saying nothing.

**Fourth: knowledge is not remembrance.** Peter's reason for speaking to people who already knew was that they needed to be stirred up to remember it. **The older generation's function is not the transmission of new information. It is the maintenance of what the young already have in memory.**

**Fifth: the older generation should arrange for the afterlife of its own voice.** Peter writes so that the church will have the truth *after my decease*. The book, the letter, the written record, the public charge — these are how a voice survives its owner.

**Sixth: affection without truth is a failure that mortgages the next generation's future.** David never asked Adonijah *Why hast thou done so?*, and his son attempted a coup. The old man's unfinished business becomes the young man's crisis.

**And seventh: the time for the speech is now, and the reason is the tent.** *As long as I am in this tabernacle.* Peter reasons from the imminence of his death to the urgency of his speech. The older man who has not yet said the thing he knows is not waiting on God; he is counting on a time that has not been promised.

### For the fathers

- **Make a list of the things you have not said.** Not the things you have not said well — the things you have not said at all. Name them. The list is usually shorter and more specific than the impression of vague unease.
- **Distinguish between a word that has been postponed and a word that has been declined.** If you have been waiting for the right moment for more than one year, you have declined. Say it this month.
- **Speak to persons, not to atmospheres.** The general warning that names no one has the appearance of courage and the effect of silence. If the matter concerns a man, go to the man.
- **Write it down.** Peter's solution to his own approaching death was a letter. If there is something you want the next generation to have after you are gone, put it in a document and give it to them while you can still be asked questions about it.
- **Answer the question David never asked.** Ask your sons and daughters why they did what they did. It is uncomfortable and it is the mechanism by which their self-assessment is corrected.
- **Say what you believe about them out loud.** Find the young men and women around you and tell them what you see in them. Most of them have never been told.

### For the young

- **Do not confuse your elders' silence with consent.** A man who does not object to what you are doing is not necessarily approving it. He may have decided that the cost of the conversation is higher than the cost of your mistake.
- **Go and ask for the word.** The duty of speech in Scripture is matched by a duty of enquiry. *Ask thy father, and he will shew thee; thy elders, and they will tell thee* (Deuteronomy 32:7). The young who never ask have no standing to complain that they were never told.
- **Ask specific questions.** "What do you think of my life?" produces nothing. "What is the one thing about me that will cause me trouble in ten years?" produces a great deal.
- **Receive the correction without defending it in the same breath.** The instinct to explain yourself while being corrected destroys the value of the correction, because the older man learns what it costs to speak to you and adjusts accordingly.
- **Do not despise a warning that turns out to be wrong.** The elder who warns you about something that does not happen was still doing his job. Punishing him for the false alarm guarantees that the next alarm will not be sounded.

### Questions for reflection

1. Who in your life is a watchman for you — someone placed where they can see what you cannot? When did they last sound the trumpet?
2. Have you withheld something you know from a younger person because saying it would cost you a relationship? What is the actual cost of each option?
3. Peter wrote to people who already knew. What do the people under your care already know and no longer remember?
4. Is there a person from whom you would refuse to hear a warning — a father, a mentor, an elder you have written off? On what basis was that verdict reached?
5. If you were writing a letter to be read after your departure, what are the first three sentences?
