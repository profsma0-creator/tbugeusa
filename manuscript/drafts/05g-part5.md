## The Fathers Must Not Become Childless in Vision

**The previous chapter addressed the danger that belongs to the young. This one addresses the danger that belongs to the older man, and it is the quieter of the two and the one that ends movements.**

**A young man who is fatherless is a tragedy in the making. An older man who is childless in vision is already a tomb, and he is frequently a well-attended one.**

**And the condition must be defined before it can be feared.** Childlessness of vision is not the absence of young people from a ministry. It is the absence of any design in the older man's work that extends beyond the length of his own life.

**A man can be surrounded by young men and be childless in vision, because he has gathered them and not fathered them, and because everything he is building requires him personally in order to function.**

**And the diagnostic question is not how many attend, but what continues when he stops.**

### Abram's complaint, and God's refusal of a substitute

And Scripture begins the whole subject with a man who was given a promise and had nobody to hand it to.

*And Abram said, Lord God, what wilt thou give me, seeing I go childless, and the steward of my house is this Eliezer of Damascus? And Abram said, Behold, to me thou hast given no seed: and, lo, one born in my house is mine heir. And, behold, the word of the Lord came unto him, saying, This shall not be thine heir; but he that shall come forth out of thine own bowels shall be thine heir* (Genesis 15:2–4).

**Consider the arrangement Abram was proposing, because it is the arrangement most religious institutions eventually adopt.**

**Eliezer was a steward, and the word is *ben mesheq beiti* — the son of the acquisition of my house, a servant who had been with him long enough to be trusted with everything.** He was competent, loyal, and probably the best available candidate.

**And Abram's reasoning was entirely sound by the standards of estate management.** He was old, the promise was unfulfilled, and a faithful steward was the practical solution. **The promise could be administered by a capable servant and no one would notice the difference.**

**And God refused it, in a sentence: *he that shall come forth out of thine own bowels shall be thine heir.***

**A steward can administer an estate. He cannot inherit a promise, because a promise is bound to a lineage and not to a competence.** And the Hebrew is emphatic about the source — out of your own body, your own substance, your own life.

**Which tells us the first thing about vision-childlessness: it cannot be solved by appointing a manager.**

**Every generation produces men who wish to secure the continuity of their work by finding a replacement and installing him.** And in the best cases, the replacement is faithful and able, and the work continues, and the form is preserved. **And what is not preserved is the thing that made the work worth doing, because a steward can hold the estate and cannot hold the faith.**

**And the practical significance is this: a man who has no spiritual children cannot acquire them by hiring.**

**An institution can transfer assets, titles, buildings, budgets and positions to a successor. And no institution can transfer a fatherhood it never had.** Which is why so many religious works preserve their structure and lose their life within a single generation, and why the loss is invisible on paper.

**And Abram's answer to God's refusal is instructive in its own right, because he did not simply accept the promise and go about his business.**

**He believed, and it was counted to him for righteousness (Genesis 15:6). And then he spent the next twenty-four years learning what the promise required of him, and the learning was not passive.** He had to send away Ishmael, the son of his own body and of his own devising, because a promise cannot be inherited by two lines. **The doctrine of Genesis 21 is that a father must be able to distinguish between the son he produced and the son he was given.**

**Which is a hard saying, and it belongs here, because the man who is building something mostly his own will find that the work produced by his own cleverness is the hardest thing in the world to give up.**

### The order in Abraham's will

And the practical arrangements Abraham made at the end of his life are recorded in two verses, and they are a masterclass in the deliberate settlement of a succession.

*And Abraham gave all that he had unto Isaac. But unto the sons of the concubines, which Abraham had, Abraham gave gifts, and sent them away from Isaac his son, while he yet lived, eastward, unto the east country* (Genesis 25:5–6).

**Three decisions are in those two sentences, and each of them is a decision most men never make.**

**The first is that one man received everything.** *All that he had unto Isaac.* Not a division, not a committee, not a plurality of heirs with equal weight. **A vision has one carrier at a time, and Abraham's estate was structured around that fact.**

**And it must be said that this is not a rule about property but a rule about inheritance of purpose.** A business may be divided among children with perfect justice. **A calling cannot be, because a calling is a direction and a direction has a point.**

**The second is that the other sons received provision.**

*Unto the sons of the concubines gave Abraham gifts.* He did not disinherit them, and he did not leave them destitute, and he did not pretend they were not his. **They were given generously and they were provided for, and they were not given the inheritance.**

**Which is the pattern for every older man with more young men than positions.** The men who are not the carrier are not to be discarded; they are to be equipped, blessed, and sent. **The failure to do this is one of the most fruitful sources of division in religious history: the sons who were not given the inheritance are left standing in the house with nothing to do, and they eventually demolish it.**

**And the third decision is the one that makes the other two effective, and it is the phrase *while he yet lived*.**

**Abraham settled the succession in his own lifetime.**

**He did not leave the question to be resolved after his death by the survivors.** He did not allow a period of uncertainty during which the strongest claimant could emerge. **He performed the transfer of the estate and the separation of the other sons while he had authority to do it, and he did it in the sight of everyone concerned.**

**And this is the single most neglected duty of the older man, and it is neglected because it costs him something to perform.** Settling a succession in your lifetime requires you to name the successor, to give him the substance of the authority, to send away the men who are not the successor with real gifts, and then to watch your own relevance diminish in real time.

**Which is why so much of the pain in religious succession happens after a funeral rather than before it.** The brothers do not fight while the father is alive. **They wait, because they know that the father cannot be confronted and cannot be contradicted, and because the conflict that would be unseemly now will be permitted later.**

**And Abraham's arrangement removes the occasion entirely.** He gave the inheritance, he gave the gifts, he sent the others away, and he did it with his own hand while he could still be disobeyed. **That is what it means for a father not to be childless in vision: his plans do not depend on his surviving.**

### "Enlarge the place of thy tent"

And the command given to a woman with no children is the clearest instruction in Scripture on the preparation required of a father who wants a posterity.

*Sing, O barren, thou that didst not bear; break forth into singing, and cry aloud, thou that didst not travail with child: for more are the children of the desolate than the children of the married wife, saith the Lord. Enlarge the place of thy tent, and let them stretch forth the curtains of thine habitations: spare not, lengthen thy cords, and strengthen thy stakes; for thou shalt break forth on the right hand and on the left; and thy seed shall inherit the Gentiles, and make the desolate cities to be inhabited* (Isaiah 54:1–3).

**Note the order of the instructions, because the sequence is a template.**

**First, she is commanded to sing before the children arrive.** *Break forth into singing ... thou that didst not travail with child.* The praise precedes the pregnancy. **And it is a praise that must be offered while the condition is still the condition, which is the definition of faith.**

**Second, she is told to enlarge the tent — before there is anyone to fill it.** *Enlarge the place of thy tent ... spare not, lengthen thy cords, and strengthen thy stakes.*

**Every one of those commands is construction work, and every one of them is unnecessary if no children are coming.** You enlarge a tent because of people who are not there yet. **You lengthen cords and strengthen stakes in preparation for capacity that the present moment does not require.**

**Which means that a father's vision for the next generation is visible in the size of what he builds relative to what he currently holds.**

**And the third instruction is the one that distinguishes enlargement from ambition: *strengthen thy stakes.***

**Cords and curtains are the expansion; the stake is the anchor.** And the instruction couples them, because an enlarged tent with weak stakes will collapse in the first wind. **This is the failure mode of ministries that grow faster than their foundations: the tent got bigger and the stakes stayed the same, and the whole structure is one storm from ruin.**

**And note the promise: *thy seed shall inherit the Gentiles, and make the desolate cities to be inhabited.***

**The children of the barren woman will occupy places that nobody has occupied.** And that is a statement about the nature of a spiritual inheritance in the New Testament era, because the places to be possessed are nations and cities and generations who were not anyone's natural children.

**So the father who is not childless in vision is the man who is building beyond his present occupancy, with strong stakes, in praise, before he can see the children.**

**And the man who is childless in vision is building exactly enough for himself, and no more, and he calls it prudence.**

### The name better than sons and daughters

And Scripture makes an explicit provision for a man who has no natural posterity at all, and the provision is the reason childlessness of vision is a sin of direction rather than a verdict of nature.

*For thus saith the Lord unto the eunuchs that keep my sabbaths, and choose the things that please me, and take hold of my covenant; even unto them will I give in mine house and within my walls a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters: I will give them an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off* (Isaiah 56:4–5).

**The eunuch, in the law of Israel, was the man who could not have children by definition.** He had no line, no inheritance in the land through issue, and no possibility of the thing Abraham was promised. **And God offers him a place and a name better than sons and daughters.**

**Note the three conditions attached: he must keep the sabbaths, choose the things that please God, and take hold of the covenant.** The promise is not made to the condition but to the covenant obedience exercised in the condition.

**And note the phrase *in mine house and within my walls.***

**The man with no children is offered a place in God's house, and the place is described as being inside walls — which is to say, near the centre rather than on the periphery.** And the name is *everlasting*, *lo yikkaret* — it shall not be cut off.

**Which means the desire for a legacy is not itself the problem.** The problem is what a man is willing to do to obtain one.

**Every older man feels the pull of *yikkaret* — the dread of being cut off, of being written out, of being forgotten.** And the two possible responses are stark and produce opposite lives.

**The first response is to secure the name by controlling the successors.** Such a man will not transfer anything until he is certain he will still be needed, and he will arrange his work so that his removal breaks it. **And he will be remembered, briefly, as a man who could not let go, and then he will be forgotten entirely.**

**And the second response is to secure the name by covenant obedience, and to spend the strength of his remaining years on people who will outlive him.** And this man's work is designed to continue without him, and the continuation is his memorial.

**And the promise states the outcome in a way that shuts down the possibility of loss: an everlasting name, in God's house, which shall not be cut off.**

### "Who knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool"

And now the fear that stops most men, stated by the man in Scripture who had the most to leave behind.

*Yea, I hated all my labour which I had taken under the sun: because I should leave it unto the man that shall be after me. And who knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool? yet shall he have rule over all my labour wherein I have laboured, and wherein I have shewed myself wise under the sun. This is also vanity. Therefore I went about to cause my heart to despair of all the labour which I took under the sun* (Ecclesiastes 2:18–21).

**Read it in three movements, because it is a diagnosis of the older man's heart done by an expert.**

**The first movement is the dread.** He looked forward and saw that everything he had built would pass into the hands of a man he could not control. **And the dread is legitimate, and every man who builds anything arrives at it.**

**The second movement is the reason for the dread, and it is a question: *who knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool?***

**The Hebrew is *chakham yihyeh o sakhal* — will he be wise, or will he be a fool?** And the terror is in the incompleteness of the question. **Solomon cannot know, and no father can, and the uncertainty is not caused by any deficiency of information that more diligence could repair.**

**And the third movement is the result: *I went about to cause my heart to despair of all the labour.***

**He despaired. And the despair was not a loss of faith in God; it was a withdrawal of investment in the future.** Which is what an older man does when he cannot guarantee the outcome: he stops putting anything into tomorrow, and he manages today instead.

**And this is the mechanism by which a vision grows childless in a man who is still building.** He looks at the uncertainty, and he reduces his risk by making his work depend on himself.

**And the remedy is not optimism and it is not a better successor.** Solomon's error was to compare what he left — *labour* — with what the next man might do with it, and to find the comparison unbearable. **And the difference in the New Testament is not that the uncertainty is resolved. It is that what is left is not labour.**

**Paul's summary at the end of his life is not a description of a man who despaired.**

*For I am now ready to be offered, and the time of my departure is at hand. I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith* (2 Timothy 4:6–7).

**He left a faith that had been kept, a course that had been finished, and letters that are still being read two thousand years later.** And the difference between him and Solomon is that he had spent the last years of his strength on men — Timothy, Titus, Luke, John Mark, and the churches of Asia — rather than on the accumulation of labour.

**Which is the whole of the matter. A man who invests only in the work must fear the successor. A man who invests in the successor has already made the succession and need not fear the man.**

### Peter's arrangement for after his decease

And the second epistle of Peter contains the most explicit statement of deliberate post-mortem planning in the New Testament, and it is the textual foundation of everything this book is doing.

*Wherefore I will not be negligent to put you always in remembrance of these things, though ye know them, and be established in the present truth. Yea, I think it meet, as long as I am in this tabernacle, to stir you up by putting you in remembrance; knowing that shortly I must put off this my tabernacle, even as our Lord Jesus Christ hath shewed me. Moreover I will endeavour that ye may be able after my decease to have these things always in remembrance* (2 Peter 1:12–15).

**Take the passage apart, because it is a plan and not a sentiment.**

**Verse 12: he will not be negligent *to put you always in remembrance of these things, though ye know them.*** The content is already known and catechetically established. **He is not adding information; he is building a mechanism for the ongoing recall of information already received.**

**Verse 13: *as long as I am in this tabernacle* — while he is alive.** The Greek is *skenomati*, tent, the same image as Isaiah's enlarged tent, and here it is a tent that will be put off. **He knows he is in a temporary structure and that the departure is scheduled.**

**Verse 14: *shortly I must put off this my tabernacle, even as our Lord Jesus Christ hath shewed me.*** He has been told about his death; the tradition is that the Lord's indication is in John 21:18–19, and Peter therefore knows the manner and nearness of the end.

**And verse 15 is the plan: *I will endeavour that ye may be able after my decease to have these things always in remembrance.***

**The Greek for *I will endeavour* is *spoudaso*, to make haste, to be diligent, to give earnest effort to a matter.** It is a working word, not a hoping word. **And the Greek for *decease* is *exodou* — his exodus.**

**So Peter's last recorded intention is to construct, in the time remaining, a means by which a body of believers will be reminded of the truth permanently after he is dead.**

**And what he produced was a letter.** Two of them, in fact, and the second one exists substantially because he knew he was about to die and wanted something that would outlast him.

**Which means the written deposit is not a lesser form of the oral deposit. It is a deliberate instrument of posthumous formation, invented for that purpose, by a man who arranged his own ending.**

**And this is why the canon exists, and why the letters are read aloud, and why this book you are holding is attempting the same thing in a slighter way.** A father who writes is not doing something inferior to a father who speaks. **He is doing the only thing that will still be speaking when he cannot.**

**And the action that follows in the epistle is the argument that the mechanism must be built before it is needed:**

*Moreover I will endeavour...* — the construction is happening while the man is alive and mobile and able to write. **A man who waits until his last week to arrange to be remembered is usually not arranging it at all.**

**Which is the practical content of this chapter, reduced to a command: write the thing now, name the man now, build the capacity now, and give the inheritance now, while you are still in the tent rather than after you have put it off.**

### Moses, and the vision that was not completed

And there is one case in Scripture where a faithful man was denied the completion of his own vision, and it is recorded with the quality of mercy in it.

*And the Lord said unto him, This is the land which I sware unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob, saying, I will give it unto thy seed: I have caused thee to see it with thine eyes, but thou shalt not go over thither* (Deuteronomy 34:4).

**Moses spent forty years in the wilderness leading a people toward a land he would not enter, and the refusal was a judgement incurred at Meribah, and the judgement had already been stated to him in Numbers 20:12.**

**And the mercy in the account is verse 1: *the Lord shewed him all the land.***

**He did not die without seeing it.** The instruction is *I have caused thee to see it with thine eyes* — a personal, deliberate showing, arranged for him on the day of his death. **God took the man up a mountain and gave him the vision in full.**

**Which means the incompletion of a vision is not the same as its failure.** Moses did not possess the land. **He saw it, he was shown it by God, and he handed the possession to a man who was standing beside him.**

**And the writer of Hebrews gives the theology of that arrangement, and it applies to every father who dies with work unfinished:**

*These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth* (Hebrews 11:13).

**Four verbs, and the third one is the one that matters most: they saw, they were persuaded, they embraced, they confessed.**

**The Greek for *embraced* is *aspasamenoi*, which is the word for greeting someone warmly at a distance — the embrace of a person who is still far off but who is certainly coming.** That is the act of a man who will not live to see it.

**And the closure of the chapter states the principle outright:**

*And these all, having obtained a good report through faith, received not the promise: God having provided some better thing for us, that they without us should not be made perfect* (Hebrews 11:39–40).

**The older generation is not completed without the younger, and the younger is not completed without the older.** *That they without us should not be made perfect.* **The generations are one body, and the perfection of the body is deferred until the last of them arrives.**

**Which removes the entire ground of the father's anxiety.** A man who dies with an unfinished work has not failed; **he has been placed in a story whose ending he is not supposed to see, and the ending is guaranteed by the God who showed him the land.**

### The servant's house and the son's house

And the writer of Hebrews draws a distinction between two kinds of household, and the distinction is exactly the question of this chapter.

*And Moses verily was faithful in all his house, as a servant, for a testimony of those things which were to be spoken after; but Christ as a son over his own house; whose house are we, if we hold fast the confidence and the rejoicing of the hope firm unto the end* (Hebrews 3:5–6).

**Both are faithful. Both are in the house. And one is a servant and the other is a son.**

**And note what the servant's faithfulness is *for*: *a testimony of those things which were to be spoken after.***

**Moses' administration was designed to testify about a later word.** The whole Mosaic arrangement was a pointer, and its fidelity consisted in pointing accurately at something that had not yet come. **And this is precisely the shape of a father who is not childless in vision: his work is explicitly constructed as a testimony about a future he will not administer.**

**And note the distinction again in the second clause: Christ is *a son over his own house*, and *whose house are we.* We are the house, and we hold it *if we hold fast the confidence and the rejoicing of the hope firm unto the end.***

**So the condition of the household is the perseverance of the members.** Which tells us that a house is not held by the competence of its founder and is not held by the strength of its structure. **It is held by the hope of those inside it, retained to the end.**

**And the application is direct. A father who builds a house of servants has built an institution. A father who builds a house of sons has built a family. The two look identical from outside, and only one of them survives his death.**

### A father's work is not finished at the birth

And Paul's language about the churches he planted supplies the last piece, and it corrects a very common error.

*My little children, of whom I travail in birth again until Christ be formed in you* (Galatians 4:19).

**The Greek is *odino*, to be in the pains of childbirth, and *palin*, again.** He had already travailed for their birth — *I have begotten you through the gospel* (1 Corinthians 4:15) — and now he is in labour a second time.

**So the first birth is not the end of the father's work; it is the beginning of it.**

**And the object of the second travail is stated: *until Christ be formed in you.***

**The Greek is *morphothe*, from *morphe*, the outward shape of a thing — and the word is used of the formation of a fetus in the womb.** Paul's labour continues until the shape of Christ is present in a person, and the shape of Christ is not produced at conversion; **it is produced by years of the kind of formation that the previous chapters of this Part have described.**

**And the word *again* tells us what had happened: they had regressed.** They were believers, and they were being turned by other teachers, and Paul's response was not to disown them but to go back into labour.

**Which is the disposition of a father rather than a leader.** A leader who loses his adherents recruits more. **A father whose children are relapsing enters the pain a second time.**

**And that is what childlessness of vision looks like from the inside.** A man can run a large and prosperous work and never once have travailed a second time over a person. **He can count attendances for decades without ever being in labour over the shape of Christ in a single soul.**

**And the test of whether a father's work was ever really paternal is whether he is willing to do the same work twice for the same person, at cost, without the credit of having recruited him.**

### The old man's prayer

And the psalm of the ageing worshipper gives the only reasonable request an older man can make of God once his strength is going.

*O God, thou hast taught me from my youth: and hitherto have I declared thy wondrous works. Now also when I am old and grayheaded, O God, forsake me not; until I have shewed thy strength unto this generation, and thy power to every one that is to come* (Psalm 71:17–18).

**The request is not for length of days, and it is not for a comfortable retirement. It is for strength sufficient to finish the telling.**

**And note the two objects of the telling: *this generation* and *every one that is to come.***

**He wants to speak to the people standing around him now, and he wants to speak to the ones who are not born.** The prayer is a father's prayer, and the *until* is the definition of a completed ministry.

**A ministry ends when the telling is finished, and not when the man is tired.** Which means an older man has a duty that only exists in his last years: to say the thing he has learned to the people who will need it after he has gone.

**And the man who is childless in vision has nobody to say it to.** Not because there is no one standing in front of him, but because he never built anything into the men around him, and therefore has no future audience.

### The Levites' second ministry

And the law of the Levites provides the shape for the later years, and it is one of the most humane provisions in the whole of Scripture.

*This is it that belongeth unto the Levites: from twenty and five years old and upward they shall go in to wait upon the service of the tabernacle of the congregation: and from the age of fifty years they shall cease waiting upon the service thereof, and shall serve no more: but shall minister with their brethren in the tabernacle of the congregation, to keep the charge, and shall do no service* (Numbers 8:24–26).

**Three stages, and every one of them is defined by Scripture.**

**From twenty-five, a man waits upon the service.** He carries, he portages, he does the physical work of the tabernacle. **This is the stage at which strength is the qualification.**

**From fifty, he ceases *waiting upon the service* and *shall do no service*.** The heavy work stops, and the cessation is commanded rather than permitted. **Which means an older man who refuses to relinquish the carrying work is disobeying the pattern rather than demonstrating zeal.**

**And in between there is the function that continues for the rest of his life: *he shall minister with his brethren in the tabernacle of the congregation, to keep the charge.***

**Two things remain his: the brethren, and the charge.**

**To keep the charge is to guard what has been entrusted — the deposit, the standard, the order of the house.** And to minister *with his brethren* is to be present among the serving men, not above them and not absent from them.

**So the older man's office, by the law, is precisely the office that prevents vision-childlessness. He stops performing the service and he begins keeping the charge, and he does it in the company of his brothers.**

**And the men who are still doing the service need exactly that.** They need a man who is not competing with them for the work, who knows what the deposit is, who is present in the house, and whose judgment has been formed by decades of carrying.

**And the man who will not move to the second stage forfeits both ministries.** He keeps performing and stops keeping the charge, and then he has no office left, because the only thing he was willing to hold was the carrying, and carrying requires strength that he no longer has.

### The four things that must not die with the father

**And the whole chapter reduces to four items, and each of them is something a father either transmits in his lifetime or takes to the grave.**

**The first is a name — a place in the house.** Isaiah 56:5, *a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters.* **The young man must know where he stands in the house before the father dies**, or the first thing he will do after the funeral is negotiate his position with whoever else is present.

**The second is a map — an explicit commission that extends past the father's lifespan.** Deuteronomy 34:4, *I have caused thee to see it with thine eyes, but thou shalt not go over thither*, followed immediately by the charge to Joshua in Joshua 1. **The successor must be told what he is to do with the remaining distance**, or he will spend his strength maintaining what the father built.

**The third is a mechanism — the arrangement by which the father is remembered after he is gone.** 2 Peter 1:15, *that ye may be able after my decease to have these things always in remembrance.* **A letter, a record, a written deposit, a house order, a named successor.** The mechanism is what allows the man to keep speaking when he cannot.

**And the fourth is a blessing — the spoken word that creates the capacity for the work.** The fathers in Hebrews blessed their sons *by faith*: Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau *concerning things to come* (Hebrews 11:20), and Jacob blessed the sons of Joseph, *worshipping, leaning upon the top of his staff* (Hebrews 11:21). **They spoke a future over men who had not earned it.**

**And note whom they blessed: men who had not earned it.** Isaac blessed the son who had deceived him, and Jacob blessed the sons who had sold their brother.

**Which is the final thing to be said about a father who is not childless in vision.** His blessing does not wait for the son to deserve it. **He speaks over the young man what the young man cannot yet be, and the speaking is part of how he becomes it.**

**And a man with no children has nobody to bless, and nobody to name, and nobody to send, and nobody to remember him — not because he lacked the capacity but because he never built the relationships in which the four things could be carried.**

### Principles

**First: vision-childlessness is the absence of any design that survives the man.** It is not the lack of young people. It is the lack of anything that continues when he stops, and it is compatible with a large and busy ministry.

**Second: a steward cannot inherit a promise.** *This shall not be thine heir; but he that shall come forth out of thine own bowels.* Institutions transfer offices; only fatherhood transfers inheritance.

**Third: settle the succession in your lifetime.** Abraham gave all to Isaac, gave gifts to the others, and sent them away *while he yet lived.* The conflicts that destroy religious works after a funeral are usually the ones that could have been resolved before it.

**Fourth: provide for the sons who are not the carrier, and send them with gifts.** The men who receive nothing but a place in the queue will eventually take the house apart.

**Fifth: enlarge the tent before the children arrive, and strengthen the stakes while doing it.** Isaiah 54 orders praise, then enlargement, then anchors. The man who builds only for his present occupancy has decided against a posterity.

**Sixth: God gives a place and an everlasting name to the man with no children at all.** Isaiah 56:5. The desire for a legacy is legitimate; the methods men use to secure it are usually not.

**Seventh: the terror of leaving labour to a fool is answered by leaving people instead of labour.** Solomon despaired over a work he could not control. Paul left a deposit, a doctrine, a pattern, and men — and they are still working.

**Eighth: a faithful ministry may end without the vision being completed, and that is not failure.** *I have caused thee to see it with thine eyes, but thou shalt not go over thither.* Moses saw and did not enter, and Hebrews calls him faithful.

**And ninth: what a father owes the young man is a name, a map, a mechanism and a blessing — and all four must be given while he is still alive to be contradicted.**

### For the fathers

- **Name what continues when you stop.** If the honest answer is nothing, you have a ministry and not a posterity, and the diagnosis is not the size of your congregation.
- **Settle the succession while you are alive.** Abraham gave the inheritance, gave the gifts, and sent the others away *while he yet lived*. Do not leave brothers to negotiate at your funeral what you could have decided at your table.
- **Provide for the men who are not the carrier.** Gifts and a sending, not a queue. The discarded sons of a house are the ones who eventually pull it down.
- **Enlarge the tent before you need the room, and strengthen the stakes while you do it.** Praise first, then capacity, then anchors — the order in Isaiah 54 is not optional.
- **Leave people, not labour.** Solomon despaired because he was leaving a work he could not control. Invest the last years in men, and the fear of the fool who inherits will lose its power over you.
- **Write the thing down now.** *I will endeavour that ye may be able after my decease...* Peter arranged to be remembered while he could still work. Whatever you know that the next generation will need, put it where they can find it when you are not available.
- **Move to the second ministry when the carrying ends.** *Shall do no service ... but shall minister with their brethren ... to keep the charge.* Keep the charge and stay among the brothers, and let the younger men do the lifting.

### For the young

- **Ask your father what he wants to continue, and whether anyone has been named.** A great deal of grief is caused by successors who assumed they were told and fathers who assumed they had said it.
- **Receive the four things deliberately.** A name, a map, a mechanism, a blessing. Ask for them if they are not offered, and write them down when they are given.
- **Do not let your father's work die of your inattention to what he built.** Enlarging the tent is your work now, and so is strengthening the stakes.
- **Expect to be told to see a land you will not occupy in his lifetime.** *I have caused thee to see it with thine eyes, but thou shalt not go over thither.* That was the arrangement for Moses, and it may be the arrangement for the man who forms you.
- **Let him keep the charge while you do the service.** His judgment is the deposit in an older body, and the man who despises the charge will spend his strength rebuilding what the charge already secured.
- **And if you have no father in the faith, build the four things yourself.** The community you form, the commission you accept, the record you keep, and the blessing you speak over the young man behind you are all available to a man who has been given none.

### Questions for reflection

1. If you were removed from your work tonight, what would continue, and for how long?
2. Have you named your successor, spoken to the man who is not the successor, and given him something real to carry away?
3. What are you building at present that only makes sense if somebody comes after you?
4. What have you written down for a generation that will not be able to ask you?
5. Who are you going to bless this year, and what are you going to say over him that he cannot yet be?
