## The Young Counselors Who Destroyed a Kingdom

**The previous chapter examined the decision. This one examines the men who shaped it, and the subject is broader than one bad afternoon in Shechem, because the cohort is a permanent feature of every generation and Scripture has a great deal to say about it.**

**A cohort is the group of peers who have a man's ear by default. They are not appointed and they are not usually recognised, and their influence is greater than that of any formal council because it operates continuously and without minutes.**

**And the two clearest cases in Scripture both concern a young man with a cohort, and in both cases the cohort destroyed a kingdom.**

**In the first, ten tribes were lost in three days. In the second, twenty thousand men died in a wood, and a father lost a son who had been advised by his friends.**

### The profile of the men who grew up with him

*And king Rehoboam consulted with the old men, that stood before Solomon his father while he yet lived ... But he forsook the counsel of the old men, which they had given him, and consulted with the young men that were grown up with him, and which stood before him* (1 Kings 12:6, 8).

**The Hebrew for *grown up with him* is *gadelu immo* — those who had grown great alongside him.** They were the prince's companions from childhood, sons of the nobility, raised in the palace in the same years as the heir.

**And their single defining qualification is a deficiency, and it determines everything they advised.**

**They had no memory of the building.**

**The old men had stood before Solomon *while he yet lived*, which means they had watched the temple rise, seen the treaties signed, watched the treasury fill and drain, and remembered the northern tribes in the years before the levy was imposed.** They knew what the country had cost and what it had been like before.

**The cohort had none of that. They were born into the finished palace.**

**For them, the temple had always been there.** The borders had always been secure, the gold had always been on the walls, the provisions had always come up from the tribes on schedule. **They had no experience of the nation at any other stage, and therefore no capacity to understand what the tribes were actually asking, or why they might mean it.**

**Which produces the first law of a destructive cohort, and it is worth stating in a form you can apply this week: a cohort cannot give you counsel about the work it did not see built.**

**And the second feature of the cohort is that it is composed of men who share your position and not the people's.** They were courtiers. **Their future depended on the king's standing, not on the tribes', and their advice can be predicted from their interest.**

**And the third feature is the one that makes a cohort so persuasive and so difficult to resist: it never disagrees with you about anything important, because it was selected by agreement.**

**These men had not been chosen for their judgement.** They were chosen because they were the same age and the same class and had the same advantages, **and men chosen on those grounds will almost always confirm the person who chose them.**

### The advice dissected

**And now the advice itself, because it is a masterclass in the counsel of a cohort and every clause of it is reproducible.**

*Thus shalt thou speak unto this people that spake unto thee, saying, Thy father made our yoke heavy, but make thou it lighter unto us; thus shalt thou say unto them, My little finger shall be thicker than my father's loins. And now whereas my father did lade you with a heavy yoke, I will add to your yoke: my father hath chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions* (1 Kings 12:10–11).

**Notice first what the advice does with the actual question.** The people asked about the *yoke*. The cohort answered about the king's *finger* and his father's *loins*.

**The counsel transformed a request about labour into a comparison of bodies.** And a comparison of bodies has only one possible outcome: whoever is bigger wins. **A cohort will always convert a practical question into a question of the leader's standing, because standing is the only subject its members understand.**

**Notice second the deliberate invocation of the father.** *My father ... my father.* The advice is framed entirely in terms of the predecessor, and the framing is the point: **the cohort is teaching the new king how to define himself against his father rather than how to succeed him.**

**Which is the second law of a destructive cohort: it will teach you to differentiate from your predecessor rather than to inherit from him.** And differentiation is easy, satisfying, and instantly visible, whereas inheritance takes years and looks like fidelity.

**Notice third the escalation.** The people asked for less; the answer offered more. **And this is not an excess of zeal; it is a strategy.** A new leader who feels his legitimacy is thin will frequently make a demonstration of severity **because severity is the cheapest available substitute for the authority he has not yet earned.**

**Notice fourth the vocabulary: whips and scorpions.** The scorpion was a lash with spikes of metal, and the image was chosen for its terror. **They advised the young king to speak in the language of pain, and the language of pain is the mark of a leader who has concluded that he cannot be loved.**

**And notice fifth, and this is the most telling element, that the counsel cost them nothing.**

**They were sitting in the palace while they recommended it. The first man to die because of it was Adoram, the officer in charge of the levy, who was stoned by the people (1 Kings 12:18).**

**Which is the third law: a cohort that has never paid for its advice will give advice it would not have paid for.** The men who tell you to be severe are rarely the men who will be standing next to you when the severity is answered.

### The second cohort: the men who "knew not any thing"

**And Absalom's rebellion is the case that shows the same machinery operating at scale, and it is the most instructive story of a generational coup in the whole of Scripture.**

**Begin with the young man's method of building a following, because it is analytically brilliant and entirely wicked.**

*And Absalom rose up early, and stood beside the way of the gate: and it was so, that when any man that had a controversy came to the king for judgment, then Absalom called unto him, and said, Of what city art thou? And he said, Thy servant is of one of the tribes of Israel. And Absalom said unto him, See, thy matters are good and right; but there is no man deputed of the king to hear thee. Absalom said moreover, Oh that I were made judge in the land, that every man which hath any suit or cause might come unto me, and I would do him justice! And it was so, that when any man came nigh to him to do him obeisance, he put forth his hand, and took him, and kissed him. And on this manner did Absalom to all Israel that came to the king for judgment: so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel* (2 Samuel 15:2–6).

**Extract the technique, because it is still in use.**

**He positioned himself where the institution's traffic was.** He stood *beside the way of the gate*, which is where the king's court sat. **He did not go and build something in the provinces; he intercepted the people who were already on their way to the legitimate authority.**

**He asked a question and let the petitioner talk.** *Of what city art thou?* And he listened to the answer, and the listening was the whole of the seduction.

**And then he said three things, in order, and each of them is a distinct act of sabotage.**

**First he validated the grievance: *thy matters are good and right.*** He agreed with the litigant before he knew the case. **Which is the oldest trick in the gathering of a following, and the cheapest: universal affirmation.**

**Second he blamed the institution without naming the king: *there is no man deputed of the king to hear thee.*** David had a court, and the court had officers, and there were thousands of cases every year. **Absalom's line created the impression that the machinery was the problem — that the king was not personally indifferent but had simply failed to appoint anyone.**

**And third he offered himself: *Oh that I were made judge in the land.***

**And then he touched them.** *He put forth his hand, and took him, and kissed him.* **Physical warmth, offered without cost, to men who had come to court hoping for a hearing and were used to being processed.**

**The narrator's verdict is one sentence: *so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.***

**And the technique deserves an application, because it is available to every young man and can be performed in a state of complete sincerity.** If you spend your time listening to people who are dissatisfied with the man above you, agreeing that their complaint is just, and implying that things would be different if you were in charge, **you are doing what Absalom did, and the destruction that follows will be attributed to you.**

**And note that Absalom never addressed the actual business of the kingdom.** He never raised the taxes, never led a campaign, never judged a single case as far as the record shows. **He built a following by the pure administration of sympathy, and the hearts he stole were never examined.**

**And now the second element of the rebellion, and it is the one that shows how a cohort is assembled in a crisis.**

*And with Absalom went two hundred men out of Jerusalem, that were called; and they went in their simplicity, and they knew not any thing* (2 Samuel 15:11).

**Two hundred invited guests who had no idea what they were attending.**

**They were *called* — the Hebrew is *qeruim*, the summoned ones, probably invited to a sacrifice at Hebron — and they went without knowing the purpose.** They were not conspirators. **They were the appearance of legitimacy, and they were being used for it.**

**Which is the fourth law: a cohort in the making will not tell you what it is for.** You will be included, honoured, invited, and informed of nothing, and by the time the trumpet sounds you will be committed.

**And note the specific sentence in verse 10: the signal was a trumpet and the message was *Absalom reigneth in Hebron*.** The followers did not have to make a decision. **They were informed that the decision had already been made, and the announcement itself created the submission it assumed.**

### Ahithophel: the elder who joined the cohort

**And here the story becomes far more dangerous than the Rehoboam case, because the rebellion did not rely on the wisdom of peers. It had a counsellor of the first rank.**

*And Absalom sent for Ahithophel the Gilonite, David's counsellor, from his city ... And the conspiracy was strong; for the people increased continually with Absalom* (2 Samuel 15:12).

**And the narrator tells us what this man's counsel was worth:**

*And the counsel of Ahithophel, which he counselled in those days, was as if a man had enquired at the oracle of God: so was all the counsel of Ahithophel both with David and with Absalom* (2 Samuel 16:23).

**His advice was as accurate as a divine oracle, and it was available to the usurper as readily as to the king.**

**Which produces the fifth law, and it is the most important one in this chapter: the counsel of an experienced man is only as good as his character, and a defected elder can destroy a kingdom more thoroughly than a room full of young men.**

**The young cohort advises out of inexperience. Ahithophel advised out of accuracy and malice, and the combination of the two is fatal.**

**And note that David's response to the news was not political. It was a prayer.**

*And one told David, saying, Ahithophel is among the conspirators with Absalom. And David said, O Lord, I pray thee, turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness* (2 Samuel 15:31).

**The king did not organise a counter-offensive when he heard. He asked God to ruin the man's advice.** Which tells you what David understood the real weapon of the rebellion to be.

**And the counsel that Ahithophel gave is recorded in detail, and the first piece is militarily the correct one.**

*Moreover Ahithophel said unto Absalom, Let me now choose out twelve thousand men, and I will arise and pursue after David this night: and I will come upon him while he is weary and weak handed, and will make him afraid: and all the people that are with him shall flee; and I will smite the king only* (2 Samuel 17:1–2).

**Twelve thousand men, a night march, a pursuit of a fleeing king while he was *weary and weak handed*, and a single-target assassination.** And the reasoning he gives is militarily sound: strike before David can regroup, and the whole kingdom falls with one man.

**And the narrator supplies the divine verdict on the whole affair, in a sentence that must be read in full:**

*For the Lord had appointed to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel, to the intent that the Lord might bring evil upon Absalom* (2 Samuel 17:14).

**Note that Scripture calls it *good counsel*.** It was good. It would have worked. **And it was defeated because God had determined to destroy the young man who had listened to it.**

**And Absalom rejected it in favour of Hushai's, and the reason is in the verse: *the saying pleased Absalom well, and all the elders of Israel* (2 Samuel 17:4) — a counsel that promises a larger army and personal leadership, and therefore a larger share of glory for the man who received it.**

**Which is the sixth law of a cohort, and it is why cohorts are so dangerous to a young man who has recently acquired authority: he will be drawn to the advice that gives him the most prominent personal role.**

**Ahithophel offered the king a night raid and a share of a victory. Hushai offered him the leading of a national muster. And the second was the fatally attractive one, and it gave David time to cross the Jordan and prepare, and it killed Absalom.**

**And the end of the wise counsellor is recorded without comment:**

*And when Ahithophel saw that his counsel was not followed, he saddled his ass, and arose, and gat him home to his house, to his city, and put his household in order, and hanged himself, and died, and was buried in the sepulchre of his father* (2 Samuel 17:23).

**He had been right and had not been followed, and he could not survive being disregarded.** A man whose identity is his accuracy will die when his accuracy is refused.

### What the advice cost

**And the account of the battle is two verses long, and it records the price of a cohort's counsel more exactly than any sermon could.**

*So the people went out into the field against Israel: and the battle was in the wood of Ephraim; where the people of Israel were slain before the servants of David, and there was there a great slaughter that day of twenty thousand men. For the battle was there scattered over the face of all the country: and the wood devoured more people that day than the sword devoured* (2 Samuel 18:6–8).

**Twenty thousand men. And *the wood devoured more people that day than the sword devoured.***

**The young men who advised Absalom — the men who had made his chariot, run before him, stood beside the gate with him, and told him the kingdom was his — one prince died hanging in an oak tree with three darts in his heart, and twenty thousand Israelites died in a forest.**

**And the kingdom was not changed. David returned to the throne.** The rebellion achieved nothing except the destruction of the generation that attempted it.

**Which is the seventh law, and it is the one that must be told to every young man who is being recruited into a faction: the cost of a coup is paid by the people who were not consulted.**

**The cohort had the hearing. The two hundred summoned guests had the good faith. The twenty thousand had the deaths. And the man who took the counsel had the oak tree.**

### David's cohort, and how it was different

**And now the contrast, because David assembled a cohort too, and it was the instrument by which the throne was eventually taken, and it was assembled in a cave rather than a palace.**

*David therefore departed thence, and escaped to the cave Adullam: and when his brethren and all his father's house heard it, they went down thither to him. And every one that was in distress, and every one that was in debt, and every one that was discontented, gathered themselves unto him; and he became a captain over them: and there were with him about four hundred men* (1 Samuel 22:1–2).

**Consider who gathered: the distressed, the indebted, and the discontented, and his own family.**

**These were not the sons of the court.** They were the men who had no position to protect, and they had nothing to gain from David's rise except the possibility of a life. **And they gathered to him in a wilderness, at the beginning, when he had nothing to give them.**

**And that is the first difference: David's men joined him in the years of nothing, and therefore their counsel was formed by the price of the thing.**

**The second difference is that the cohort was tested, and its enlistment was conditional on loyalty that David himself could not verify.**

*And David went out to meet them, and answered and said unto them, If ye be come peaceably unto me to help me, mine heart shall be knit unto you: but if ye be come to betray me to mine enemies, seeing there is no wrong in mine hands, the God of our fathers look thereon, and rebuke it. Then the spirit came upon Amasai, who was chief of the captains, and he said, Thine are we, David, and on thy side, thou son of Jesse: peace, peace be unto thee, and peace be to thine helpers; for thy God helpeth thee. Then David received them, and made them captains of the band* (1 Chronicles 12:17–18).

**He asked them openly whether they had come to help him or to betray him, and he invoked God as the judge of the answer.** Which is the exact opposite of the palace cohort's assumption of agreement. **David interrogated the men who came to him, and the men who answered well became his captains.**

**And the third difference is the quality that Scripture singles out among them:**

*And of the children of Issachar, which were men that had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do; the heads of them were two hundred; and all their brethren were at their commandment* (1 Chronicles 12:32).

**Men who understood the times and knew what Israel ought to do.**

**That is the definition of the cohort a young man should have: not men who share his youth and his table, but men who can read the moment and say what the house should do.** And the phrase *all their brethren were at their commandment* tells us they were themselves men under authority, which is the qualification no palace companion in the Rehoboam narrative ever displayed.

**And the summary of the whole host at Hebron gives the criterion:**

*All these men of war, that could keep rank, came with a perfect heart to Hebron, to make David king over all Israel* (1 Chronicles 12:38).

**Two qualifications and both are about the inner life rather than the skill: they could keep rank, and they came with a perfect heart.**

**They could keep rank, which means they were men under discipline and not free agents with opinions. And they came with a perfect heart, which means their motive was not the share of the spoil.** A cohort of that description will tell a young leader the truth, and will remain when he is wrong, and will not be assembled by flattery.

### Elihu: the young man who got it right

**And Scripture does not leave the impression that a young man should never speak, and it provides one counter-example in full, in the book of Job.**

**Elihu is the youngest of Job's comforters, and he is the only one of them who is not condemned at the end of the book (Job 42:7).** And his conduct shows the difference between a cohort and a wise young man.

*Now Elihu had waited till Job had spoken, because they were elder than he. When Elihu saw that there was no answer in the mouth of these three men, then his wrath was kindled ... And Elihu the son of Barachel the Buzite answered and said, I am young, and ye are very old; wherefore I was afraid, and durst not shew you mine opinion. I said, Days should speak, and multitude of years should teach wisdom. But there is a spirit in man: and the inspiration of the Almighty giveth them understanding. Great men are not always wise: neither do the aged understand judgment* (Job 32:4–9).

**Five things in that passage, and they are a complete code of conduct for a young man who disagrees with his elders.**

**He waited.** *Elihu had waited till Job had spoken, because they were elder than he.* The first act of the wise young man is patience, and it is exercised before he has said anything at all.

**He was afraid.** *Wherefore I was afraid, and durst not shew you mine opinion.* **He did not begin with the confidence of the cohort; he began with reluctance.** And a young man who is afraid to speak is far safer than one who is eager.

**He honoured the principle of seniority.** *I said, Days should speak, and multitude of years should teach wisdom.* **He affirms the very rule that would silence him, and he affirms it before he sets it aside.**

**And he sets it aside on a specific ground that is not his own insight: *but there is a spirit in man: and the inspiration of the Almighty giveth them understanding.***

**The one thing that justifies a young man speaking to an older one is not his better information. It is revelation.** And revelation is a claim that must be made with trembling, because it makes God the party and removes the young man's claim to be clever.

**And fifth, he still did not assert himself as a peer.** The rest of his speech addresses Job directly and the three friends critically, and he is answered by God with the others and not separately.

**The contrast with Rehoboam's men could not be sharper.** They spoke first, they spoke with confidence, they said nothing that could have been formed by prayer, and they had no fear of anyone. **Elihu waited, trembled, affirmed the elder's right, appealed to the Spirit, and was not rebuked.**

**And the practical test for a young man is therefore not whether he has a better answer. It is whether he is able to deliver it in Elihu's manner.** If it can only be said in the manner of a cohort — at the gate, in the corridor, in the group chat, with the settled conclusion that the older man is finished — **then it must not be said at all, because the manner is the message and the message is a coup.**

### Five laws of a cohort

**And the whole subject reduces to five laws, and each of them can be checked in a week.**

**The first law: a cohort cannot counsel you about what it did not watch being built.** Rehoboam's companions were born into the finished palace and had no idea what the nation had cost. **If the men closest to you only know your work in its strong form, they cannot tell you what to do when it is weak.**

**The second law: a cohort will teach you to differentiate from your predecessor rather than to inherit from him.** *My little finger shall be thicker than my father's loins.* Differentiation is quick, visible and satisfying; inheritance is slow, invisible and required.

**The third law: a cohort will always convert a practical question into a question of your standing.** The people asked about the levy and the answer concerned anatomy. **Watch whether the men around you discuss the problem or your reputation.**

**The fourth law: the advice costs them nothing, and the people pay.** The cohort recommended the scorpions; Adoram was stoned; two hundred invited guests went *in their simplicity, and they knew not any thing*; twenty thousand died in a wood.

**And the fifth law: a wise older man who has defected is more dangerous than any number of young enthusiasts.** Ahithophel's counsel was *as if a man had enquired at the oracle of God*, and it was aimed at the king's life. **Test the character of the experienced adviser before you test his accuracy, because accuracy is exactly what a bitter man has most of.**

**And the scriptural warnings about companions are not gentle:**

*He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed* (Proverbs 13:20).

*Be not deceived: evil communications corrupt good manners* (1 Corinthians 15:33).

*My son, if sinners entice thee, consent thou not. If they say, Come with us ... cast in thy lot among us; let us all have one purse* (Proverbs 1:10–11, 14).

**Note the phrase *let us all have one purse*.** A cohort is a group with a shared interest, and the shared interest is stated before the crime is described. **And the young man who joins a group with a shared purse has joined a group whose counsel will be governed by the purse.**

**And the proverb that describes the end of the Rehoboam case is the one that mentions the king's age:**

*Woe to thee, O land, when thy king is a child, and thy princes eat in the morning!* (Ecclesiastes 10:16).

**The Hebrew for *child* is *na'ar*, and Rehoboam was forty-one.** The word does not describe his years. **It describes a man with the authority of a king and the counsel of his childhood companions, eating in the morning while the country waits.**

### Principles

**First: a cohort is the peer group with a man's ear by default, and it exerts more influence than any formal council.** It operates continuously, without minutes, and without accountability.

**Second: the cohort cannot counsel a man about the work it did not see built.** *The young men that were grown up with him.* They had no memory of the palace when it was a building site, and no understanding of what the nation had cost.

**Third: a cohort will counsel you to differentiate from your predecessor rather than to inherit from him.** *My father hath chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions.* Inheritance is slow and faithful; differentiation is quick and fatal.

**Fourth: cohort advice converts a practical question into a question of your standing.** The people asked about the yoke, and the answer was about the king's little finger. Severity is the cheapest substitute for authority a new leader has not yet earned.

**Fifth: the advice costs the advisers nothing, and the people pay.** Adoram was stoned, two hundred men went *in their simplicity, and they knew not any thing*, and twenty thousand died in the wood of Ephraim.

**Sixth: a bitter elder with accurate counsel is more dangerous than a room of young enthusiasts.** *The counsel of Ahithophel ... was as if a man had enquired at the oracle of God.* Test character before accuracy.

**Seventh: the cohort that forms a young man rightly is assembled in the wilderness and not the palace.** *Every one that was in distress, and every one that was in debt, and every one that was discontented.* They joined before there was anything to inherit.

**Eighth: the qualification of a good cohort is that it could keep rank and came with a perfect heart.** *Men that had understanding of the times*; men who were themselves under authority; men whose motive was not the spoil.

**And ninth: a young man may correct his elders, but only in Elihu's manner.** He waited, he was afraid, he affirmed the rule of seniority, he appealed to the Spirit rather than his own insight, and he was not rebuked. **The manner is the message.**

### For the fathers

- **Identify your son's cohort by name, and ask who has his ear when you are not in the room.** A cohort is recognised by continuous access, not by title.
- **Ask whether the men around him have ever watched anything being built.** *The young men that were grown up with him.* If they know his work only in its strong form, they cannot counsel him when it is weak.
- **Give him access to men who stood before you.** The old men's qualification at Shechem was that they had stood before Solomon *while he yet lived*. Introduce your successor to the men who knew your work when it was a burden.
- **Watch for the adviser whose accuracy you cannot question and whose character you have not tested.** Ahithophel's counsel was as an oracle of God, and he was already disaffected.
- **And do not be surprised when the young man's cohort recommends severity toward you.** *My little finger shall be thicker than my father's loins.* Differentiation is the natural counsel of men who did not build the house.

### For the young

- **Ask who your cohort is, and whether you chose them or merely grew up with them.** *The young men that were grown up with him.* A group selected by proximity cannot counsel you about the future.
- **Never build a following by listening to grievances about the man above you.** Absalom said *thy matters are good and right* to every man who came to the gate. Sympathy without authority is a coup being assembled.
- **Test counsel by its direction: does it tell you to serve or to be feared?** The elders promised permanence from one day of servanthood; the cohort offered scorpions.
- **Ask who pays for the advice before you take it.** The cohort sat in the palace; Adoram was stoned and twenty thousand died in the wood of Ephraim.
- **If you must correct an older man, do it in Elihu's manner.** Wait until he has spoken. Be afraid. Affirm his right to speak first. Appeal to the Spirit and not to your own insight. And if the thing can only be said at the gate, in the corridor, or in the group, do not say it.
- **Assemble a cohort of men who could keep rank and came with a perfect heart.** *Men that had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do.* Men under discipline, who joined you before there was anything to inherit.
- **And beware the experienced friend who is bitter.** He will be right about many things, and the accuracy will be his justification for the damage.

### Questions for reflection

1. Who has your ear by default, and what qualification did those men have to be there?
2. When you last received advice, did it concern the problem or your standing?
3. Whose grievance have you been validating in private, and what would it take for that to become a conspiracy?
4. Who has paid for the counsel you have taken in the last five years?
5. If you had to correct an older man this month, could you do it in Elihu's manner, or only in the manner of Absalom's gate?
