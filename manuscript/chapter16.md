# Chapter 16

## Systemic and Structural Trauma

**Central question.** How do collective, historical and structural harms affect individuals, and how should neurobiology engage with them?

### Learning objectives

- distinguish individual, collective and structural trauma;
- describe historical trauma and its contested evidence base;
- explain how discrimination and poverty affect stress physiology;
- evaluate the concept of trauma-informed organisations;
- identify the limits of neurobiological explanation for social harms.

## Introduction

Most trauma research studies individuals. But much trauma is collective: war, genocide, displacement, apartheid, colonialism, mass incarceration, natural disaster. And much harm is structural: it operates through laws, institutions, economies and norms rather than through a single perpetrator.

This chapter examines these levels. It asks what neurobiology can contribute to understanding collective and structural harm, and what it cannot. It argues that biological language can illuminate mechanisms—how discrimination gets under the skin—but that it can also individualise and depoliticise problems that require social change.

> **A note on terminology.** Historical trauma refers to cumulative emotional and psychological wounding across generations resulting from massive group trauma. Structural trauma refers to harm embedded in institutions and social arrangements. Collective trauma refers to shared psychological effects of a traumatic event on a group. The terms overlap and are used inconsistently in the literature.

## 16.1 Collective trauma

Collective trauma occurs when a group experiences an event that shatters shared assumptions and identities. It affects individuals, but it also affects the group's narratives, rituals, institutions and sense of safety.

Research on collective trauma draws on sociology, anthropology and history as well as psychology. It examines how communities remember, commemorate and transmit experiences, and how they rebuild.

Neurobiological methods are poorly suited to studying collective trauma directly. They can study individuals within affected groups, but they cannot capture the group-level processes that shape meaning and recovery.

## 16.2 Historical trauma

The concept of historical trauma was developed in relation to Indigenous communities in North America, particularly the legacy of boarding schools and forced assimilation. It proposes that massive group trauma has effects that persist across generations.

Empirical evidence is mixed. Studies report elevated rates of mental health problems, substance use and suicide in some Indigenous communities, but attributing these to historical trauma specifically is difficult because contemporary disadvantage, discrimination and ongoing colonialism are also powerful determinants.

The concept has political and clinical value: it acknowledges that harms are not only individual and not only past. It also risks overextension, and it should be used carefully rather than as a catch-all explanation.

## 16.3 Discrimination and stress physiology

Experiences of discrimination are associated with elevated stress hormones, blood pressure, inflammation and poor mental health. These associations are found across many groups and are not explained away by socioeconomic status.

Discrimination can be acute (a specific incident) or chronic (ongoing exposure to bias and exclusion). Chronic discrimination is a stressor that is unpredictable, uncontrollable and identity-relevant, which makes it particularly potent.

Biological measures can document the physiological cost of discrimination. They cannot, by themselves, prove causation, and they should not be used to individualise a structural problem. Their value is in showing that social experience has biological consequences—an argument against the idea that discrimination is "just" a perception.

## 16.4 Poverty and deprivation

Poverty exposes people to multiple stressors: food insecurity, housing instability, unsafe environments, limited access to health care and chronic uncertainty. These stressors affect stress physiology, cognitive bandwidth and mental health.

The relationship between poverty and trauma is bidirectional. Poverty increases exposure to traumatic events and reduces access to resources for recovery; trauma can also lead to loss of income, housing and social support.

Neurobiological research on poverty has documented associations with cortical development, language exposure and stress physiology. These findings should be interpreted carefully: they describe population patterns, not individual potential, and they can be used to justify deficit narratives if misapplied.

The argument that early socioeconomic adversity becomes biologically embedded is developed by Shonkoff and Garner (2012).

## 16.5 War and displacement

War and displacement produce trauma through direct violence, loss, separation, deprivation and uncertainty. Refugees often experience multiple traumas before, during and after migration, including detention, exploitation and discrimination in host countries.

Post-migration stressors—uncertain legal status, poverty, isolation, discrimination, difficulty accessing services—predict mental health outcomes alongside pre-migration trauma. This is an important corrective to the assumption that trauma is located only in the past.

Interventions should address both trauma and post-migration conditions. Clinical services that ignore housing, legal status and social support are unlikely to be sufficient.

## 16.6 Structural violence

Structural violence refers to harm that results from social arrangements rather than from identifiable individuals. It includes inadequate housing, unsafe work, pollution, food insecurity and unequal access to care.

Structural violence is often invisible in clinical settings because it does not present as a discrete event. A patient may present with chronic stress, depression or physical illness without reporting a "trauma" in the conventional sense.

Clinical practice can respond by asking about social conditions, connecting patients to resources and advocating for change. This is not a distraction from neurobiology; it is an application of the understanding that stress physiology is shaped by environment.

## 16.7 Trauma-informed organisations

Trauma-informed care is an organisational approach that recognises the prevalence of trauma, understands its effects, avoids re-traumatisation and supports recovery. It involves changes in policy, environment, language and staff training.

Evidence for trauma-informed organisational interventions is limited but growing. Studies report improvements in staff knowledge, patient experience and some clinical outcomes. The concept has been criticised for becoming a slogan without substantive change.

Genuine trauma-informed practice requires attention to power, safety, choice and transparency. It is not simply a training course; it is a change in how organisations operate.

## 16.8 Community resilience

Community resilience refers to the capacity of a community to withstand and recover from adversity. It involves social networks, shared identity, local leadership, infrastructure and access to resources.

Community-based interventions can be effective because they work with existing structures and reach people who do not access clinical services. They can also be more acceptable and sustainable than externally imposed programmes.

Research should be community-led where possible, with attention to consent, benefit-sharing and cultural appropriateness.

## 16.9 Measurement and attribution

Measuring the effects of collective and structural trauma is difficult. Exposure is often ongoing, comparison groups are hard to define, and outcomes are shaped by many factors.

Attribution requires designs that can separate the effects of a specific historical event from ongoing disadvantage. This is rarely possible with observational data, and researchers should be explicit about the limits of their claims.

Qualitative and participatory methods can complement quantitative approaches by capturing meaning, process and lived experience.

## 16.10 Responsibility of the field

The neurobiology of trauma has a responsibility to engage with social context. Biological explanations can be used to depoliticise suffering—to say that the problem is in the brain rather than in the conditions that produced it.

This does not mean that biology is irrelevant. It means that biological findings should be situated within social and political analysis, and that researchers should consider how their work might be used.

A responsible field acknowledges uncertainty, avoids biological determinism and supports interventions at multiple levels: individual, family, community and policy.

## 16.11 Displacement, asylum and clinical practice

Displaced people often carry several layers of adversity: violence or persecution before flight, dangerous journeys, loss of family and status, and prolonged uncertainty about the right to remain. Post-migration conditions—detention, crowded accommodation, restricted work, discrimination, separation from relatives—are among the strongest predictors of distress, and they are modifiable in principle (Fazel et al., 2012).

Clinical practice should therefore address both past trauma and present circumstances. Assessment should ask about legal status, housing, income, language, family contact and access to health care, not because these are "social issues" separate from biology, but because they shape sleep, nutrition, safety and the capacity to engage in treatment.

Interpreters should be trained, briefed and given time. Family members, particularly children, should not interpret in sensitive assessments. Written materials should be available in the person's preferred language, and consent should be obtained in a way that does not depend on literacy or on fear of authority.

Clinicians should be clear about their role. They may be asked to provide reports for asylum proceedings, where consistency is often treated as a marker of credibility. Trauma-related memory is not uniformly consistent, and the absence of a detailed narrative does not indicate fabrication. Reports should state the limits of the assessment and avoid claims that the clinician cannot support.

Working with displaced people also carries an emotional load. Vicarious traumatisation, moral distress and burnout are common, and supervision and organisational support are protective rather than optional.

## 16.12 Community partnership and research ethics

Research on collective and structural trauma has often been extractive: communities provide data and receive little in return. Partnership approaches attempt to change that relationship.

Community-based participatory research involves community members in defining questions, designing instruments, interpreting findings and deciding how results are used. It takes time, requires payment and training for community partners, and does not remove the need for ethical review or scientific rigour.

Several principles follow. Consent should be ongoing rather than a single signature. Data should be governed with attention to who owns it, who can access it and what happens to it after the study ends. Findings should be shared in accessible forms before academic publication. Interventions tested in a community should not depend on resources that disappear when the grant ends.

Researchers should also be cautious about framing. Describing a community primarily through its trauma can reinforce stigma and obscure agency, culture and resistance. A formulation that includes structural causes should also include what communities have built, preserved and changed.

These principles apply with particular force where the researcher is more advantaged than the participants, and where the topic—violence, dispossession, state action—carries political risk. Ethical review is necessary but not sufficient; judgement, humility and accountability matter as much as procedure.

## Composite case study

A refugee patient presents with insomnia and intrusive memories. Assessment identifies pre-migration violence but also post-migration stressors: uncertain status, crowded housing and discrimination. The plan combines trauma-focused therapy with practical support and advocacy. ## Chapter summary

Trauma occurs at individual, collective, historical and structural levels. Neurobiology can document how social harms affect bodies, but it cannot capture group processes or substitute for social analysis. Discrimination, poverty, war and displacement affect stress physiology and mental health, and post-migration conditions matter as much as past events. The field should engage with social context, avoid biological determinism and support interventions at multiple levels.
