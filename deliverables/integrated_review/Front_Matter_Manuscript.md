## Preface

Trauma neuroscience asks how experiences of threat, injury, loss, violence, and helplessness interact with the nervous system over time. No single brain region, hormone, or biological measure provides a complete answer. Understanding requires attention to cells and circuits, development, endocrine and immune regulation, learning and memory, relationships, health, culture, and social conditions.

This doctoral-level textbook connects biological explanation to methodological scrutiny and responsible professional action. Its sequence moves from foundational concepts and neuroanatomy through cellular and molecular processes, learning, stress physiology, disorders, recovery, measurement, intervention, and research translation. Introductory explanations support access to advanced material without replacing the prior study and critical reading expected at doctoral level.

A recurring distinction is between what a study observes, how a finding is interpreted, and what decision it can justify. Group differences do not determine the condition or history of an individual. Biological language should clarify uncertainty rather than confer an appearance of certainty that the evidence does not support.

The book treats trauma neurobiology as one part of a broader account of human responses. It avoids reducing people to symptoms or measurements and connects scientific reasoning to ethical practice, cultural understanding, participant protection, and equitable access to services.

## How to use this book

Begin with each chapter's objectives and identify the concepts, methods, and decisions that organise its argument. Relate technical terms to a clear definition, a level of analysis, and an observable measure. Use tables and figures to compare relationships and limitations rather than memorise labels in isolation.

For clinical and service applications, discuss assessment, treatment, risk, and neurotechnology within appropriate supervision and current professional guidance. For research tasks, treat proposed questions as starting points: undertake a structured literature review, define the population and constructs, justify each measure, and obtain the required scientific and ethical review before developing a protocol.

Teaching cases illustrate reasoning; they do not establish treatment effectiveness or provide sufficient information for independent diagnosis. Where an exercise supplies no data, do not invent results. State which information is available, what is missing, and what conclusion is defensible.

## Study sequence

Chapters 1–3 establish foundations, functional anatomy, and lifespan development. Chapters 4–7 examine cells, molecules, neurotransmission, plasticity, learning, and memory. Chapters 8–11 address stress physiology, genetics, threat learning, emotion regulation, interoception, and dissociation.

Chapters 12–15 consider PTSD, complex trauma, resilience, and psychoneuroimmunology. Chapters 16–19 develop biomarkers, imaging, treatment mechanisms, artificial intelligence, and emerging technology. Chapter 20 integrates research methods, translation, ethics, policy, and future directions.

A twenty-week schedule can assign one chapter each week. A shorter semester may group related chapters into teaching blocks while preserving prerequisite knowledge. Reading should be combined with terminology review, figure or table interpretation, critical appraisal, case discussion, and a research activity. Laboratory observation, software training, and supervised clinical discussion require separate arrangements where they form part of the programme.
